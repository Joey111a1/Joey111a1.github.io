Tennessee says child rapists deserve to die. Now it prepares to execute one of their victims

What happened

Christa Pike, once sentenced to death as a 20-year-old after the murder of Colleen Slemmer, now faces lethal injection in Tennessee even as her lawyers press a clemency bid built on the childhood abuse and rape history they say the jury never heard.

The video that began the story

The case is anchored by a video timestamped 30 March 1996. Pike appears with her auburn hair in a white scrunchie, crying quietly as a female judge reads the sentence: “It is ordered that you shall be put to death by execution as prescribed by law,” followed by the explanation that her body would be subjected to electric shock “that you shall die.” Pike turns toward the camera, searches for her mother in the courtroom, and begs, “Can I please hug my mom before I go,” before being taken away without that hug.

Pike had just turned 20. She had been 18 on the night of 12 January 1995, when she and her then boyfriend, Tadaryl Shipp, brutally beat and murdered Colleen Slemmer, 19, on a University of Tennessee campus. Now 50, Pike is scheduled to be put to death by lethal injection at Riverbend maximum security institution in Nashville on 30 September 2025. Tennessee has described her as among the “worst of the worst,” while her lawyers are asking Governor Bill Lee to spare her life.

The abuse case her lawyers say was hidden

The clemency petition, filed on Wednesday, argues that Pike’s case cannot be separated from what happened to her before the murder. It says she was subjected to repeated sexual and physical abuse from early childhood onward, beginning with rape by her paternal grandmother’s boyfriend, Ernest Johnson, when she was between two and five. A forensic evaluation by psychologist Bethany Brand, based on 13 hours of clinical interviews at the Debra Johnson rehabilitation center in Nashville, says Johnson orally raped her in preschool, threatened her with harm, and locked her in a dark chicken coop where spiders crawled on her.

Other abuse surfaced later. At 11, Pike was raped outside her house by a 37-year-old neighbor, Claude Davis, and later attacked at 17 by a stranger who dragged her into the woods and raped her. The evidence says law enforcement knew about the later assaults, and that child protective services became involved only after Pike developed an infection from the attack by Davis. Pike’s lawyers say the jury never heard any of this at trial, nor the years of physical abuse, neglect, or the suicide attempts she made as a child.

The petition also points to her mental health. Pike was only diagnosed years after the trial with bipolar disorder, which her lawyers say helped explain the violent outburst that ended in Slemmer’s death. Sandra Babcock, who has been involved in Pike’s representation since 2019, told the Guardian that if Tennessee executes her, it will show the death penalty is reserved “not for the worst of the worst, but for the most damaged and vulnerable among us.”

A governor’s decision, and a state law in tension

Tennessee has not disputed the abuse details. At a recent hearing, state attorney Will Ayers said, “I think everybody in this courtroom wishes that those things had never happened to Ms Pike. We all wish that we were not here today and that she was not on death row.” Governor Lee now holds the power to commute Pike’s sentence to life without parole, which would be a first; Pike’s lawyers say he has never granted clemency in a death case and is “zero for eight.”

The case also sits against the backdrop of Tennessee’s 2024 expansion of capital punishment. In May 2024, Lee signed legislation extending the death penalty to people who rape a child, after lawmakers argued that such crimes cause lasting psychological, emotional and sometimes physical harm. The measure was described in the evidence as unconstitutional under long-standing US Supreme Court precedent because no one is murdered in a rape-only case. Against that political message, Tennessee is now preparing to execute a woman whom the clemency petition describes as a victim of multiple child rapes.

Pike’s lawyers say she has spent almost three decades in solitary confinement since the video of her sentencing. They say she was held for years in an 8ft-by-10ft cell for up to 24 hours a day, and that her isolation was especially severe because she was the only woman on Tennessee’s death row. Two years ago, she won a lawsuit that slightly loosened those conditions. Her lawyers say she is now receiving treatment for bipolar disorder, has become a mentor to newer prisoners, and shows remorse for Slemmer’s death.

Even so, the execution date has become a countdown. Her lawyer, Kelleigh Gleason, said Pike has had to ask herself whether each milestone is her last Thanksgiving, Christmas or birthday. If last-minute legal challenges fail, her lawyers have asked that only female corrections staff be present in the death chamber. Tennessee has not guaranteed that, leaving Pike with the possibility that men will restrain her and search for a vein for the lethal injection needle — a prospect she fears would repeat the violations of her childhood.

Even in safe-haven Massachusetts, trans care is at risk. Young people are fighting to protect it
global_front_pagepublic_healthpolitics_and_powermaterial_harmanchor
What happened

Trans activists from across Massachusetts occupied attorney general Andrea Campbell’s Boston office building on Wednesday, taking hormone replacement therapy in the lobby and bringing needles, gels, pills and sharps containers to demand stronger protection for youth gender-affirming care. Their protest exposed a gap between Massachusetts’ legal protections and the care some hospitals are still withdrawing.

The youth-led group Protect Trans Futures said it would not leave without a commitment to protect trans kids’ civil rights. Organizers asked Campbell to order hospitals to reinstate care, or to investigate or sue those that had stopped providing it. Some allies feared that confronting a sympathetic attorney general could backfire; others said the action was simply forcing the state to meet its own standards.

Why activists chose Campbell

Teddy Walker, a 21-year-old with Protect Trans Futures, said Massachusetts politicians at the local, state and federal level were “very naive” to think there was not a massive loss of care. “The Trump administration’s policies are working exactly as intended,” he said.

The group’s argument was that Massachusetts remains one of the legally “safest” places for trans people, but safety on paper has not stopped barriers from appearing in practice. Gender-affirming care is still legal for all in the state, yet the Trump administration’s attempt to undermine state laws has created what activists described as a chilling effect.

Care is still legal, but access has shrunk

The pressure on Massachusetts hospitals began after the federal fight over trans health care intensified. In the first weeks of Donald Trump’s second term, he signed an executive order threatening to cut off federal funding for hospitals that provide gender-affirming medical care to trans youth. Later, health secretary Robert F Kennedy Jr sought to target Medicaid funding to hospitals providing youth gender-affirming care.

Even though many of those efforts have been tossed, the evidence says the fallout has still reached Massachusetts. At least three local hospitals or health systems ended gender-affirming care programs for patients under 19: Fenway Health, Outer Cape Health Services and Baystate Health. A fourth Massachusetts hospital canceled all appointments for a gender-affirming surgery for adult trans women, even though adults were not the subject of Trump’s executive order.

Fenway’s move was a particular blow. The state’s largest LGBTQ+ health provider stopped youth hormone care and puberty blockers last October. Other clinics and hospitals took in some of those patients, but not all of them found a quick replacement. One 18-year-old, Josh, said restarting the intake process after Fenway stopped his care took over a year. He said it shattered his trust that his home state would be able to protect him.

The strain has also changed the choices people make about where to live. Safara Malone, a senior at Harvard University, moved to Massachusetts from Texas in 2024 because she expected fewer barriers and more explicit support. With graduation less than a year away, she said she is now unsure where she will feel safe living next.

The protest ended in arrests, not a deal

Campbell has repeatedly said that trans health care “remains legal and protected” in Massachusetts, and she has warned hospitals that ending youth gender-affirming care may amount to discrimination under state law. She met with a group of trans youth advocates in April after some hospitals ended care, and on the same day as the protest she joined 21 other state attorneys general in suing the Trump administration over its attempt to block Medicaid coverage for youth gender-affirming care.

Still, organizers said they had come because Campbell had not gone far enough. Some advocates wanted to avoid weakening a sympathetic attorney general by escalating the fight, but many trans organizers saw the action as holding the government to its own standards.

After the attorney general’s building closed, organizers were handed a phone and offered a meeting by an assistant attorney general. They refused, saying they had already taken that step months earlier without results. After a few hours, nine organizers, including Walker, were arrested by state troopers. The attorney general’s office said it was not involved in the decision to arrest the protesters.

For Protect Trans Futures and its allies, the dispute remains unresolved: whether Massachusetts will simply say trans youth care is legal, or use its power to make sure it is still available.

After overtourism besieged Big Sur, the worst finally happened: ‘People kept saying someone is going to die’
global_front_pageglobal_south_and_exploitedclimate_disastereconomy_and_businessmaterial_harmanchor
What happened

Big Sur’s summer of wildfires, road closures and heavy visitor traffic has pushed a tourism-dependent coast into a sharper reckoning, after recent traffic deaths and a series of congested events made residents ask how many visitors the place can carry before the damage becomes irreversible.

The 120-mile stretch of rocky promontories, beaches and redwood forests at the foot of the Santa Lucia mountains had already been living through what residents described as a long summer of hell. Wildfires, repeated closures and disruptions to hotels, restaurants and campsites hit in a region where tourism accounts for about 90% of the local economy.

Fires, closures and a strained road network

One large fire broke out in the highlands east of Big Sur in early August and remained less than 25% contained. Two weeks later, a second fire erupted 30 miles to the south; firefighters could barely reach it, and it grew larger than the first. Residents know natural catastrophe is part of life in a remote place, but this summer’s fires sharpened questions about whether mass tourism is sustainable in an era of climate change and unpredictable weather.

The trouble had started earlier in the year, when Highway 1 reopened after three years of repair work following heavy landslides. That reopening restored access to Big Sur from the Monterey peninsula in the north to Cambria in the south, but it also brought a surge of visitors back to the coast. Local officials and residents said many of them were mainly interested in selfies at a handful of well-advertised spots, especially Bixby Bridge, where people stopped in the road to take pictures and blocked traffic for miles.

Visitors also trampled plants, camped illegally and tossed trash around, according to local reports described by residents and officials. Kate Daniels, who represents Big Sur on the Monterey county council, said she successfully pressed for a parking moratorium at Bixby Bridge after what she called a change in visitor behavior. “This is one of the most beautiful places you’ve ever seen, yet people have decided their goal is to take a picture of themselves on a bridge … It’s really difficult and dangerous, and that road cannot handle it,” she said.

Car Week and the warning that came true

The low point for many residents came in mid-August during Monterey county’s annual Car Week, which draws thousands of vintage and exotic cars and about 100,000 visitors. Some attendees repurposed the Big Sur coastline as a playground for souped-up coupes and convertibles even as the Timber fire was raging and fire crews were trying to set up defensive lines.

Photos and videos showed car fans making high-speed runs on Highway 1, doing doughnuts at intersections and gathering in groups for photos while fire trucks struggled to move through the congestion. Daniels called it “a nightmare” and said, “People kept saying that someone is going to die.”

By the end, people did die. On the final weekend, a 20-year-old out-of-towner driving a Dodge Challenger struck Brian Roberts, a well-known Big Sur figure nicknamed Brewski. Friends said Roberts had spent many of the previous days railing against Car Week behavior. Accounts differed on how the crash happened: some friends said he was crossing Highway 1 to watch the sunset, while others said he was in the road yelling at cars after they blocked the turn-off to Palo Colorado Road, where he lived.

Days later, an 80-foot skid mark was still visible near the scene, along with circular burn marks where vehicles appeared to have been doing doughnuts. A friend who arrived shortly after said he found bloodstains on the median and tarmac. The following day, another 20-year-old visitor from outside San Francisco crashed a silver BMW convertible into a tree a few miles north of Big Sur, killing one passenger and injuring himself and two friends. Police said the driver recorded a blood alcohol level of 0.13 and were investigating the death as gross vehicular manslaughter.

Mourning, income and the question of how many is too many

Traffic deaths happen in Big Sur every few months, either because drivers go too fast on the narrow, winding road or because they are distracted by the scenery and plunge off the side. Even so, Roberts’s death hit especially hard. “The community felt disrespected because the land was on fire,” Lindsey Kirk, a friend from the San Francisco Bay Area, said. “It’s crazy that the fire services couldn’t get through because of all the cars they brought in.”

Some residents blamed Monterey county officials for using Big Sur’s beauty to attract big events — including marathons, golf tournaments and Car Week — while doing little to protect the people who live there. See Monterey, the tourism body promoting the region, had urged visitors to drive the speed limit, park legally and show “Big Sur Deserves Big Respect.” It also encouraged Car Week attendees to explore the Big Sur coast. Eleven days after Roberts’s death, the organization announced a “banner year,” saying hotel revenue was up, visitor spending exceeded $100 million and more than $4 million had been raised for charity. Left out were the businesses that shut for the week or hired security and put up fences so their lots would not become impromptu motor sports rallies.

Then another flare-up in the Timber fire sent sparks and ash down from Mount Manuel and prompted what many residents said should have happened earlier: a comprehensive closure of Highway 1, including most hotels and restaurants and some of the most visited spots, such as Pfeiffer Beach and McWay Falls. Authorities gave residents 24 hours either to stock up on essentials or leave town. Mourners cheered the decision, but others pointed out the cost: when Big Sur shuts down, hourly workers at hotels and restaurants lose income, and those who are evacuated can face housing and other expenses.

The community tried to help.

Gamified abuse: How a PH forum rewards submissions of non-consensual intimate content

What happened

A 430,000-member Philippine forum rewards users for uploading non-consensual intimate content, turning stolen videos and photos into status, traffic and ad revenue while victims such as Mishy confront how their private moments were reposted and monetized.

Shortly after discovering that intimate videos of her had been uploaded online, Mishy kept returning to one question: why would someone share something that was never theirs in the first place? Her answer was blunt in the evidence: there is something to gain.

How NSFWPH turns abuse into participation

The forum at the center of the story is NSFWPH, which has more than 430,000 registered members. Unlike a conventional pornographic website, it operates as an internet forum where members create threads, comment, react to posts and earn points. Those interactions feed a merit system that unlocks additional privileges and promotes users through ranks such as “Established Member,” “Elite,” “Contributor” and, at the top, “Legendary.”

Higher-ranked members can gain access to locked content, including additional information attached to posts such as the names of victims or links to their Facebook and Instagram accounts. The structure, as described in the evidence, encourages members to find material worth posting, whether by obtaining videos from former partners, redistributing Telegram content or manipulating women into sending intimate images. On NSFWPH, participation itself becomes part of the exploitation.

The system is not only about forum status. It also rewards posting in ways that can be monetized. The evidence says users can earn advertising revenue through download links and, in some cases, sell access to collections on other channels. The platform itself is built around traffic: according to Semrush, NSFWPH receives more than 10 million page views each month, with users viewing an average of 25 pages per visit and staying for more than 14 minutes. Each page displays around five advertisements using ExoClick.

The money trail behind the uploads

One of the platform’s most prolific contributors is a user known as Mikey Kun. He holds the “Legendary” rank, has amassed 4,902 posts and ranks third-highest by post count on the site. Among those uploads are sexually explicit videos of Filipina women, including livestreamers on BIGO and material described as “leaks” and “scandals.”

Most of Kun’s posts use shrinkme.io for monetization. A user who wants to download one of his videos clicks a shrinkme.io link, passes through a series of pop-up ads and then reaches the download link. For every 1,000 users that click, Kun gets a payout. In the Philippines, shrinkme.io’s official payout rate is US$4 per 1,000 views.

The evidence says a scan found more than 200 posts with corresponding shrinkme.io links in just July and August 2026. In random sampling of those posts, the majority reached at least 1,000 views on the forum, with one post reaching at least 12,000 and a few reaching only 600 or 700. The story calculated that if 100 of those 200 posts reached at least 1,000 clicks, that would mean Kun earned $400, or roughly P24,000.

Kun also directs audiences outside NSFWPH. Under the same identity, he maintains Facebook pages and Discord communities that point followers toward more content. His two Discord servers, Mikey’s Vault and The Nowhere, collectively host nearly 6,000 members. One advertises a VIP-only section offering “Exclusive” and “SHS” sets to paying members. His Facebook page has 4,400 followers. Access costs P500, according to a post on his Discord channel Mikey’s Vault, payable through GCash.

What the law, police and victims say

The evidence says Philippine law already prohibits much of what is happening. The Anti-Photo and Video Voyeurism Act of 2009 makes it illegal not only to record sexual acts or private parts without consent, but also to copy, distribute, publish, sell or broadcast such recordings without the written consent of everyone involved, even if the recording was initially consensual. The later Safe Spaces Act further strengthened protections by penalizing gender-based online harassment, including the non-consensual uploading or sharing of photos, audio or videos with sexual content.

Lawyer Dana Batnag said consent has to be informed, specific and freely given. A person can consent to documentation but not to posting, and manipulation can mean consent was not freely given. She also said consent to take a photo or record a video does not mean consent to distribute, share or post it, and that a person can retract that right at any time.

Respicio Law, as cited in the evidence, says a person who receives leaked private photos and forwards them may also face liability. “I only shared it” is not always a defense, and group chat members, page admins, channel operators and social media users should not repost, save, mock or circulate intimate images.

Batnag said the administrators and owners of NSFWPH should be identified because the forum is trafficking sensitive personal information in the form of intimate photos and videos. She said the data subject has the right to know who looked at the picture, who gave it to the site, how it was obtained and to whom it was shared. She also said that even if the website is hosted in another country, its owners could still face consequences under the Data Privacy Act because it deals with information of Filipinos.

Liza Garcia, executive director of the Foundation for Media Alternatives, said a platform like NSFWPH inflicts “a lot of harm” on victims, especially young women, including emotional harm, psychological harm, reputational harm and financial harm.

For the Philippine National Police Anti-Cybercrime Group, NSFWPH sits inside a wider pattern of online sexual exploitation and non-consensual sharing of intimate material. From 2025 through August 2026, the unit conducted 138 operations involving cyber warrants, entrapment operations, arrest warrants and rescue operations, leading to the arrest of 160 people involved in violations of the Anti-Photo and Video Voyeurism Act. Authorities also rescued minors and apprehended seven children in conflict with the law.

But going after the platform itself is harder.
