The battle for Taiz has intensified as Houthi forces press toward positions overlooking Yemen’s third-largest city, exposing civilians to mortar fire, displacement and disrupted livelihoods while government forces backed by Saudi Arabia try to hold the surrounding high ground.

Fighting closes in on Taiz

South-west of Taiz, the boundary between government-held territory and Houthi positions is difficult to define in the mountains. Government forces took a correspondent through checkpoints to the edge of their control, where no man’s land began and Houthi fighters were reported to be advancing only a few hundred metres from some positions.

The immediate danger became clear when a drone approached overhead. A soldier accompanying the group said it was the first drone and that such sightings were usually followed by an attack. The buzzing grew louder as the group took cover under bushes, then left by vehicle. Within about 10 minutes, strikes began landing nearby, although it was unclear where they hit.

The western approaches to Taiz have become the focus of the latest fighting. Government forces hold the city centre, while Houthi positions lie to the north and east. The two sides are contesting high ground that overlooks the route south. If the Houthis secure those areas, government forces could find it harder to retake Mokha, a strategic Red Sea port 85km west of Taiz that the Houthis seized on 10 September.

The seizure followed the collapse of a ceasefire that had largely held for four years. It also gave the Houthis control of territory overlooking the Bab al-Mandab Strait, a major shipping corridor linking Europe and Asia. Taiz lies at a vital intersection between Houthi-controlled areas in northern Yemen and government-held areas in the south, leaving the city surrounded by front lines in several directions.

Civilians remain beside the shelling

Homes are scattered across the mountain slopes, but many have been abandoned by families fleeing the fighting. Near the front line, the quiet of deserted roads was broken by livestock and the sound of incoming and outgoing fire.

Ali, who gave only his first name because he feared repercussions if the Houthis took control, said two mortars had landed on his property. He was living about 1km from the front line with his wife and sisters, caring for his goats. He had sent his children to a displacement camp because they could no longer endure the shelling and gunfire.

“I am terrified,” he said, but added that without the animals his family would have no way to survive.

The effects of the fighting were also visible in Taiz’s main hospital. At al-Thawra hospital, every intensive-care bed seen by the correspondent was occupied by fighters and civilians brought from the front lines. Twelve-year-old Ghana Nabeel was unconscious after shrapnel struck her head while she was herding goats. Her family said an incoming Houthi mortar had landed nearby, and a doctor said the injury had damaged her brain.

Ambulances were also travelling toward the hospital as the road from Aden approached Taiz. At least five were counted within half an hour. The city itself could appear normal after dark, with shops open, restaurants busy and children playing, but shelling and gunfire from the surrounding mountains regularly broke through the ordinary sounds.

Displacement follows the offensive

About 20km south of Taiz, the al-Monig displacement camp was receiving families who had fled Mokha and villages affected by the fighting. Two hundred families had arrived during the previous 10 days, according to information given at the camp.

Maryam Saleh, a pseudonym, arrived a week earlier with her husband and five children, the youngest aged seven. She said the family had walked barefoot from Mokha carrying nothing except their children. Her nephew was killed by mortar shrapnel while they were fleeing, she said. The family encountered clashes and sniper fire ahead and behind them and saw armed men on a mountain.

Another resident, Fatma Mohammed Saleh, said she had been separated from her family while escaping. Unable to walk, she said she crawled for two days through gunfire and shelling before a motorcyclist took her to the camp. She was sleeping outside another family’s tent and said she had no shelter, mattress or family with her.

On 22 September, the UN refugee agency said more than 130,000 people had been displaced across Yemen during that month, in addition to 5.2 million already displaced by the civil war. It warned that a further 100,000 people could be forced from their homes over the next three months.

The government side said it was preparing for a major Houthi offensive, after contacts from the Houthi side said their forces were consolidating troops. At a nearby checkpoint, a government fighter said the bodies of 18 soldiers were lying in an area his unit could fire across but could not reach.

The conflict also involves outside support and allegations about the forces being used. Saudi Arabia leads an international coalition backing Yemen’s government and providing weapons, while Saudi Arabia, Western countries and UN experts have said Iran supplied the Houthis with weapons, funding and military advice. Iran has denied supplying weapons and says it supports the Houthis politically.

There have also been persistent reports of Houthi recruitment of child soldiers. The BBC also reported seeing teenagers serving with government forces, underscoring how the fighting has drawn civilians and children into a battle whose front lines continue to shift around Taiz.

The Hidden State Russia Is Building Inside Ukraine
global_south_and_exploitedpolitics_and_powermaterial_harmmajor supplement
What happened

Russia is building a durable system of governance across occupied Donetsk, Luhansk, Zaporizhzhia, and Kherson, tying residents to Moscow through passports, public services, courts, schools, property rules, and security structures. The changes affect millions of civilians and could make restoring Ukrainian sovereignty more difficult even after a cease-fire.

The occupation is increasingly extending beyond military control. Russian institutions are replacing Ukrainian ones, while Russian documents and administrative systems are becoming necessary for everyday life. Schools use Russian curricula, and Russian authorities govern access to housing, pensions, employment, benefits, education, communications, and other public services.

On June 13, Vladimir Putin told Russian officials that Moscow’s task was to unify the occupied territories with Russia by 2030 in legal, economic, educational, cultural, and information terms. The strategy described here is therefore not limited to holding territory at the front: it is aimed at making Russian rule durable and Ukrainian civic life difficult to restore.

Passports, services, and coercive administration

The process has roots in Crimea, which Russia annexed in 2014. Moscow imposed Russian citizenship, made documentation necessary for employment and basic services, introduced Russian law, and replaced Ukrainian institutions. In the territories occupied after the 2022 full-scale invasion, the same model has been harder to impose because of resistance networks, large industrial centers, and hundreds of thousands of residents who identify as Ukrainian.

Russian federal courts now claim jurisdiction in occupied areas, while Russian taxation, migration, security, and military systems operate there. In 2025, Moscow added occupation administrations to its “Governor’s Dashboard,” a monitoring system that tracks construction, budgets, and administrative compliance across Russia. The stated effect of this integration is to separate the territories from Kyiv and bring residents under Russian oversight.

Residents are required to obtain Russian passports to receive pensions, find formal employment, collect state benefits, or study. Local mobile operators also require Russian identity documents for SIM cards. A young man from Kherson said he obtained a passport after being threatened with a gun because he feared detention or conscription. Sophia, a 24-year-old from occupied Donetsk, said Russian authorities repeatedly told residents that Kyiv had abandoned them and that Moscow was the only source of valid documents. Without them, she said, Russia was “the only place I could legally go,” although she did not want to live there.

For men aged 18 to 65, Russian citizenship also brings military registration and punishment for noncompliance. The Atlantic reports that Russian forces forcibly mobilized approximately 50,000 Ukrainians between February 2022 and the end of 2025, describing this as a violation of international humanitarian law.

Property is another instrument of control. Homes left behind by people who fled may be classified as ownerless or unregistered. Owners seeking to retain them must reregister under Russian law by the end of the year, a process requiring Russian citizenship and a personal return to occupied territory. Since 2024, at least 34,000 properties belonging to Ukrainian citizens have been confiscated or identified for confiscation, according to the account. Some homes have been allocated to Russian citizens encouraged to settle there.

An economy organized around Russia’s war

Moscow has invested billions of dollars in reconstruction since 2022, officially for housing, roads, and public services. The evidence describes those funds as also supporting patronage, resource extraction, and infrastructure serving Russian military needs. About 90 percent of industrial facilities in occupied Ukraine have been closed or repurposed; mines, machine-building plants, metallurgical facilities, and chemical plants have been absorbed into Russian defense production or commandeered for military use.

Since 2023, Russia has expanded roads and ports for military logistics and resource extraction, including about $425 million in railroad spending. The occupied ports of Mariupol and Berdyansk have been integrated into Russia’s maritime network, through which grain, energy, minerals, and other products are exported. In 2025 alone, more than 2 million tons of grain were exported from occupied Ukraine.

For local residents, the resulting economy offers few jobs and low salaries. One resident of occupied Luhansk said many men preferred temporary or unofficial work because formal employment meant going directly to a military-enlistment office. The account also describes worsening social tensions and resentment that Russians brought into the territories are paid substantially more than locals.

Security policy reinforces this economic and administrative system. Occupation authorities use what some call “preventive threat elimination,” treating passive dissent as a security risk. Signal, Ukrainian applications and media, pro-Ukrainian Telegram channels, or suspicious contacts can attract scrutiny and, in some cases, detention or beatings. The Office of the United Nations High Commissioner for Human Rights estimates that Russian occupation forces detained about 15,000 civilians from February 2022 through August 2025, though the true number may be higher.

Children and the struggle over identity

About 1.6 million Ukrainian children live under occupation, and their education has become a central part of the effort to reshape society. Schools emphasize hostility toward Ukraine and the West, glorify Russian symbols, and prohibit teaching in Ukrainian, according to the evidence. A new curriculum introduced this year across kindergarten, schools, and universities promotes Russian identity and praises “defenders of the fatherland,” including participants in the war against Ukraine.

Russia has also expanded military-linked youth organizations. The Movement of the First has more than half a million members in occupied Ukraine, Yunarmia has at least 44,000, and VOIN has enrolled at least 10,000 young people since 2024. These groups introduce children as young as six and young adults to firearms, tactical medicine, drone piloting, survival skills, reconnaissance, and sniping.

Residents interviewed for the account described pressure on children to join and warnings against criticizing the occupation or maintaining contact with relatives outside the territories. A caregiver from Kherson said academic subjects had been displaced by military training and propaganda, beginning in kindergarten.

The resulting system is intended to outlast battlefield changes. Russian institutions, property transfers, identity documents, economic integration, surveillance, and ideological education are creating conditions that could complicate any future Ukrainian return.

Matching layers through activation geometry

The first stage constructs a whitening transform for each layer from calibration activations. The candidate relationships form a weighted graph. science_cs_aipolitics_and_powereconomy_and_businessscience anchorpreprint_not_peer_reviewed
What happened

Large language models are being trained not only to prove mathematical statements but to decide which statements are worth pursuing. A preprint by researchers at FAIR@Meta, CERMICS, Institut Polytechnique de Paris and New York University describes a system that ranks theorem candidates by estimated proof difficulty and uses those rankings to expand a machine-verified mathematical library.

The researchers define a theorem’s “intrinsic interestingness” as the ratio between the length of its proof and the length of its statement: a result that is easy to state but difficult to prove ranks highly. They also argue that this measure correlates strongly with an extrinsic measure of a theorem’s downstream utility. The framework uses Lean 4 and its `mathlib` library, whose formal proofs provide a dependency graph of definitions, premises and theorems.

From proof difficulty to new theorem candidates

The system models the conditional computational cost of proving a target theorem from a given set of premises. This is intended to capture how the difficulty changes when additional lemmas are available, while also supporting a compositional relationship between intermediate and final proofs.

To estimate that cost before a proof is written, the researchers fine-tuned Qwen3.6-27B with group relative policy optimization for 350 steps. On 4,615 held-out validation prompts, they report that the trained model was better calibrated than frontier general-purpose models. Its rankings placed basic algebraic identities near the bottom, analysis in the middle, and famously difficult-to-prove statements such as Fermat’s Last Theorem for exponent 3 near the top.

The resulting estimate was then used as a reward for generating conjectures. The researchers report that optimizing for their metric reduced the share of generated statements judged substantially or fully overlapping with `mathlib` from 91.9% to 30.6%, which they present as evidence that the system produced more out-of-distribution mathematics. They retained 20 proven statements from each of eight mathematical areas, producing 160 statements per model, and report that the trained model outperformed Claude Opus 4.6 when prompted to generate interesting theorems across those areas.

The longer-term design repeatedly promotes selected, verified statements so that discoveries from one round become premises for the next. In comparisons with retaining every verified statement, random promotion and promotion based on longest proofs, the researchers report that interestingness-based selection produced the highest running mean of promoted-statement interestingness, as well as the strongest quality, diversity and “most interesting” judgments.

The paper presents this as a route toward self-expanding, machine-verified mathematical libraries that can choose targets without human-supplied questions. It also acknowledges a limitation: proof length does not capture every dimension of mathematical value, including whether a theorem connects previously unrelated objects or unifies different areas. The work is an arXiv preprint dated September 23, 2026, rather than a peer-reviewed publication.

ExplorationBench: Measuring AI Systems' Exploration in Verifiable Alien Worlds
science_cs_aipolitics_and_powertechnology_and_aiscience anchorpreprint_not_peer_reviewed
What happened

ExplorationBench introduces a benchmark for testing whether AI systems can discover and use unfamiliar rules through experimentation, rather than recall. Its synthetic “alien worlds” make each result exactly verifiable while probing a capability relevant to scientific and engineering work: choosing evidence-gathering actions under uncertainty.

The benchmark uses two executable sandboxes: AlienCode, with 31 discovery targets and 70 tasks, and AlienLogic, with 24 discovery targets and 70 tasks. Each system receives the same flawed manual and fixed worked examples, then completes four rounds in which it chooses probes, runs them in the environment and adds the results to its exploration history. It is subsequently tested on held-out tasks. The worlds deliberately conflict with familiar knowledge, so the researchers argue that pre-training recall alone cannot solve them.

The preprint evaluates ten AI systems, giving each three independent exploration trajectories per sandbox. The researchers report that in AlienCode, the strongest trajectory reaches 87.6% held-out accuracy after four rounds, while comparable model turns without environmental feedback produce only 0.5–11.0% accuracy. Seven of the ten systems exceed 60% on their best trajectory. AlienLogic begins with higher accuracy because some standard natural-deduction structure remains intact, and its best trajectories reach 58.1–83.8%.

The experiments also test whether performance comes from the system’s choice of probes or merely from receiving useful evidence. Replaying the probes from a system’s best trajectory lowers accuracy for nine of ten systems in AlienCode, while randomized probes provide little benefit. The researchers report a median accuracy of 66.0% for autonomous exploration, compared with 40.7% when those probes are replayed without the system choosing them and 5.7% with fixed probes. In AlienLogic, however, autonomous exploration can change accuracy by between −12.4 and +13.8 percentage points, showing that continued exploration may damage performance.

The results distinguish learning a rule from applying it. Supplying the complete rule set after exploration improves 26 of 30 trajectories by a median of 14.5 points, suggesting that some systems discover useful rules but do not consistently use them. Performance also varies sharply between trajectories and tasks; a system’s ranking in one sandbox barely predicts its ranking in the other.

ExplorationBench is presented as an arXiv preprint, not peer-reviewed research. Its authors emphasize that the benchmark measures acquisition of unfamiliar rules in two deterministic, executable environments—not real scientific discovery, which involves noisy observations, costly or irreversible experiments, open-ended hypotheses and much longer horizons.

PACT: From Credit Assignment to Critic Alignment
science_cs_aipolitics_and_powerscience anchorpreprint_not_peer_reviewed
What happened

The AllSpark Team proposes PACT, a reinforcement-learning training method for large language models that links token-level credit assignment to critic training. The preprint reports higher results than PPO, GRPO and SAO on mathematical reasoning and coding benchmarks, while offering a formal account of how rewards should be attributed across generated tokens.

The work addresses a problem in LLM post-training: reinforcement-learning outcomes are often measured for an entire response, but training signals must determine how that outcome should be assigned to individual tokens. The authors formulate three regularity conditions—Completeness, Prefix Consistency and Neutrality—and prove that, together, they uniquely determine token-level credit. In the resulting representation, a token’s credit is the change in the model’s conditional expected reward between successive prefixes. The same framework can also produce coarser, segment-level credit by aggregating token contributions.

The paper then uses this representation to reinterpret several existing methods. It argues that an ideal teacher in On-Policy Distillation acts as an implicit critic, producing an expected policy-gradient update proportional to that induced by the unique token-level credit. It also shows that response-level REINFORCE Leave-One-Out signals, despite being less granular, can produce the same expected policy-gradient contribution. Further analysis addresses approximate credit sparsity under bounded outcome rewards and examines how errors in Generalized Advantage Estimation can become comparable to the underlying credit signal.

These results motivate Policy Aligned Critic Training. PACT updates the actor first, then retrains the critic against the updated policy. It uses importance-sampling correction for critic training, with ratio filtering, to account for the difference between the rollout policy and the newly updated actor. The goal is to align the critic more closely with the policy whose behavior it is evaluating.

In the reported experiments, PACT reached 72.87% average accuracy across four agentic mathematical-reasoning benchmarks, exceeding GRPO by 8.80 percentage points and PPO by 13.16 points. On SWE-bench Verified, it achieved a 67.4% pass rate, ahead of PPO by 2.4 points, GRPO by 2.0 points and SAO by 3.8 points. The mathematics results used Qwen3.5-4B with OpenCode, while the coding evaluation used Qwen3.6-35B-A3B with Codex.

The work is an arXiv preprint dated September 22, 2026 and has not been peer reviewed; the performance figures and theoretical conclusions are therefore claims reported by the authors.

Coding Agents for Generalized Task and Motion Planning Problems
science_cs_aipolitics_and_powerscience anchorpreprint_not_peer_reviewed
What happened

Coding agents generated reusable programs for simulated task-and-motion-planning problems, outperforming hand-engineered planners and other language-model baselines in the reported evaluation; the result suggests they can reduce specialized engineering while still leaving difficult dynamic tasks largely unsolved.

Task and motion planning (TAMP) combines discrete choices—such as what action to take—with geometric, kinematic and dynamic constraints. The preprint investigates whether coding agents can discover reusable strategies across related problem instances instead of relying on the substantial TAMP-specific components required by existing methods.

For each environment, an agent received a task description, simulator access and a fixed synthesis budget. It could interact with the simulator while writing and refining a program, which was then frozen and tested on unseen initial conditions. The study evaluated Claude Code with Opus 5 and Codex with GPT-5.6 Sol or GPT-6 Astra across 28 simulated environments from the KinDER and PDDLStream benchmarks. These covered two- and three-dimensional kinematic and dynamic tasks, with object counts beyond those used in the original benchmark.

Across seven synthesis methods, the researchers evaluated 980 generated programs on 100 held-out instances each, producing 98,000 evaluation episodes. On the 16 environments with an available planner, the three agent configurations achieved mean success rates ranging from 56% to 95%, compared with 47% for the hand-engineered planners. The agents also outperformed one-shot generation and the LLMGenPlan generalized-planning baseline across all 28 environments. As object counts increased, their programs maintained higher success while using, on average, an order of magnitude less computation per instance than the planner.

The results varied by model and setting. GPT-6 Astra had the highest reported mean success, while Opus 5 outperformed GPT-5.6 Sol in most environments. Giving Opus 5 and GPT-6 Astra access to the environment source code increased their mean success rates from 74% to 84% and from 86% to 95%, respectively, although source access could also make some programs substantially slower.

Interaction appeared to be central to the agents’ behavior: logs showed them calibrating physical models, testing edge cases and refining strategies. They also found unexpected approaches, including non-prehensile maneuvers and uses of the environment layout that the authors say had not appeared in prior work on these benchmarks. However, some dynamic three-dimensional environments—particularly tasks involving sweeping or pouring many small objects—remained largely unsolved. The findings are presented in an arXiv preprint and have not been peer reviewed.

Verifiable Hidden Dynamics Play: Generating Agentic RL Environments from Solved Mechanisms
science_cs_aipolitics_and_powerglobal_south_and_exploitedtechnology_and_aieconomy_and_businessscience anchorpreprint_not_peer_reviewed
What happened

A preprint from researchers at Georgia Institute of Technology and Alibaba proposes VHD-Play, a method for generating stateful reinforcement-learning environments from solved mathematical mechanisms. It aims to help language-model agents learn long-horizon decision-making with hidden information, evolving conditions and delayed outcomes.

Language-model agents can perform well when a problem is written out, yet struggle when the same task requires repeated tool use, information gathering and decisions whose consequences emerge over time. VHD-Play addresses this gap by reversing a common environment-building workflow: instead of creating an interactive setting first and defining its reward or evaluator afterward, it begins with a sampled mathematical model and solves it.

A corpus-grounded system then renders the model’s decision process as stateful tools and interfaces. The executable dynamics and the trajectory-scoring reference are derived from the same solved mechanism, so the environment’s outcomes can be checked against a known solution. The interfaces can also hide selected parameters and reveal them through interaction, creating information-asymmetric tasks rather than simply presenting all problem data at once.

The preprint reports 3,300 admitted environments, each with at least 10 tools, covering 28 topical domains and multiple mechanism families including inventory optimization, routing, knapsack, linear and quadratic programming, scheduling, search and negotiation. The reported cost is approximately $0.01 to $0.03 per admitted environment, and episodes average 59.7 assistant turns.

Training Qwen3.6-35B-A3B on 2,200 environments from three mechanism families raised its mean agentic score across five optimization families from 0.204 to 0.815. Gains were reported on held-out instances from the training families and on 800 environments spanning eight unseen families. The paper also reports improvements on external function-calling and tool-use evaluations that used their native protocols and shared neither instances nor interfaces with the generated training environments.

The experiments indicate that the main difficulty is not always solving the underlying optimization problem. In the paper’s comparison, the same tasks scored a macro average of 0.962 in written-out form but 0.204 in agentic form for the base model. The authors report that most of this gap is associated with stateful interaction rather than the mathematical problem alone. The trained checkpoint also improved the agentic score to 0.815 while retaining near-perfect performance on the written-out versions.

These findings are presented as research claims in an arXiv preprint. The proposed mechanism-first pipeline is intended to make agentic training environments easier to expand while keeping their dynamics and evaluation tied to a common, solver-derived reference.

Glucosamine, a popular joint supplement, linked to faster Alzheimer’s progression
science_generalpublic_healthscience anchorscience_journalism
What happened

A University of Florida study has linked glucosamine use with faster progression from mild cognitive impairment to dementia, and with higher mortality among people who already had Alzheimer’s disease or related dementias. The findings raise concerns about a widely used joint supplement but do not prove it causes worsening disease.

Mild cognitive impairment involves measurable memory or thinking problems that are greater than expected with normal aging but do not necessarily substantially disrupt daily life. Using artificial intelligence, researchers analyzed deidentified UF Health records collected from 2012 to 2024. Glucosamine use was reported by 8% of patients in both groups: 2,750 people with MCI and 1,896 with Alzheimer’s disease or related dementias.

After accounting for age, sex and demographics, users with MCI had a 25% higher likelihood of progressing to dementia. Among those who already had Alzheimer’s disease or related dementias, glucosamine use was associated with a 25% higher mortality risk; the same association was not observed in the MCI group. Researchers cautioned that the health-record analysis was observational, so differences between supplement users and non-users could help explain the results.

The team also investigated a possible biological pathway involving the attachment of sugar structures to proteins. Analysis of human Alzheimer’s brain tissue found more of this sugar tagging than in normal controls. In genetically modified mice, glucosamine increased the process and was associated with worse social-memory deficits, while a chemical treatment that suppressed it improved memory performance.

The study, published in *Nature Metabolism*, suggests that altered metabolism may contribute to neurodegeneration alongside better-known Alzheimer’s features such as amyloid plaques and tau tangles. The researchers said the results are preliminary and require a controlled human clinical trial to determine whether glucosamine directly accelerates progression, which patients might be most vulnerable, and whether people should change their use of the supplement.

One CRISPR treatment cut “bad” cholesterol in half for a full year

What happened

A first-in-human Phase 1 trial from Cleveland Clinic found that a single infusion of the experimental CRISPR-Cas9 therapy CTX310 produced sustained reductions in blood fats among 15 people with lipid disorders that had not responded adequately to medication. After 12 months, participants receiving the highest dose had a 52.5% reduction in LDL (“bad”) cholesterol and a 47.8% reduction in triglycerides from baseline. No serious adverse events related to the therapy were reported during the one-year follow-up.

CTX310 delivers the gene-editing system to the liver to switch off ANGPTL3, a gene involved in regulating fats circulating in the blood. The findings were presented at the 2026 European Society of Cardiology annual meeting and published simultaneously in the *New England Journal of Medicine*. The therapy remains experimental; researchers plan to continue safety monitoring for another 15 years. The study was funded by CRISPR Therapeutics AG, and Cleveland Clinic cardiologist Luke Laffin, its first author, said larger studies are needed.

Why rheumatoid arthritis pain may persist even when inflammation is under control
science_generalpublic_healthscience anchorscience_journalism
What happened

Researchers at Semmelweis University say rheumatoid arthritis symptoms can persist even when joint inflammation improves because factors beyond inflammation may help sustain them. Their analysis examined links between difficult-to-treat rheumatoid arthritis and depression, smoking, obesity, sleep disorders, and other health conditions. Most patients respond to treatment, but an estimated 6–28% do not achieve lasting remission despite therapy; in Hungary, the disease affects tens of thousands of people.

The team’s publications in *Nature Reviews Rheumatology* and *The Lancet Rheumatology* describe a model intended to help doctors identify what is maintaining symptoms and tailor treatment earlier. Under the treat-to-target approach, improving inflammation alongside continuing pain or fatigue could prompt clinicians to investigate chronic pain, depression, sleep problems, or obesity rather than automatically increasing medication. The researchers describe a possible cycle in which pain and depression reduce activity, contribute to weight gain, and worsen sleep and mood, which can then intensify pain and make daily activities harder. They also plan to study whether artificial intelligence can identify patient subgroups for more personalized treatment.

New soil test could reveal how soil microbes help feed plants
science_generalglobal_south_and_exploitedscience anchorscience_journalism
What happened

Researchers have developed a simpler, less expensive test for measuring DNA-bound phosphorus (DNA-P) in soil, a biologically active form associated with living microorganisms. The method was refined by an international team including researchers from Sultan Qaboos University, the James Hutton Institute and Oman’s Environment Authority, in a study published in the *Journal of Agricultural and Marine Sciences*. The researchers applied it to 32 soil types from across the United Kingdom.

DNA-P makes up only a small share of total organic phosphorus, but its concentration showed strong relationships with soil pH, microbial biomass phosphorus, organic matter and phosphorus dissolved in soil water. These relationships suggest it may offer a window into the more active, microbe-influenced part of the soil phosphorus cycle and help investigate how microbes affect phosphorus available to plants. The revised procedure removed unnecessary enzyme treatments while retaining the precision and sensitivity needed for measurements. Ultrafiltration remained essential because it separates DNA-bound phosphorus from other phosphorus-containing compounds. The researchers say the approach could support further work on soil fertility, nutrient management and more efficient use of finite phosphorus resources.

Scientists say house cats could help unlock better cancer treatments for humans
science_generalpublic_healthscience anchorscience_journalism
What happened

Scientists have mapped the genetic changes driving cancers in almost 500 domestic cats across five countries, identifying similarities with human and dog tumors. The study could improve treatment for cats and provide clues about how genes and shared environments influence cancer in people.

Cancer is a leading cause of illness and death in cats, but researchers have historically known much less about the mutations behind feline tumors than about human or canine cancers. The international study, published in *Science*, analyzed tumor DNA on an unusually large scale and created a freely available resource for further research.

The team found that many “cancer driver” genes—genes involved in controlling cell growth, division, damage repair or cell death—also appear in human and dog cancers. In aggressive feline mammary tumors, more than half of the samples carried mutations in FBXW7, a gene that normally helps regulate proteins involved in cell growth. In human breast cancer, changes in the same gene are associated with a worse prognosis.

Researchers also found similarities across cancers affecting cats’ blood, bones, lungs, skin, gastrointestinal system and central nervous system. Because household cats can share exposure to smoke, air pollution, chemicals and other environmental factors with their owners, naturally occurring cancers in pets may help scientists study interactions between genetics and surroundings.

The findings also point toward precision oncology, in which treatment is guided by a tumor’s molecular features. Some chemotherapy drugs were more effective against cat mammary-tumor tissue carrying mutated FBXW7. However, that result came from tissue samples and does not yet show that the approach will work in living cats or people.

The researchers used tissues veterinarians had already collected for diagnostic purposes, rather than gathering all-new samples. They say the database can support more personalized cancer care for cats and help knowledge move between veterinary and human medicine.

1.7-billion-year-old fossils reveal a crucial clue to the rise of complex life
science_generalscience anchorscience_journalism
What happened

Mudstone cores stored at the Northern Territory Geological Survey in Darwin, Australia, contain microscopic fossils from an ancient inland sea that covered much of northern Australia more than 1.5 billion years ago. A study published in *Nature* examined these rocks to investigate the origin of eukaryotes—the complex cells found in animals, plants, algae and fungi. The oldest fossils in the collection date to about 1.75 billion years ago and are described as the oldest currently known eukaryote fossils.

Researchers crushed and dissolved samples of the cores, identifying more than 12,000 fossils in the remaining organic material. They also analysed the surrounding mudstones and their chemistry to reconstruct the environments where the organisms lived. The fossils occurred in settings ranging from coastal mudflats to the open sea, but only where the sediments showed that oxygen was present. Oxygen-free samples contained only simpler prokaryotic forms, such as bacteria and archaea. The results suggest that even the earliest known eukaryotes, living between 1.7 and 1.4 billion years ago, depended on oxygen, supporting the hypothesis that oxygen helped drive their evolution.
