‘Everything is gone’

What happened

A devastating flood has destroyed much of Devighat, a riverside commercial centre in Nepal’s Nuwakot district, leaving homes, businesses, bridges and roads buried or washed away. Residents fear people and animals may still be trapped beneath the mud, while authorities continue rescue work and begin assessing the scale of reconstruction.

Devighat reduced to ruins

The Trishuli River now runs through what was once a settled neighbourhood near the China-Nepal border. Its banks have been torn open and covered with broken concrete, stones, debris and heaps of mud. Buildings stand as stripped skeletons, with doors and windows gone; some mud stains reach the fourth floor. Collapsed walls and exposed foundations show the force of the August 26 floods.

Devighat’s commercial centre lies where the Trishuli and Tadi rivers meet, about 60 kilometres northwest of Kathmandu. A steel Bailey bridge has been shattered in the water, while sandals, quilts, mattresses, plastic chairs and broken gas cylinders remain scattered along the riverbank. Many homes are empty, and a heavy stench of decay hangs over the ruins.

Local resident Devika Podel said vast amounts of mud, boulders and debris swept down the Trishuli, carrying dead people with them. She fears human and animal remains may still be buried beneath the thick mud. Recalling the shaking ground during the disaster, she compared it with the 2015 earthquake.

Podel said nearly 600 houses and structures had been destroyed. She had operated a small business in Devighat but said she had now lost everything. Govind Prasad Dhahal, an elderly businessman, said a few thousand people had lived in the area and that it had been an important business centre. His rice and grain shop, he said, was now beneath the rubble.

Residents are sheltering in temporary tents at a nearby school. They received warning calls from upstream Betrawati Bazar at about 9am, but many initially dismissed them because a similar warning the previous year had produced only a minor flood. Some people went to the riverbanks to record videos; Podel said many of those people were now missing.

Floodwaters arrived within minutes

A 20-member delegation from the International Centre for Integrated Mountain Development, including government officials, aid workers and journalists from Bangladesh, India, Japan and Bhutan, visited the disaster sites on Tuesday. Sudan Bikash Maharjan, a remote sensing analyst with ICIMOD’s Cryosphere department, said the disaster began nearly 80 kilometres upstream and the water reached Devighat within minutes.

The Trishuli’s flow in that area normally ranges from 200 to 500 cubic metres per second during the monsoon, Maharjan said. On the day of the disaster, it reached 17,000 cubic metres per second, carrying sediment and boulders. He said the waters struck a hill and were diverted, washing away a steel bridge over the Tadi River.

At Trishuli Bazar, 13 kilometres upstream from Devighat, the delegation found two bridges linking Trishuli Bazar with Nuwakot Bazar completely destroyed. Maharjan said around 7,000 households and commercial establishments had been destroyed across Nuwakot, and that a power plant was largely buried or washed away.

The flood also destroyed 42 kilometres of the road connecting Nuwakot with the Chinese border, a key trade route. Residents and business owners had moved elsewhere, according to Maharjan. In Devighat, flood marks remained at least 20 feet above the ground, illustrating the height reached by the water and sediment.

National losses remain incomplete

Nepal’s National Disaster Risk Reduction and Management Authority said in its September 12 report that 1,386 people had been declared dead and at least 5,130 remained missing. Rescue teams had rescued 13,728 people. The same report recorded the complete destruction of 4,800 commercial establishments, 13 hydropower plants, 68 suspension bridges, 37 motorable bridges and 69 kilometres of roads.

Those figures remain provisional. Badri Pyakurel, an NDRRMA focal person, said the assessment was not complete and that estimating the total damage would take time. The Nepal Army-led rescue mission was still searching for bodies in the debris, while some remote areas had not yet been reached by rescue teams.

Maharjan said the government would assess buildings still standing before allowing people to return to their homes. Nepal’s initial damage and needs assessment estimates that $4.77 billion will be required to fund recovery and rebuilding. Much of that cost will involve infrastructure destroyed by the floods, including roads, bridges and hydropower plants.

For Devighat’s residents, recovery will mean rebuilding not only homes but also businesses and a local economy that has been swept into the river. As Dhahal put it, “Everything is gone.”

Fiji declares HIV emergency. One in 60 people now have the virus as drug crisis fuels outbreak

What happened

Fiji has declared a national HIV emergency after testing revealed a rapidly expanding outbreak, with an estimated one in 60 adults living with the virus. The crisis is affecting people who inject drugs, sexually active adults, pregnant women and newborns, while limited diagnosis and treatment leave many cases unidentified.

The emergency was declared on Tuesday by Health Minister Dr. Atonio Lalabalavu, who said Fiji recorded 2,060 new HIV diagnoses in 2025. He described the figure as evidence of the epidemic’s seriousness and the need for an urgent, coordinated response.

Fiji, an archipelago of less than a million people, first declared an HIV outbreak in January 2025 and expanded testing. The resulting estimates indicate that one in every 60 adults is living with HIV, compared with one in 167 Fijians estimated to have been living with the virus five years earlier.

The increase contrasts with a global decline in new infections. UNAIDS adviser Renata Ram said worldwide new HIV infections fell by an estimated 42% between 2010 and 2025, while Fiji experienced an “extraordinary increase” over roughly the same period.

Injecting drug use has become a major transmission route

The outbreak has several contributing factors, but injecting drug use has become a rapidly accelerating route of transmission, Ram said. Fiji has emerged as a transit point for narcotics trafficked from the Americas toward markets in Australia, New Zealand and parts of Asia. Methamphetamine stockpiles smuggled onto the islands have also contributed to a local addiction crisis and placed additional strain on the health system.

National health statistics cited in the reporting show that people who inject drugs accounted for roughly half of new HIV cases in 2025 where the transmission route was identified. Sexual transmission accounted for most of the remainder. Needle sharing, low condom use, multiple sexual partners, limited testing and stigma were identified as factors making the outbreak harder to control.

The health minister said one in 50 pregnant women in Fiji is infected with HIV. The virus can pass from an infected mother to her infant during pregnancy, labor and delivery or breastfeeding. Save the Children Fiji said 59 babies acquired HIV from their mothers last year.

The epidemic has also reached newborns directly. Save the Children Fiji said a two-month-old baby became the youngest identified patient living with both HIV and tuberculosis. HIV weakens the immune system and makes it easier for a person to contract TB.

Many people do not know their status

The available figures may not represent the full scale of the crisis. Ram said only about 39% of people living with HIV in Fiji know their status, while fewer than a quarter—22%—are receiving treatment. Without treatment, HIV can progress to AIDS, leaving the body highly vulnerable to serious illnesses and cancers.

Fiji recorded 117 HIV-related deaths in 2025, according to national health statistics, compared with 25 in 2021. The low treatment rate also differs sharply from the global average cited in the evidence, where 78% of people living with HIV are accessing treatment.

Joeli Colati, executive director of Fiji Network Plus, a community organization supporting people living with HIV, said the barriers extend beyond healthcare. Stigma, poverty, transport problems, concerns about confidentiality and fear can all discourage people from seeking care. Colati, who has lived with HIV for 19 years, said some people fear being recognized at an HIV clinic.

He also pointed to an information gap, including limited understanding of how HIV is transmitted and how effective treatment can be. In Fiji’s deeply religious society, premarital and casual sex are generally considered taboo, which can further complicate testing and conversations about prevention.

Fiji plans expanded testing and prevention

Lalabalavu said Fiji would move quickly to expand testing and treatment and improve access to pre-exposure prophylaxis, or PrEP, a medication used to help prevent HIV infection. The government also plans to introduce a formal needle-and-syringe program.

The minister urged anyone who may have been exposed through shared needles, other injecting equipment or sex without a condom to seek testing before becoming unwell. He also called on the public not to shame, exclude or threaten people because of their HIV status, saying stigma drives people away from services that can help them.

Colati similarly urged people not to let fear determine whether they seek care. He said knowing one’s HIV status can provide the ability to take action, while treatment, support and hope can make a difference for people living with the virus.

Pentagon findings put the images in a wider picture

The inspector general’s report, published the day before the images were reported, said Iranian strikes had damaged or destroyed hundreds of buildings and structures at US bases in Kuwait, Bahrain, Qatar, the United Arab Emirates, Saudi Arabia, Iraq, Oman and Jordan. The report said dozens of US aircraft had been damaged or destroyed after the conflict began, including five refuelling aircraft damaged or destroyed on the ground in Saudi Arabia. A separate account cited losses including four F-15 fighters destroyed, one F-35 damaged, seven KC-135 tanker aircraft damaged and as many as 30 MQ-9 Reaper drones destroyed. It cautioned that its review involved uncertainty because the Department of Defense had not answered requests for information, leaving it reliant on government databases and public reports.

Brian Katulis, a former national security official and senior fellow at the Middle East Institute, said the visible damage indicated that the United States had not adequately prepared its defences. He also argued that the conflict had weakened broader military readiness by diverting assets from Asia and depleting munitions.

The inspector general separately reported “strategic inventory shortfalls” and bottlenecks in the industrial base for resupplying munitions. It said the Pentagon was working to accelerate procurement and production and stockpile critical materials, but warned that expanding production capacity would take significant time. Those findings contradicted President Donald Trump and Secretary of War Pete Hegseth’s claims that the war had not caused munitions shortfalls.

The scale and timing of repairs remain unclear. The reports do not establish whether every damaged base will be rebuilt or who will pay for the infrastructure work. They do show that the consequences extended beyond individual buildings: equipment losses, possible replacement of an obsolete radar aircraft and shortages of munitions have become part of the dispute over the war’s cost and its effect on US military capacity.

Venezuela’s People Problem
global_south_and_exploitedwar_and_conflictpolitics_and_powerpublic_healtheconomy_and_businessmaterial_harmmajor supplement
What happened

Two earthquakes in northern Venezuela exposed more than the country’s vulnerability to natural disasters: they revealed how far its public institutions and professional workforce have deteriorated. The missing equipment, depleted emergency capacity, and shortage of technical expertise could complicate both disaster recovery and plans to rebuild the oil-rich country.

The earthquakes struck on June 24, killing at least 6,000 people and injuring tens of thousands, according to the supplied account. Days later, Venezuela’s Seismological Research Foundation, known as Funvisis, appealed publicly for the return of a GPS sensor that had disappeared from a rocky outcrop in western Venezuela.

The instrument had monitored millimeter-scale movements along the Boconó fault for 13 years. The fault was considered the likely source of the first earthquake. When technicians went to retrieve the data stored by the device, they found it missing. More than two months later, Funvisis still did not know who had taken it.

The missing sensor became a symbol of a wider institutional collapse. Venezuela once had between 250 and 300 seismic-monitoring stations; no more than five were still functioning. The country had also lost many of the engineers, doctors, nurses, firefighters, and rescue workers needed during a disaster, after a generation-long exodus of skilled professionals from state institutions.

How expertise was driven out of public institutions

The account attributes Venezuela’s decline from Latin America’s wealthiest nation to one of its poorest over two decades to mismanagement, corruption, and poor governance that began under Hugo Chávez, president from 1999 to 2013, and continued under Nicolás Maduro. Interim President Delcy Rodríguez, who took power after the United States captured Maduro in January, inherited a state lacking the technical expertise to rebuild damage caused by the earthquakes or repair an economy that had collapsed earlier.

The central problem is presented as a shortage of human capital, not simply money. Bureaucrats, planners, regulators, and inspectors are needed to turn funding into functioning power systems, ports, water networks, and clinics. The account says Chávez’s government treated professional expertise as politically suspect and replaced experienced managers with loyalists and military officers in major public utilities.

The Caracas water company Hidrocapital, the Caracas metro, and the electricity company Edelca were among the institutions affected. Water and power shortages became common. Edelca’s Caruachi dam, begun in 1997, took nine years to complete. The Tocoma dam, begun in 2007, remained unfinished despite almost $10 billion in investment. A Brazilian contractor later admitted paying as much as $98 million in bribes to Venezuelan officials and intermediaries between 2005 and 2016 to secure contracts.

The same pattern reached Funvisis and other scientific institutions. At Venezuela’s leading scientific-research center, physicist Claudio Mendoza was dismissed after criticizing the country’s rejection of scientific expertise. The account says scientists were systematically purged between 2006 and 2011. Venezuela fell from fifth to 11th among Latin American producers of scientific papers between the early 2000s and 2022; by 2016, researchers who had emigrated produced roughly 33 percent of the country’s scientific publications.

The oil industry lost its technical base

The consequences were particularly severe in the oil sector, which is central to any recovery plan. Before Chávez, the state-owned oil company PDVSA operated with substantial autonomy and had expanded internationally, including through its acquisition of Citgo in 1991. Its research arm, the Venezuelan Institute of Oil Technology, or INTEVEP, employed about 170 full-time Ph.D. researchers and held roughly 1,200 U.S. patents before 2003.

That year, Chávez fired PDVSA’s board and appointed a military officer to run the company. After workers went on strike, he dismissed more than 18,000 employees—between 60 and 80 percent of staff in provisioning, planning, maintenance, and exploration. INTEVEP’s patents fell to about 100 over the following decade, while party loyalists and military personnel filled vacancies.

PDVSA’s payroll expanded from 28,841 employees in 2003 to 177,770 in 2014, while oil production fell from about 3 million barrels a day before the purge to an average of 512,000 barrels a day in 2020. Former employees moved abroad, including to Alberta and Colombia, where Venezuelan oil specialists contributed to a near doubling of Colombian production by 2011.

President Trump has said Venezuela will be run “properly” and “professionally” and has promised that American investors will spend $100 billion rebuilding its energy infrastructure. But the account argues that funding cannot replace the professionals needed to plan and execute that work. Many former experts have emigrated permanently; others distrust the government or do not want to return to institutions that removed them.

The United States is overseeing talks between Rodríguez’s government and the democratic opposition over recovery and reconciliation. The proposed solution is to make expertise, rather than political loyalty, the basis for public appointments and to rebuild confidence among Venezuelan professionals.

Funvisis is identified as an early priority, both for public safety and for oil exploration. Seismic data help companies assess underground reservoirs before drilling, but Venezuela’s records are outdated, its instruments scarce, and many of its geophysicists now work in Calgary, Houston, or Bogotá. The account’s conclusion is that Washington may provide money or lift sanctions, but only Venezuelans can restore the state’s professional capacity—and determine whether those who left will return.

The researchers argue that question–answer supervision constrains the probability of producing an answer, but does not uniquely determine the latent reasoning trajectory used to reach it. Different optimization choices—including random seeds, training paths, fine-tuning methods, and hyperparameters—can therefore produce specialists with different trajectory distributions while using the same data and answer objective.

Rather than treating distillation only as a way to build a smaller model, the study uses it to observe what a specialist’s generated trajectories carry. The student does not inherit the specialist’s parameters or optimization constraints; it receives only sampled trajectories and answers. Differences among the resulting students therefore provide an indirect probe of the specialist’s latent behavior, according to the paper.

Across 27 specialist–student pairings, the researchers report a very strong correlation between specialization and generalization profiles. Students generally reflected the balance established by their corresponding specialists across in-task, in-domain, and out-of-domain evaluations. This relationship held across chemistry, physics, and low-resource multilingual settings and across different model families.

The paper’s central claim is that specialist optimization becomes a form of supervision design when gold reasoning is absent. Training choices determine which answer-consistent trajectories receive high probability, and those selected trajectories become the rationale data used for downstream learning.

The experiments compare full fine-tuning (FFT), Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA), Layer-Selective Tuning (LST), and Anchored Supervised Fine-Tuning (ASFT). What happened

Mind2Dialogue proposes training language models to respond to people’s unspoken beliefs, goals and changing circumstances by simulating those mental states. Its authors report large gains in personalization and some theory-of-mind evaluations, while results vary across models and the work remains an arXiv preprint.

The framework addresses what the authors describe as a supervision gap in assistant training. Existing dialogue datasets rarely contain responses explicitly grounded in a user’s private beliefs, goals or circumstances, while real users’ underlying states are not directly observable. Private conversations between people who know one another well could provide such examples, but collecting them at scale would require consent and substantial annotation.

Mind2Dialogue instead generates training interactions with a psychology-guided user simulator, called M2D-Sim. The simulator preserves a user’s personal characteristics while updating beliefs, goals, emotions and circumstances as the conversation develops. A behavior controller varies how the simulated user communicates, and scenarios give that user reasons to seek assistance across multiple turns and sessions.

The central design is a shared, evolving mental state. That state drives the simulated user’s behavior and is also visible to an Oracle assistant while it generates a response. The Oracle therefore has information about what the user believes, wants or feels, even when those factors are only partly expressed in the dialogue. The resulting exchanges form the M2D-Corpus.

How privileged supervision is transferred

Mind2Dialogue uses privileged distillation to transfer the Oracle’s state-informed responses to a deployable student model, referred to as M2D-Chat. During supervised fine-tuning, the student receives the observable conversation and the Oracle’s response targets, but not the evolving mental state that generated the interaction. The state is also withheld during inference.

This creates an information asymmetry: the teacher can demonstrate how to respond to the person behind a request, while the student must learn to provide assistance without direct access to the simulator’s internal state. The authors present this as a way to use hidden-state information for training without requiring users to expose such information at deployment.

The paper evaluates human-aware learning through two related capabilities. Personalization measures whether a model acts on a user’s preferences and circumstances. Theory of mind examines whether it can reason about beliefs and actions. The evaluation uses independently constructed benchmarks whose content was excluded from the training corpus, including PersonaMem-v1, PersonaMem-v2, PrefEval, ToMi and BigToM.

Reported gains across model families

Training on the full Mind2Dialogue corpus improved every reported personalization metric over corresponding instruction-tuned Qwen, Llama and OLMo baselines. The authors report gains of 26.6 to 40.9 percentage points in preference-following generation.

For Qwen2.5-7B, the paper reports a 33.4-point improvement on PrefEval generation and a 10.0-point gain in PersonaMem-v2 multiple-choice accuracy compared with the base model. The reported improvements were not limited to personalized assistance: Qwen and Llama also improved across all three evaluated theory-of-mind tasks. Qwen gained 13.0 points on BigToM forward-belief accuracy.

The results were not uniform across models. OLMo improved on ToMi but declined on both BigToM tasks, indicating that the reported benefits for mental-state reasoning varied with the underlying model. The paper therefore presents Mind2Dialogue as improving several measured capabilities, rather than as producing consistent gains on every evaluation for every model.

The authors argue that realistic user simulation alone is insufficient: a simulator may generate plausible behavior without giving an assistant reliable targets for interpreting and acting on implicit intentions. Mind2Dialogue’s distinctive contribution is to let the Oracle observe the state that produces that behavior, then use its responses as supervision for a model that does not receive the state directly.

The work is presented in arXiv version 1, dated 14 September 2026, and is identified as a preprint that has not undergone peer review. Its longer-term proposal is to use user simulation as a foundation for AI collaborators that can understand the beliefs and intentions behind people’s words and support their goals in education, work and everyday life.

Continuous-wave laser absorption spectroscopy of the thorium-229 nucleus

What happened

Continuous-wave laser absorption spectroscopy has detected the low-energy nuclear transition of thorium-229 directly in a calcium fluoride crystal, replacing the slower fluorescence readout used in earlier work and providing a faster route toward a highly stable solid-state optical nuclear clock.

The 8.4-electronvolt transition in thorium-229 (Th-229) is attractive for nuclear-clock applications because its frequency could provide a very accurate and stable reference. The transition occurs at a wavelength of about 148 nanometres in the vacuum-ultraviolet (VUV) range. Earlier experiments excited the nuclei using pulsed laser systems, where only a small fraction of the photons matched the narrow nuclear resonance, and detected the excitation through fluorescence.

The study demonstrates a different approach: a narrow-bandwidth, continuous-wave (CW) solid-state laser excites the Th-229 nuclei at sub-nanowatt power, while the signal is measured from the small reduction in laser power transmitted through the crystal. Because the measurement observes absorption directly, it does not have to wait for the excited nuclei to emit their slowly decaying fluorescence.

A narrow-bandwidth source changes the detection method

The VUV light is generated by frequency-converting an infrared diode-laser system operating at 1,187 nanometres. The system produces radiation near 148.4 nanometres through successive frequency-doubling stages, including a final conversion in a strontium tetraborate (SrB₄O₇, or SBO) crystal. About 1 nanowatt of VUV power is generated before optical losses; approximately 70 picowatts reaches the detector after passing through mirrors, viewports, lenses and the thorium-doped sample.

The laser can be operated with a linewidth estimated at 10 kilohertz or less when stabilized to a high-finesse cavity. This is substantially better matched to the crystal-broadened nuclear resonance than earlier four-wave-mixing sources. In the absorption measurement, the laser frequency is alternated between the resonance and a point 2 megahertz away, and the difference in transmitted intensity is used to calculate the absorption.

The method avoids a major inefficiency of fluorescence detection. In calcium fluoride, the Th-229 excited state has a radiative decay time of about 600 seconds. Earlier fluorescence measurements therefore required long illumination and detection intervals, and sometimes additional waiting for the nuclear population to return to the ground state. Absorption measures the excitation essentially in real time, so the interrogation sequence is determined by signal-to-noise optimization rather than by the radiative lifetime.

The researchers report that absorption recordings are typically five times faster than fluorescence measurements. With frequency modulation and the narrow-linewidth laser, the detection cycle for one resonance was reduced by about two orders of magnitude compared with the fluorescence approach.

Two thorium environments reveal different crystal fields

The experiment examined thorium-doped calcium fluoride, using a crystal segment about 3.1 millimetres in diameter and 4.2 millimetres long. The researchers observed absorption lines from two distinct thorium environments, or dopant centres, in the crystal. Five lines were associated with a thorium dimer defect known as the D-centre, while a further line was attributed to the O-centre, whose proposed defect structure has octahedral symmetry.

The D-centre lines had widths between 339 and 472 kilohertz in the initial measurements, with the linewidth limited largely by the laser. With improved laser stabilization, the researchers measured a 91(2)-kilohertz full width at half maximum for one D-centre transition. The absorption signal also produced an error signal with a zero crossing at resonance, a feature that can be used to stabilize the laser frequency directly to the nuclear transition.

The O-centre displayed a broader line of about 1.1 megahertz. Even so, its line shape allowed the researchers to place an upper bound of less than 0.1 volts per square angstrom on the relevant static electric-field gradient at the thorium nucleus. That is far below the roughly 100 volts per square angstrom reported for the D-centre. The result indicates that the ions surrounding the O-centre have a highly symmetric arrangement, making its nuclear resonance less sensitive to changes in lattice spacing.

The frequency difference between the O- and D-centres was measured as 3.99(2) megahertz. The researchers attribute this isomeric shift to the different electronic environments around the thorium nuclei and report agreement between the measured shift and calculations of the proposed defect structures.

Implications for a solid-state nuclear clock

The absorption signal is small: the estimated absorption probability for individual transitions is between 0.15% and 0.5%, and fewer than one in 10 million nuclei is excited at any given time. Nevertheless, the transmitted laser beam provides a sufficiently strong signal for photon-counting detection, with a detected flux of about 6 million photons per second.

Using the measured linewidth, absorption fraction and photon flux, the researchers estimate a shot-noise-limited fractional frequency instability of approximately 2 times 10^-12/sqrttau, where tau is the averaging time in seconds. They project that higher VUV power, cavity-enhanced frequency doubling, lower thorium concentrations and alternative crystal hosts could improve performance by more than two orders of magnitude. Their stated expectation is an instability of 10^-16 or better after about 10^4 seconds, although this is a projected improvement rather than a demonstrated clock result.

The work establishes direct absorption detection of the Th-229 nuclear transition and shows that a CW VUV source can combine narrow spectral width, efficient use of laser power and rapid readout. It also provides a way to compare different thorium sites in a solid through laser Mössbauer spectroscopy, which probes how the nuclear resonance responds to its crystal environment.
