What happened

Washington’s pressure campaign has left Iran threatening escalation even as Tehran and Oman keep working on a possible Strait of Hormuz arrangement, creating a standoff between sanctions-and-strikes pressure and a fragile diplomatic track that could still collapse.

US President Donald Trump has dismissed diplomacy with the Islamic Republic, saying a June ceasefire agreement “isn’t worth the paper it’s written on,” while his administration has piled on sanctions, military strikes and a maritime blockade that stops goods from leaving or entering Iran. CNN said the multifront campaign has put the country under unprecedented pressure and added strain to a fatigued population.

Washington’s pressure campaign

The White House’s approach, as described in the report, is not framed as a push to bring Iran back to talks so much as a campaign of coercion. Trump said last week on Truth Social that he liked “our position now much better,” citing “almost total control of the Hormuz Strait” and Iran’s “economy totally collapsing,” and he added that Iranians were “just playing out the inevitable.”

The reporting also said US military pressure has continued alongside the economic campaign. It cited last week’s strike that killed 18 people, including four at a wedding celebration and a child, after shrapnel from a US strike hit a house in a village along the Strait of Hormuz, according to Iranian officials. The same account said the US Navy has escorted vessels carrying crude out of the strait, while regional producers such as the United Arab Emirates and Saudi Arabia have shifted some exports away from Hormuz.

Tehran’s response and internal strain

Iranian military officials are increasingly responding with threats of their own. They have been touting new tactics, warning that nations taking part in the blockade could be targeted and that the conflict could broaden. Mohsen Rezaei, the head of Iran’s Supreme National Security Council, said in an interview with Iranian media on Sunday: “We concluded that new strategies must be adopted in the war, in negotiations, and in confronting the blockade.”

Rezaei also suggested Iran could widen its own blockade beyond the strait by creating a new “exclusion zone” in the Persian Gulf and the Gulf of Oman. Other officials and lawmakers, CNN said, have been sending deterrent messages about new weaponry and tactics, including attacks on warships and bases. Hamidreza Azizi of the International Crisis Group said Tehran is trying to balance a war-weary population that needs economic relief against an ideological base that rejects capitulation.

The same report said Mojtaba Khamenei remains absent and that Tuesday would mark his sixth month in hiding, which it linked to an attempt to avoid the fate of his father and predecessor, Ayatollah Ali Khamenei, who CNN said was killed in an airstrike at the start of the US-Israeli war against Iran in late February.

The Strait of Hormuz track

Even as the rhetoric hardens, Iran and Oman have been slowly negotiating management of the Strait of Hormuz over the past few weeks. On Monday, Iranian Foreign Ministry spokesman Esmaeil Baghaei said a “temporary safe route” through the strait would likely be registered with the International Maritime Organization in the “coming days.” it remains unclear whether the US would recognize any agreement reached between Iran and Oman.

Qatari Foreign Ministry spokesperson Majed al-Ansari told CNN’s Becky Anderson that mediators see two main obstacles to ending the conflict: political will and technical solutions. He said political will depends on finding “the right narrative” for the parties to come out of the crisis, while the technical side involves “juggling four or five balls at the same time” to find the needed solutions.

CNN said Iran’s top officials, including President Masoud Pezeshkian, have repeatedly signaled they want to return to the June agreement, which observers view as favorable to Tehran. But hard-line conservatives inside Iran remain critical of any deal with the US, accusing the president and his negotiators of compromising wartime gains. Hossein Taeb, head of the hard-line Basij paramilitary volunteer group, said Monday that “the Iranian nation accepts neither threats nor coercion, nor negotiations whose outcome would be the weakening of the country’s rights.”

A fragile path back to talks

That leaves Iran, in the report’s framing, trying to avoid both pressure without compromise and compromise without domestic backlash. Azizi said the preferred outcome is diplomacy that can be presented at home as strength rather than retreat, but he warned that once both sides test each other’s red lines, the risk of miscalculation rises sharply.

In that view, the immediate question is whether the limited diplomatic track around Hormuz can hold long enough to ease pressure, or whether the blockade, strikes and counter-threats move the confrontation further toward the kind of calibrated escalation both sides are already signaling.

A result that reaches far beyond Magdeburg

The scale of the win matters because it would be the first time the far right has taken the helm of a German state since World War II. Saxony-Anhalt also gives AfD its strongest state result yet, and the party’s leaders have treated it as a mandate to govern.

Friedrich Merz, the chancellor and CDU leader, said Monday in Berlin: “We are all deeply shaken. This electoral result will have consequences. We cannot simply return to normality.” He also said the coalition government — made up of the CDU, its Bavarian sister party the CSU and the SPD — has “an extremely low standing nationwide.”

Pressure inside the coalition

The defeat immediately widened tensions inside the federal coalition. Merz said Germany has to own up to its mistakes even if the rise of the radicals is part of a wider EU trend. The SPD signaled the same kind of alarm from its side. The Social Democrats were not hit as hard as the CDU in Saxony-Anhalt: they won 9.3% and will have eight deputies, only one fewer than in the previous legislature. Even so, the result has shaken the coalition’s foundations, with both governing parties acknowledging the political blow.

What happens in Saxony-Anhalt next

AfD still does not automatically control the state. Its 39 seats fall short of an absolute majority, and other parties have maintained the firewall against entering government with it. But the party believes the Saxony-Anhalt result could still open the door.

Thomas Schulze, the BSW candidate in Saxony-Anhalt, said Monday that his party is willing to abstain in a third vote so that AfD could take power with a simple majority. That possibility keeps the question of government formation open, even after the scale of AfD’s win.

Merz also tried to reassure outside observers, saying institutions will guarantee that a hypothetical AfD-led regional government would not destabilize German democracy. At the same time, he warned that Saxony-Anhalt could be only the first chapter in a broader far-right wave in the 2027 election cycle, when France, Italy, Spain and Poland are due to vote.

Rabid seals bite at least 176 people as fatal disease spreads, threatening other species
global_south_and_exploitedpublic_healthmaterial_harmregional expansion
What happened

A marine rabies outbreak among Cape fur seals on South Africa’s western and southern coasts has left at least 176 people bitten and raised fears that the virus could move into other seal and marine mammal species, with Antarctica and remote island breeding grounds among the places scientists are watching.

From sporadic attacks to a confirmed outbreak

Researchers say reports of hyperactive, aggressive seals first emerged in late 2021, but the first infection was only confirmed by laboratory analysis in early 2024. The situation escalated after two high-profile attacks in Cape Town in May 2024, including a prolonged in-water attack on surfers at Muizenberg beach by a juvenile seal that bit surfboards and injured swimmers.

Since then, confirmation has followed for several more rabid seals in the Western Cape, Northern Cape and Namibia, along with a strongly suspected case at Jeffreys Bay in the Eastern Cape. The human toll has been wide-ranging: at least 84 attacks involved people on commercial “swim-with-seals” tourism trips, while beach walkers, swimmers, surfers and kayakers were also bitten. In one case, a victim needed plastic surgery and skin grafts after more than 20 puncture wounds, and researchers say some people have faced lingering insomnia and flashbacks after the attacks.

Why the disease worries scientists

The count of 176 human victims only covers local attacks recorded by the end of 2025, and the researchers say it likely understates the true scale because it excludes probable attacks in Namibia and Angola. They warn that as the virus spreads, seal-human and seal-dog encounters are likely to increase, threatening public health, marine tourism and recreation.

Scientists say the disease is now endemic in Cape fur seals, and in their research article, Dr Tess Gridley and 22 co-researchers describe the detection as the first outbreak of rabies in a free-ranging marine mammal species. The World Organisation for Animal Health has also warned other countries to stay alert for signs of rabies spreading into other marine mammals, calling the sustained transmission in Cape fur seals an “unprecedented development in the ecology of rabies.”

The researchers fear spillover into more vulnerable species, including southern elephant seals, Subantarctic and Antarctic fur seals, crabeater seals and leopard seals. They say the risk could extend as far south as Antarctica, to Marion Island, and even to other continents and remote mid-ocean islands.

Vaccination is limited, so officials are targeting the highest-risk animals

The researchers say broad rabies control through vaccination is currently “neither practical nor feasible,” although it may become necessary if the virus reaches more vulnerable marine species. Dr Lesley van Helden, senior state veterinarian in the Western Cape Department of Agriculture, said mass vaccination of seals is impractical because there is no delivery method that could realistically immunize up to two million wild Cape fur seals.

Van Helden said bait-based vaccination, which has worked for some land species, is not suitable for free-ranging seals because they do not chew prey or scavenge ashore. She said there are roughly 1.5 million to two million Cape fur seals in about 40 breeding colonies stretching nearly 3,000km from Algoa Bay through Namibia into southern Angola.

As a precaution, a programme has been set up to vaccinate vagrant southern elephant seals against rabies, and “strategic vaccination” is also under way for semi-habituated seals around the Cape Town harbours and for healthy vagrant seals that come ashore along the South African coast.

The result adds to a field that only a few years ago was widely dismissed as impractical. Single-cell proteomics aims to profile thousands of proteins in individual cells, giving a more direct readout than RNA alone because not every transcript is translated and protein levels can be shaped by many other factors. Matthias Mann of the Max Planck Institute of Biochemistry says the proteome reflects the cell’s current state.

The technology still comes with steep demands. Isaac Newton’s 1665–66 breakthrough year at Woolsthorpe Manor, where he developed ideas in calculus, gravity and optics, is being revisited today alongside the plagiarism accusations that later followed his rise.

Jed Buchwald and Mordechai Feingold, in *The Winding Trail to Newton’s Principia Mathematica*, use Newton to test psychologist John Hayes’s 1989 “ten-year rule” of creativity, which says major breakthroughs usually follow a decade of practice. Newton stands out as an exception, unlike examples such as Charles Darwin, Albert Einstein and Tim Berners-Lee.

The authors, who have spent decades studying Newton’s manuscripts and published works, focus on the slow evolution of *Philosophiae Naturalis Principia Mathematica*, published in Latin in 1687 and the work in which Newton set out his laws of motion and universal gravitation. They say there is no reliable evidence that he studied maths or science at school. Sent to Trinity College, Cambridge, in 1661 with his guardians expecting him to become a Church of England minister, he enrolled in humanities subjects and appears to have turned seriously to science only in his final undergraduate term, in 1664–65, just before the Great Plague forced him into solitude.

In that isolation, Newton made three linked advances: he laid the foundations of differential and integral calculus, inferred that the same force pulling an apple to the ground also governs the Moon’s orbit, and used prism experiments to show that white light contains the colours of the rainbow. That unusually rapid burst of work is what makes Newton the standout case in the book’s account of genius, credit and dispute.
