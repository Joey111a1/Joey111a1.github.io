UN says US strikes on Iran school that killed over 150 may amount to war crimes

What happened

The United Nations says there are reasonable grounds to believe the United States carried out indiscriminate attacks in Iran, including a strike on a primary school that killed more than 150 people, around 120 of them children, and another attack that killed 22 civilians.

The findings concern two strikes carried out on February 28, during attacks by the United States and Israel on Iran. The Independent International Fact-Finding Mission on Iran said the incidents may constitute the war crime of launching indiscriminate attacks that caused civilian deaths, injuries or damage to civilian objects. Its report was released Thursday and is due to be presented to the UN Human Rights Council in Geneva.

The report is not a judicial ruling. It represents the mission’s assessment that the available information provides “reasonable grounds to believe” the United States was responsible. The US has not acknowledged carrying out either strike.

The Minab school strike

The first attack hit Shajareh Tayyebeh Primary School in Minab, southern Iran. The UN mission said a Tomahawk missile struck the school while US attacks were also targeting an adjacent Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps facility.

A count cited by the mission put the death toll at more than 120 children. Other reporting cited more than 150 people killed overall, including approximately 120 girls and boys. The mission said the school itself was the intended point of impact, rather than an unintended casualty of an errant missile or collateral damage from an attack on the nearby military compound.

The investigators concluded that the United States had failed to do everything feasible to verify that the target was a military objective. They said the circumstances supported the conclusion that the attack was indiscriminate. The report also said the failure went beyond negligence, according to reporting on its findings.

US responsibility has remained publicly contested. In May, US Central Command commander Adm. Brad Cooper told the House Armed Services Committee that the Minab attack “correlated” with a US strike. In June, President Donald Trump said that “nobody did that on purpose” and that “mistakes are made.” The White House and Pentagon did not immediately respond to requests for comment on the UN findings.

The Lamerd sports complex attack

The second strike hit a clearly identifiable sports complex and residential area in Lamerd, southern Iran, killing 22 civilian men and women, according to the UN report. US Central Command has denied responsibility for that attack.

The mission said the strike used Precision Strike Missiles, or PrSMs, which disperse large numbers of tungsten pellets. It concluded that using the weapon in a populated civilian area meant the attack could not be directed at a specific military objective or limited to military effects. The investigators described the choice as reckless in light of the likely extent of civilian death or injury.

The mission therefore applied the same legal characterization to both incidents: launching indiscriminate attacks in violation of the principle of distinction under international humanitarian law. The report urged the relevant authorities to investigate alleged violations independently and hold those responsible to account.

A wider UN assessment of Iran

The report also examined Iran’s response to nationwide protests that began in late 2025 over the rising cost of living. The investigators said Iranian authorities had intensified the use of repressive laws, lethal force and executions, and that security forces killed protesters in all 31 provinces. The report said at least 221 children were reportedly among those killed, while healthcare facilities were attacked and tens of thousands of people were arrested.

It described the repression as involving crimes against humanity, including murder, imprisonment or other severe deprivation of liberty, torture and other inhumane acts. The mission said at least 29 men were executed between March and August 2026 following expedited trials connected to the protests, while more than 70 others remained at imminent risk of execution.

The investigators called on the United States, Israel and Iran to end hostilities and urged UN Human Rights Council members to pursue a permanent peace and accountability for alleged violations by all three parties.

What happened

The number of migrants and refugees reaching Europe by sea has fallen sharply since January, according to the International Organization for Migration (IOM). About 60,000 people arrived by boat in 2026 so far, 39 percent fewer than the roughly 98,000 recorded during the same period last year. Yet at least 2,292 people have died or gone missing attempting the crossing, up from 1,999. Many were traveling on overcrowded, unsuitable boats departing Libya’s coast.

The deadliest routes remain those across the Mediterranean and Atlantic, the IOM said. Central Mediterranean arrivals fell by more than half, while deaths rose from 844 to 996; in Greece, deaths increased from 284 to 417 despite fewer arrivals. Spain recorded declines in both arrivals and overall deaths, but 879 people still died or went missing on routes leading there. IOM Director General Amy Pope said the figures show fewer people are arriving while “people are still dying and going missing in alarming numbers,” and called for international cooperation, stronger protections against trafficking and exploitation, and expanded safe, regular pathways. The agency said the actual death toll is likely higher because some boats disappear without being recorded.

What happened

Sweden’s Prime Minister Ulf Kristersson has resigned after final election results gave four left-leaning parties a three-seat parliamentary majority, clearing the way for Magdalena Andersson to seek a government. The narrow margin and sharp differences over ministerial posts, taxes, nuclear power and immigration leave the next administration uncertain.

Kristersson stepped down after the final count confirmed that his right-leaning bloc had lost Sunday’s parliamentary election. The four-party opposition bloc, led by Andersson’s Social Democrats, won by 50,359 votes and will hold 176 seats in the 349-seat parliament, compared with 173 for the Tidö bloc: Kristersson’s three-party coalition and the Sweden Democrats (SD), who had been expected to join the administration after the vote.

In a resignation letter posted on social media, Kristersson said the tight result and conflicting positions among parties could complicate the formation of a new government. He called for openness among all parliamentary parties so that a prime minister could be accepted and a government could secure support for its budget. He said the parliamentary speaker would lead the next stage and asked to be relieved of his duties so that the process could begin immediately.

Andersson begins the effort to form a government

Andersson, Kristersson’s predecessor and the Social Democrats’ leader, invited the Left Party, Centre Party and Greens to initial coalition discussions. In a victory speech, she said voters had chosen “a new direction” based on unity rather than division, and pledged to pursue that direction.

She identified the climate crisis, schools, healthcare, elderly care and immigration policy as priorities. The Social Democrats are also understood to be seeking changes to the previous government’s immigration, integration and climate policies. However, the party’s own result remained difficult: it stayed Sweden’s largest party but recorded what was described as record-low support, with 28.0% of the vote.

The centre-left bloc’s majority is therefore enough to control the parliamentary arithmetic but not necessarily to produce a straightforward coalition. Minority governments and agreements between parties outside formal coalitions are common in Sweden, and Andersson is expected to seek broad cooperation with one or more parties rather than rely only on a conventional coalition.

Disputes inside the winning bloc

The most immediate obstacle is the relationship between the Centre Party and the Left Party. The Centre has ruled out joining a government that gives cabinet posts to the Left, while the Left has demanded a place in government in return for its cooperation. Andersson has previously ruled out a government including the Left Party but has sought its support in parliament.

The parties also differ over taxation and energy policy. The Left, Social Democrats and Greens support tax increases, while the Centre wants tax cuts. The Greens oppose nuclear energy, and the Left supports a billionaire tax. These disputes could complicate both government formation and the passage of a state budget.

The Centre’s leader, Elisabeth Thand Ringqvist, said her party would act constructively to help Sweden obtain a functioning government, while maintaining that political extremes should not dominate it. The Left’s leader, Nooshi Dadgostar, described the election as a mandate for change and said voters had rejected the Tidö arrangement.

The election also produced an unexpected result for the Sweden Democrats. The anti-immigration party lost support for the first time in a general election since its foundation, falling from Sweden’s second-largest party to third and receiving 17.5% of the vote, three percentage points below its 2022 result. Its influence under the outgoing arrangement had been considerable even though it was not formally part of the previous government.

The speaker will oversee the next stage

Speaker Andreas Norlén is now expected to consult representatives of every party and put forward proposals for a new prime minister. Parliament could vote on a candidate as early as September 29, although negotiations are expected to last weeks. After the 2022 election, forming a government took 37 days.

Andersson is understood to be reaching out to all opposition parties except the Sweden Democrats. The Liberals and Christian Democrats have ruled out cooperation with the left, while Kristersson has indicated that he is prepared to explore options among non-socialist parties if Andersson cannot succeed.

For now, the election has given Andersson’s bloc a numerical advantage but no settled governing arrangement. Her immediate task is to reconcile parties that agree on replacing Kristersson’s government but remain divided over who should serve in it and which policies should define its budget and programme.

Why the SC is telling Congress to do its job on political dynasties
global_south_and_exploitedpolitics_and_powermaterial_harmeconomy_and_businessregional expansion
What happened

The Philippine Supreme Court has declared that Congress has a mandatory constitutional duty to enact an anti-political-dynasty law, putting pressure on lawmakers to implement a provision that has remained without legislation for 39 years and concerns the concentration of political power in elected families.

Article II, Section 26 of the 1987 Constitution states that the state must guarantee equal access to public-service opportunities and prohibit political dynasties as defined by law. Yet Congress has not passed legislation banning them. A 2024 Philippine Center for Investigative Journalism study found that eight in 10 district representatives belonged to political dynasties, while 36 of the 54 party-list groups in the 19th Congress had at least one nominee from a dynasty.

The Supreme Court’s 86-page decision, made public on September 16, followed four petitions seeking action against political dynasties. The Court granted three and denied petition GR No. 277747, which argued that the Commission on Elections should disqualify candidates from dynasties, for lack of merit.

The ruling declared Congress to have a constitutional duty to enact the required law and called on it to do so “with utmost urgency.” Written by Associate Justice Ramon Paul Hernando, it said Congress’s prolonged failure had allowed dynasties to persist and that concentrating political power among a small number of people had “distorted democracy, weakened accountability, and fostered conditions conducive to corruption and political patronage.”

Why the Court says Congress can be compelled

The decision addressed the separation of powers between the executive, legislative and judicial branches. The Supreme Court acknowledged that drafting and passing legislation belongs exclusively to Congress, but said the judiciary can intervene when constitutional violations occur. Under Article VIII, Section 1, Congress is not beyond judicial review.

The Court said it could compel Congress to perform a constitutionally required, non-discretionary duty, but could not dictate the substance of the law. It characterized legislative inaction and delay in this case as grave abuse of discretion.

The justices also rejected Congress’s argument that the anti-dynasty clause was not self-executing and therefore could not support a mandamus petition. The Court abandoned positions expressed in earlier cases, including Biraogo v. Comelec and Guingona Jr. v. House of Congress and Senate, saying those unsigned resolutions had limited doctrinal value. It held that refusing to act would leave the constitutional command ineffective.

The Court offered nonbinding considerations for Congress, including covering relatives up to the fourth civil degree, preventing relatives from holding office simultaneously or succeeding one another, applying the restrictions to national and local posts, and extending them to covered persons in the same administrative region.

Congress has received 101 anti-dynasty bills since 1987, but only two reached plenary. The House has already approved House Bill No. 8389, introduced by Sandro Marcos and House Speaker Bojie Dy, and sent it to the Senate. The Supreme Court said Congress retains control over the bill’s final design; critics argue the measure does not address cross-level or cross-jurisdiction dynasties, immediate succession, or party-list dynasties.

The stars had been observed with the coronagraph at the W. M. Keck Observatory in Hawaii, which blocks much of a star’s light so that fainter nearby objects can be detected.

Elias 2-24 b orbits a star about 450 light-years from Earth and lies roughly 55 times farther from its star than Earth is from the Sun. It was found inside a gap in the surrounding disk, which contains dust, gas, ice and rock. Such gaps can indicate that a forming planet is clearing a path through the material around its star.

The system had already posed a problem for astronomers. ALMA observations in Chile revealed the disk’s gap about 10 years ago, and the European Southern Observatory’s Very Large Telescope later detected a faint point of light inside it. Researchers questioned whether the object was a planet because existing theories suggested that a Jupiter-sized planet should take about 5 million years to form at Jupiter’s distance from the Sun—and even longer at a much greater distance.

Bernardi and colleagues searched the Keck Observatory Archive, a NASA-funded collaboration with the NASA Exoplanet Science Institute at Caltech/IPAC. They found the object in observations from 2018 and 2020. Tracking its movement showed that it behaved like a planet orbiting Elias 2-24 rather than an imaging artifact or a distant background star.

The result is significant because the previous youngest known planets—a four-way tie involving planets around PDS 70 and WISPIT 2—are all more than 5 million years old. Lucas Cieza, a co-author from Chile’s Instituto de Estudios Astrofísicos, said Elias 2-24 b indicates that current planet-formation models are missing important processes.

The planet also illustrates a broader observational gap: thick dust makes infant planets difficult to detect, while most of the roughly 6,000 confirmed exoplanets have been found by the transit method and are billions of years old. NASA’s Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope, with a more advanced coronagraph, could help find more planets during these earliest stages and test the models against additional young systems.

Hidden “immune organ” in the skull may help fight brain cancer

What happened

Researchers at Washington University School of Medicine in St. Louis identified previously unknown, lymph node-like immune structures in the skull bone marrow of mice. The structures appear to act as nearby immune hubs for the brain, responding to brain cancer before more distant lymph nodes receive warning signals. The team also found similar immune cells in human skull bone marrow, suggesting the system may not be limited to mice. Published in *Nature*, the study is described as the first to identify immune hubs of this kind inside bone.

In a mouse model of glioblastoma, disrupting the skull immune hubs caused tumors to grow faster and shortened survival. The researchers then placed a gel containing three immune-boosting proteins beneath the scalp to increase antibody production in the skull bone marrow. This triggered immune activity first in the skull hubs and later in nearby lymph nodes; treated mice rejected tumors more effectively and lived longer than controls. The findings build on earlier work identifying lymphatic vessels in the dura mater and channels connecting the skull, dura, and brain. Senior author Jonathan Kipnis said the discovery could eventually support treatments that influence immune activity in neurological diseases through these nearby hubs.
