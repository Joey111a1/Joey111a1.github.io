While Puerto Ricans protest for reliable access to water, the governor washes her hands

What happened

Puerto Ricans are protesting prolonged, unreliable water service as drought exposes deeper failures in the island’s ageing aqueduct system. In San Juan, residents say water may return for only a few hours every several days, while proposed luxury development has intensified fears that scarce supplies are being prioritized over basic needs.

Dhara Rivera de Jesús keeps at least 30 large water containers at her home in San Juan, filling them whenever service returns. She has gone more than a year without consistent water for bathing, flushing toilets and cooking, a disruption that has affected her work and daily routine while forcing her to spend more time and money buying water and prepared food.

This summer, falling levels in Puerto Rico’s key dams dropped below the thresholds needed to supply roughly 500,000 people. Governor Jenniffer González-Colón declared a state of emergency, urged residents to use water prudently and said in August that “this situation is out of our hands.” She did respond to a request for comment, but residents and political figures have rejected the idea that the crisis is beyond the government’s control.

Drought has exposed an older infrastructure crisis

Residents of San Juan had been facing water insecurity for months before the emergency declaration. In June, an aqueduct rupture left more than 100,000 customers without service. After what was described as the driest July on record and further declines in water levels, San Juan and surrounding areas were placed under rotating 48-hour shut-offs, leaving more than 180,000 homes without public access to potable water.

Alfredo Cuyar, a resident of San Juan’s Santurce neighborhood, said that water had previously been available there about 90% of the time but that “having water is the exception” now. He described the problem as one of politics and management, rather than drought alone.

A 2019 infrastructure report by the American Society of Civil Engineers found that nearly 60% of water treated by Puerto Rico’s Aqueduct and Sewer Authority, known as Prasa, was lost, primarily through inaccurate meters, unauthorized consumption and water-main leaks. Drinking-water stations were opened in some affected municipalities, and the National Guard was activated to deploy water-tank trucks. But residents say rationing, expensive cisterns and eating out are not adequate solutions, especially when 37% of the population lives below the federal poverty level.

Rivera and Cuyar formed the Coalición Exigimos Agua, or We Demand Water Coalition, in October. Its demand is straightforward: reliable water service. Protests organized by the coalition have drawn thousands across the archipelago, while Puerto Rican communities in New York and Philadelphia have protested the water crisis and the proposed Esencia development.

Other groups, including Colectiva Feminista en Construcción, Movimiento Socialista de Trabajadores y Trabajadoras, La Jota and Se Acabaron Las Promesas, have joined the campaign. In August, several groups protested outside hotel events hosted by González-Colón for her birthday and campaign fundraising. On Labor Day, dozens more demonstrated in San Juan for major changes to water infrastructure and Prasa’s management.

Rivera said, “We recognize the drought, but we also recognize that there is an intellectual drought.” Verónica Figueroa Huertas of Colectiva Feminista en Construcción said water should not be treated as a luxury or privilege but as something guaranteed by the government.

Residents challenge the government’s priorities

The protests have called for immediate action on leaks and water waste, as well as a halt to new construction permits in areas with fragile water infrastructure. In August, Puerto Rico’s resident commissioner, Pablo José Hernández Rivera, asked Prasa’s president for information about federal funds available to the utility and the amount of water being lost.

Cuyar said the administration had done nothing to resolve the leaks, whether because it lacked crews or failed to assign them. Representatives Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez and Nydia Velázquez likewise said the crisis resulted from “federal neglect and mismanagement,” not from a single drought or infrastructure failure, and rejected González-Colón’s characterization that the situation was out of her hands.

Activists also argue that the government could require rainwater-catchment systems, which they say could provide millions of gallons annually. Rivera said officials were trying to normalize conditions that were not normal, adding that not everyone could afford a cistern.

The dispute has sharpened around Esencia, a planned 2,000-acre mega-resort and residential complex in Cabo Rojo, on Puerto Rico’s south-western tip. The development is expected to consume more than a million gallons of water, while nearby residents have experienced intermittent service for years. At a recent local hearing, developers said they were still exploring water sources.

Esencia developer Roberto Ruiz Vargas said the project would be fully self-sufficient, with on-site rainwater collection, wastewater reuse and underground sources. He said it would be funded independently, place no demand on public water infrastructure and have surplus capacity that could support the surrounding community.

Local residents and economists remain concerned. Héctor Tavárez Vargas, president of the Puerto Rico Association of Economists, warned that the project’s scale and luxury orientation could increase housing pressure, displacement risks and an enclave economy with limited ties to local activity. Beatriz Llenín Figueroa of the Coalición Defiende a Cabo Rojo said the community either ran out of water or faced low pressure on long weekends.

For Rivera, the San Juan shortages and the fight over Esencia reflect the same failure: insufficient government response to threats from climate change, infrastructure breakdown and displacement. The coalition’s central demand remains unchanged—water that residents can reliably use for the basic functions of daily life.

Iran’s Houthi allies capture strategic island on vital oil shipping route
global_front_pageglobal_south_and_exploitedwar_and_conflictpolitics_and_powereconomy_and_businesspublic_healthmaterial_harmanchor
What happened

Iran-aligned Houthi forces have captured Perim, a strategic island in Yemen’s Bab el-Mandeb Strait, after Saudi-backed government forces withdrew. The seizure expands Houthi control over a vital route between the Red Sea and the Gulf of Aden, while Saudi oil exports already face pressure from disruption around the Strait of Hormuz.

Four Yemeni government sources told Reuters that the Houthis reached Perim on Friday. Two sources from the Saudi-backed, internationally recognised government said its forces had pulled out of the island and that the Houthis had also captured Dhubab, the mainland Red Sea coastal town facing Perim.

The takeover followed a rapid Houthi advance along Yemen’s western coast. The group captured the port city of Mocha on Thursday and also took the Red Sea islands of Hanish, according to the reported account. Saudi Arabia, which supports the Yemeni government, carried out airstrikes on Houthi positions in Mocha on Friday.

Why Perim matters to shipping

Perim, also known as Mayun, is a volcanic island near the narrowest part of the Bab el-Mandeb and divides the waterway in two. The strait links Asia to Europe through the Red Sea and the Suez Canal, with roughly 10% to 12% of global maritime trade typically passing through it.

The Houthi advance gives the group a stronger position from which to disrupt shipping. A Guardian analysis said full control could allow the Houthis to restrict passage or charge tolls, although the immediate reports described the capture of the island rather than complete control of all shipping through the strait.

The route has become especially important to Saudi Arabia because the kingdom has increasingly relied on the Red Sea and Bab el-Mandeb to export crude after Iran effectively closed the Strait of Hormuz. Control of both waterways by Iran and its allies would put two major regional energy corridors under pressure. Oil prices rose above $100 a barrel as fighting in Yemen escalated.

Saudi crude supply fell by 2.3 million barrels per day in August to 6 million bpd, the lowest level in more than three decades, according to the International Energy Agency. The reported fall was partly attributed to attacks on ships transiting Bab el-Mandeb by groups linked to the Houthis. Preliminary tracking data also showed seven vessels passing through Hormuz on Thursday, down from 11 the previous day and below the 10-day average of 10.

Houthi military spokesman Yahya Saree said maritime navigation remained safe for all shipping except Saudi vessels covered by an earlier ban. He said the group would continue its policy of responding to blockade with blockade and escalation with escalation until what he called the aggression against Yemen stopped and the blockade on the Yemeni people was lifted.

Yemen’s government faces a wider setback

Yemeni government forces said they would try to retake areas seized during the Houthi offensive and would deploy weapons and aircraft against Houthi movements in strategic areas. A military spokesman urged civilians to avoid the road between Taiz and Mocha and to stay away from Houthi equipment.

The advance has also intensified scrutiny of divisions among forces opposing the Houthis. Amr al-Bidh, the foreign affairs representative of the separatist Southern Transitional Council, said Saudi Arabia had alienated southern forces that were needed to resist the Houthis. He argued that the group’s presence near the strait could let it control the waterway without using sophisticated weapons there and could provide new income from ports along Yemen’s Red Sea coast.

The Guardian reported that political divisions in areas outside Houthi control had raised concern in Western capitals that Aden, the government’s temporary capital, could eventually come under threat. The renewed fighting has already caused substantial humanitarian harm: one report attributed more than 500 deaths and the displacement of about 46,000 people to the offensive, while another cited more than 1,000 people killed or wounded and tens of thousands displaced.

Iran welcomed the Houthi advances in an official statement on Friday. Reuters reporting, citing Yemeni government, Iranian and regional sources, said the offensive had received direct guidance from Iran’s Revolutionary Guards. Two Iranian sources also claimed that Tehran had urged the Houthis to intensify attacks on Saudi Arabia and promised more funding, weapons and senior officers. Iran describes the Houthis as allies but publicly denies directing them militarily and says they are not a proxy.

Saudi Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman reportedly called President Donald Trump twice on Thursday to request US strikes against the Houthis. Trump rejected the request, according to Axios reporting cited in the accounts, while US officials said the administration did not intend to take direct military action against the group at that time. British diplomatic sources likewise did not expect Trump to authorise bombing of Houthi positions.

Pressure on Saudi Arabia’s alternative export route

Saudi Arabia has used Aramco’s East-West pipeline to move crude from the kingdom’s eastern coast to the Red Sea, offering an alternative route when Hormuz is disrupted. Satellite imagery verified by Reuters showed smoke near the pipeline on Thursday, but Saudi Arabia did not confirm that an incident had occurred. The Saudi government media office and Aramco did not immediately respond to requests for comment.

The shipping crisis is unfolding alongside attacks and counterattacks in the wider conflict between Iran and the United States. Iran confirmed on Friday that representatives from Iran, Iraq and the Gulf Arab states would meet in Oman on Monday to resume discussions about safe commercial shipping routes through Hormuz. The Financial Times first reported the planned meeting.

Yemeni government forces have indicated that they will attempt to reverse the Houthi gains. Whether they can retake Perim and the surrounding coastal positions will determine whether the capture remains a battlefield reversal or becomes a lasting shift in control of a major shipping chokepoint.

What happened

Researchers from the University of Sydney, the Baird Institute and Royal Prince Alfred Hospital have found evidence that adult human hearts produce new muscle cells after a heart attack. The response is too weak to replace all destroyed tissue, but it could guide future treatments for heart failure.

The finding, published in *Circulation Research*, challenges the long-held assumption that heart muscle lost during an attack cannot regenerate. First author Dr. Robert Hume said the heart remains scarred after an attack but still produces new muscle cells, opening possibilities for therapies that might strengthen this natural repair process.

Scientists had previously observed increased mitosis—the division and reproduction of cells—in the heart muscles of mice after a heart attack. The new research is described as the first demonstration of the same phenomenon in humans. However, the researchers stressed that the heart’s regenerative response is not currently strong enough to prevent the damaging effects of an attack or restore all the muscle that has been lost.

A key part of the work was a method for collecting living heart tissue from consenting patients undergoing bypass surgery at Royal Prince Alfred Hospital in Sydney. Researchers obtained samples from both diseased and non-diseased areas of the heart. The technique was developed by Professor Paul Bannon and Professor Sean Lal, who hold roles at the University of Sydney, the hospital and the Baird Institute.

Because the samples came from living human hearts, the researchers said they could study heart biology in a laboratory model that more closely reflects what happens in patients. The team has already identified several proteins in these samples that had previously been linked to heart regeneration in mice. Those proteins may provide clues about how to encourage damaged human hearts to produce more muscle cells.

The potential stakes are substantial. A heart attack can destroy as many as one third of the cells in the human heart, and some survivors later develop heart failure, in which the heart cannot pump enough blood to meet the body’s needs. The evidence describes heart transplantation as the current cure for heart failure, but notes that donor organs are available to only a small fraction of patients: Australia has about 144,000 people living with heart failure and performs roughly 115 heart transplants each year.

Professor Lal said the ultimate goal is to use the discovery to create new heart cells that could reverse heart failure. Such regenerative treatments remain a future aim; the immediate result is evidence that the adult human heart has at least some natural capacity to rebuild muscle after an attack.
