The Babies Left Behind in America

What happened

Immigration arrests and deportations are leaving children in the United States without a parent, sometimes with unprepared or temporary caregivers. In Mobile, Alabama, 2-year-old Eber has spent nearly a year with Estela Garcia after ICE deported his parents to Venezuela, while his family struggles to secure his return.

Each morning, around 8 a.m., Eber wakes Estela in the bed they share. She changes his diaper and dresses him as he coos, “Mama, mama.” Estela, 50, has three sons of her own, but Eber is not one of them. She took him in after his mother, Marlyn, was detained and deported, intending to care for him temporarily and reunite him with his parents.

Instead, Eber remains in Estela’s home. His mother had pleaded to be deported with her children first, but was sent to Venezuela in February. Eber could not travel because he needed surgery for a lung cyst; Estela spent three days and nights beside him in the hospital. Venezuelan officials said they would add her name to a list of parents seeking the return of their children, but gave no timeline.

The disruption has transformed Estela and her husband Miguel’s four-acre property from a retirement sanctuary into a makeshift day care. The couple moved to Mobile 20 years ago to raise their sons. Miguel, a remodeling contractor, built a workshop, chicken coop and houses for two of their children. Now Estela says the strain has caused her to lose weight, while their bank account has fallen to $27. Her family asks what she has done.

How Marlyn’s children were separated

Marlyn was pulled over near Mobile in November while on a grocery trip. She had a work permit, had attended her ICE check-ins and had a pending asylum application, according to her account. The officer turned her over to ICE. She spent about an hour in the Mobile County Metro Jail, then 10 days in an ICE detention center in Mississippi before being transferred to a 1,000-bed facility in Louisiana.

Her children initially moved through several improvised arrangements. Eber, who had just stopped breastfeeding, was placed with an undocumented woman in a rusty trailer without heat; the caregiver put socks on his hands to keep him warm and was too afraid of ICE to take him to a doctor when he became sick. His 7-year-old brother, Isaac, stayed with a caregiver who worked nights and sometimes could not get him to school.

Marlyn sent ICE three messages a day asking to be deported with Eber and Isaac. She said officials repeatedly told her they were working on it. After three months in detention, she was deported alone. Eber remained with Estela. Isaac was later taken to a foster home in Texas, where he was to await an immigration proceeding and seek “voluntary departure” to Venezuela.

The case also placed Estela under investigation. County officials examined whether she was trafficking the children, even though she had agreed to help after friends and former co-workers sought a safe caregiver. The investigation eventually closed after a social worker intervened.

A broader separation crisis

The account describes Eber’s case as part of a wider pattern linked to the second Trump administration’s drive to increase deportations. A Brookings Institution analysis estimated that about 205,000 children had been affected by the loss of at least one parent as of April, with 71 percent identified as U.S. citizens. Brookings researchers estimated that more than 35,000 children had been left without either parent.

A New York Times review of data obtained from the Deportation Data Project found that ICE arrested 49,000 people in July, described as a record for the agency, and that most had no criminal record. The cases included people who had entered through parole programs or sought asylum while pursuing permanent legal status. A Department of Homeland Security spokesperson called the data inaccurate but did not provide corrected figures, and said a pending legal-status application did not shield immigrants from arrest.

The source account also reports that DHS revised its family-separation policy in July 2025. Earlier guidance required officers to reunite detained parents with their children before deportation or confirm an official caregiver. The revised language included the qualification “when operationally feasible,” which appeared to permit faster deportations even when children’s arrangements were unresolved.

DHS disputed the broader characterization, saying the agency “does not separate families or deport U.S. citizens.” The spokesperson said that, absent abuse or neglect, parents can choose to take their children with them. The account also notes that DHS did not comment on Marlyn’s individual case.

The consequences extend beyond children placed in safe homes. A related case described 2-year-old Orlin Hernandez Reyes being left with a caregiver known to be dangerous after his mother was detained and deported to Honduras. Orlin was later found dead with broken bones and signs of sexual abuse. His caregiver was charged with murder and pleaded not guilty. Then-ICE Director Todd Lyons blamed the mother.

Legal aid workers described a system in which immigration and family law overlap, often requiring families to find two lawyers while assistance remains scarce. The Young Center assigned six people to immigration-related family separations and had worked on more than 150 cases, with a growing waitlist. The organization reported cases involving children couch-surfing, caregivers becoming homeless and infants separated from breastfeeding mothers.

The separations can become harder to reverse over time. Family courts may defer to a child’s existing living arrangement, even when the parent abroad has not been accused of abuse or neglect. Sharon Balmer Cartagena, a family lawyer, said some parents lose custody simply because deportation makes them unavailable. She has helped arrange documents and transportation for children traveling to deported parents, but warned caregivers to plan for a primary caregiver, a backup and a backup for the backup.

For Estela, the immediate question remains whether Eber can return to Marlyn. During a video call, Estela spent 10 minutes encouraging the toddler to greet his mother or blow her a kiss. Marlyn said little.

What happened

South Africa’s cardiovascular disease burden is being highlighted on World Heart Day, 29 September. The Heart and Stroke Foundation South Africa says about 225 South Africans die each day from heart disease or stroke. Together, the conditions account for almost one in six deaths, with more than half of cardiovascular deaths occurring before age 65. Many of those who die are breadwinners, parents or caregivers in midlife. The World Heart Federation’s campaign is urging people to recognise warning signs and demand stronger action.

High blood pressure is identified as a major driver, contributing to one in two strokes and two in five heart attacks. An analysis of screening campaigns conducted across South Africa from 2017 to 2021, involving more than 11,000 people, found hypertension in nearly 30% of participants. Only 54% of those with hypertension knew they had it, 47% were receiving treatment and about a quarter had controlled blood pressure; awareness was just 24% among adults under 40. Public health researchers are calling for routine checks in clinics, workplaces and pharmacies, integrated care for conditions including hypertension, diabetes and HIV, reliable medicine supplies, and policies that make prevention and treatment affordable.

The incident comes amid an increase in reports of sexual violence involving students. Cárcamo said boys and men can be taught that proving masculinity involves controlling others, while Silva described a culture in which another person’s body becomes an object and empathy for suffering is lost.

Silva said those dynamics could be especially difficult to confront in institutions shaped by privilege, power and hierarchy, but stressed that the problem was not confined to one school. She said young people were absorbing values centered on imposing power and that prevention needed to address isolation and the loss of community as well as sex education.

President Keiko Fujimori has demanded a “rigorous and swift investigation” and said anyone found guilty should face the full weight of the law. The investigation and the juvenile proceedings will determine responsibility for the alleged assault, while the family’s planned actions could also examine whether adults or institutions failed to intervene or report it.

Old hearts age backwards: transplanted organs adjust to host's biological age

What happened

Hearts transplanted between animals and people with large age gaps appear to shift toward the recipient’s biological age, suggesting that older donor organs might be usable more often and potentially expand a scarce supply for patients waiting for transplants.

Jesse Poganik of Harvard University and colleagues studied how transplanted hearts change their biological age—the cellular damage accumulated over time—in mice and human recipients. In the mouse experiments, extra hearts from three-month-old, one-year-old and 1.5–1.67-year-old animals were grafted into mice from all three age groups. After four to six months, the researchers measured DNA methylation across about 320,000 genetic regions, looking for patterns associated with ageing.

The biological ages of the grafted hearts moved closer to those of their hosts. Young hearts in older mice showed accelerated ageing, while older hearts in younger mice appeared biologically rejuvenated. The result is consistent with earlier parabiosis research, in which mice shared a circulatory system and the blood of younger animals was linked to longer life and improved memory and learning in older mice.

The team then examined biopsy samples from 11 historical heart transplants at Brigham and Women’s Hospital in Boston. The donor–recipient age gaps ranged from eight to 24 years for older hearts transplanted into younger people, and from 38 to 50 years for younger hearts transplanted into older people. Poganik said the human samples showed the same age-shifting effect found in mice.

Electronic medical records from hundreds of recipients were also assessed one year after transplantation. Heart rate, posterior-wall thickness and exercise capacity were associated with the recipient’s age more than the donor’s, although only some functional measures followed the biological-age pattern. Poganik said some effects may be irreversible.

The findings, posted on bioRxiv and not yet peer reviewed, could support considering more older donors.
