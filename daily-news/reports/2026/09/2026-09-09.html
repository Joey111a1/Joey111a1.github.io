Israeli journalist says Palestinians face threat of expulsion from the river to the sea

What happened

Amira Hass has warned that Palestinians from the Jordan River to the Mediterranean Sea face a growing threat of forced expulsion, saying Israel now has the political and military machinery to push mass displacement further.

A warning that displacement has been normalised

The veteran Israeli journalist said the danger is not just rhetorical. Speaking to Middle East Eye, Hass said Israel now has an increasingly powerful ideological, political, economic and military infrastructure capable of facilitating mass displacement. “It’s not a prophecy. It’s a warning. It’s expressing my fear,” she said.

Hass, whose parents survived the Holocaust, has lived in the Gaza Strip and the occupied West Bank since 1993 and has reported on Israel’s occupation of Palestinian territory for Haaretz for more than three decades. Earlier this month, she published a column arguing that Israel was seeking to “erase” Palestinians rather than debate a diplomatic solution for a Palestinian state.

She said the pressure was aimed at Palestinians in Gaza, the West Bank and among Palestinian citizens of Israel. “Israel makes life unbearable everywhere now,” she said. “It’s not temporary. It’s a policy in Gaza, it’s a policy in the West Bank, and it’s a policy in Israel proper.”

Gaza, the West Bank and inside Israel

Hass said religious-nationalist and far-right forces had become Israel’s strongest political actors, giving them increasing influence over government policy and state bureaucracy. “They are the strongest, and they have been dictating the track,” she said.

She argued that the pressure to leave should be understood as part of a broader policy that has become routine across Israel’s political spectrum. “It has been completely normalised,” she said. “We know this has been a process. It didn’t start with this government or the previous one. It has been built up over years.”

Her warning comes against a backdrop of open calls by Israeli officials for Palestinians to be expelled from Gaza over the past three years. Last week, Israeli Defense Minister Israel Katz said Israel was awaiting US approval to expel Palestinians from the Strip. He said there was “no real solution for Gaza in the end without this migration”, and that the government was “organised and prepared to get them out by sea, by air, by every way possible”.

Fear, fragmentation and pressure to leave

In the occupied West Bank, Hass described “unprecedented despair and fear and helplessness,” especially in Palestinian villages facing repeated settler attacks. She said even small groups of settlers can paralyse whole communities, while the presence or actions of Israeli soldiers can stop Palestinians from helping one another.

She described visiting a Palestinian family whose home had been attacked months earlier and was now surrounded by fencing and metal doors, leaving the family unable to reach trees only metres away. She also cited a Palestinian vineyard in Beit Ummar, where she said settlers uprooted all 1,000 trees. Movement restrictions and checkpoints, she said, block access to agricultural land and grazing areas, while home demolitions further disrupt daily life. She added that settler influence over Israel’s Civil Administration has grown, linking demolitions and other restrictions to wider government policy.

Hass said Palestinians are being left isolated. “There is no real Palestinian leadership that can face it, or that can take decisions, or that can do anything,” she said. “People are left very alone and lonely in this.”

She said the current moment is the result of a much longer process in which Palestinians have been politically and geographically fragmented while Israel kept control over most of the territory. She described the Oslo Accords process as having produced Palestinian “Bantustans,” and said Israel previously accepted a compromise between wanting Palestinians to disappear and recognising that their removal was not then considered possible.

Now, she said, some forces inside Israel believe the time has come to go further. “Now we have the forces who think that this is a time, this is the right time also to achieve another stage of taking more land and pushing more Palestinians out, out of the borders and out of the world. Out of existence.”

Hass said the threat also extends to Palestinian citizens of Israel, arguing that far-right forces increasingly see their presence as a problem. Making life harder, she said, can push those with the means to leave. “There is a very tiny and thin difference… between expulsion and emigration,” she said. “When people feel that they cannot stand it any longer, they emigrate. They choose to emigrate, those who can.”

She also said the state is using similar tactics across Gaza, the West Bank and inside Israel, including economic pressure and the use or toleration of armed groups that undermine Palestinians’ sense of safety. She condemned the international community for not responding decisively, saying governments have repeatedly found new reasons to delay meaningful action.

Heavy breathing: Mpumalanga community speaks out as study links Sasol pollution to 1,000 deaths
global_front_pageglobal_south_and_exploitedpublic_healthpolitics_and_powereconomy_and_businessmaterial_harmregional expansion
What happened

A new assessment of Sasol’s Secunda industrial complex has put pollution-linked health damage at the center of debate in Mpumalanga, as residents describe worsening breathing problems and the company questions the study’s conclusions.

Dineo Hoffman, who has lived in Embalenhle for more than 28 years, said she grew up under the shadow of Sasol Secunda and, like many people around her, once hoped to work for the petrochemical giant. Instead, she said, a learnership ended when she failed a medical screening known as the Rehabilitation and Functional Assessment, or RFA, after being told her lungs were not okay.

Hoffman said the test made her realise she had been sick for a while without knowing it. She said her breathing has become more strained over time and that she now struggles to walk up stairs without running out of breath. Her story is one of the lived experiences behind what the report describes as a daily reality for many people in fence-line communities.

What residents say life near Secunda has been like

Another resident, Sharon Mbonani, said the health worries in Secunda have shaped her family life. She said her first child died a month after birth in 2017, although that death was not officially linked to air pollution. When her second child later showed health problems, she said she took him to his father’s family in Springs for specialist care.

Mbonani said a doctor told them the child was living in a place with high emissions, and examinations showed a throat infection that had multiplied four times. She said the child is now almost seven and thriving, but that he has been raised away from her so his health can come first.

The report frames these accounts as part of a wider burden on people living close to the Secunda industrial complex. It says children and infants are hit especially hard because their lungs are still developing and they breathe more air relative to their body weight than adults.

What Sasol’s pollution is doing

The Centre for Research on Energy and Clean Air, or Crea, says pollution from Sasol’s Secunda plant can be linked to roughly 1,000 premature deaths and 260 new cases of childhood asthma each year, creating an annual social cost of R19-billion. The report, released on Monday, 7 September, says the health burden does not stop at asthma.

In 2024 alone, the report attributes 1,000 premature deaths to exposure to particulate matter and nitrogen dioxide from the industrial complex. It also says plant emissions caused 1,300 children to live with active asthma, 1,100 pre-term births, 820 low-birth-weight infants, 2,030 asthma-related emergency room visits, and 2,600 years lived with disability linked to chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, Type-2 diabetes and strokes.

The harm begins in the womb and continues through childhood. Additionally, the health and economic costs are borne largely by society rather than by the polluter. Sasol’s Secunda plant, described in the report as the world’s largest coal-based synthetic fuels and chemicals complex, supplies around 30% of South Africa’s liquid fuel demand.

Sasol disputes the framing as regulators and activists face pressure

Sasol told Daily Maverick that it was reviewing the methodology and conclusions of the Crea report, saying it would be premature to comment before completing a careful review and that it could not endorse the figures presented. The company also cautioned against attributing health outcomes to a single source, saying air quality in the Highveld Priority Area is influenced by multiple industrial, domestic, transport and regional sources.

On compliance, Sasol defended its alternative sulphur dioxide limits, saying its application did not seek general leniency but aimed to regulate emissions on a load basis rather than a concentration basis. The company said the approach, backed by an independent health risk assessment, is expected to deliver greater overall reductions in sulphur dioxide load than a concentration-based limit alone.

Sasol also pointed to long-term efforts to cut pollution, saying it has invested more than R9.5-billion since 2015 in emission-abatement measures. It said it expects a 30% reduction in sulphur dioxide emissions from the Secunda steam plants by 2030, and listed boiler turndowns, low-NOx burners, particulate matter retrofits and volatile organic compound abatement projects. It has also submitted an Emission Reduction and Management Plan under Highveld Priority Area regulations and is awaiting feedback from licensing authorities.

The report places those claims against South Africa’s emissions rules, which it says are already lenient. It says the government doubled the allowable sulphur dioxide limit in 2020 for older coal combustion installations from 500 mg/Nm³ to 1,000 mg/Nm³, and Secunda’s West and East stacks were later allowed to run under daily limits of 1,700 mg/Nm³ and 1,400 mg/Nm³ through March 2030.

Crea and civil society stakeholders say the response should include health-based reviews of exemption licences, legally binding annual pollution cuts, independent monitoring, stronger national standards and community-centred enforcement. The report also points to the 2022 High Court ruling in the “deadly air” case, which found that failure to enforce air quality standards in the Highveld Priority Area violated residents’ constitutional rights.

The least free place on earth: Occupied Ukraine
global_front_pageglobal_south_and_exploitedpolitics_and_powerwar_and_conflictpublic_healthclimate_disastermajor supplement
What happened

Russia-occupied eastern and southern Ukraine have been named the world’s least free territories for a second year, as residents face forced citizenship, secret detention, school indoctrination and property seizures that are reshaping daily life and raising fears over how much Ukrainian identity will survive.

Why occupied Ukraine tops the list

Freedom House placed the occupied areas above South Sudan, North Korea and the Gaza Strip, saying the system there is built on surveillance, repression, indoctrination and state theft. The democracy tracker says inhabitants are denied political rights, freedom of expression and protection from secret detention, deportation and other abuses.

The group says people in these territories live under repressive Russian legislation, including forcibly imposed citizenship and the use of antiterrorism laws against political dissidents and religious minority groups. Moscow, however, insists the areas are not occupied at all and says they are simply part of Russia, meaning they fall under Russian law.

Danielle Bell, who heads the U.N. Human Rights Monitoring Mission to Ukraine, called the occupation “one of the most well-documented systems of repression, oppression and human rights violations” because so much of it is visible on the public record. She said it amounts to “a systemic approach to restricting people’s rights and fundamental freedoms.” Since 2022, the mission has documented at least 16 Russian laws and decrees limiting the rights of Ukrainian citizens in these territories.

Forced papers, detention and exile

One of the clearest tools of control is passportization: occupation authorities classify Ukrainians in the seized territories as foreigners and compel them to obtain Russian citizenship if they want access to health care, social services, work or property ownership. Freedom House says this has helped entrench Russian control, while also changing the region’s demographics.

Liudmila Chub described how that pressure worked in practice. She said her employer in occupied Melitopol told her in 2023 that she had to get a Russian passport to keep working, or he would be fined. After she went to the passport office and recorded an angry video about being forced to take what she called a “chicken passport,” security guards handed her to occupation police. She said she then spent eight months in detention in Kherson, was later made to read a statement on camera saying she did not want to live in Russia, and was finally transported under armed guard to the Russian border with Georgia.

Chub said she still considered herself lucky because other detainees she met had obtained Russian citizenship only to be accused of spying or treason, offenses that can carry huge sentences. HRMMU says it has documented more than 400 detentions in occupied territories on treason- and espionage-related charges, though it says incommunicado detention makes the true number impossible to track.

That secrecy is part of the same system. Serhii Gryshchenkov, a 59-year-old IT specialist from occupied Sevastopol in Crimea, was taken from his home by Russian security forces in May 2025, according to his daughter Darina. She said the family could not find out where he was for months, and that he was held incommunicado and tortured for a year before being charged with treason in June 2026. She said the family still does not know the details of the case and has heard of other prisoners who remained secretly held for years.

Homes, schools and the shrinking space for Ukrainian life

The pressure extends beyond arrests. HRMMU says more than 60,000 private properties in occupied Ukraine had been identified as “ownerless” as of July this year, and at least 6,500 had been expropriated, often later handed to veterans of Russia’s war or sold to newcomers from Russia on preferential terms. The occupation authorities can seize empty property unless it has been re-registered in person by an owner who must also be a Russian citizen.

People trying to return home have run into “filtration” checks, arrests and entry bans at Moscow’s Sheremetevo airport, which the story describes as the only possible entry point into occupied territories because land borders are closed. One woman identified only as Olena said she fled occupied Donetsk in 2014, kept paying utility bills on her and her parents’ apartments for 12 years, and later discovered she had been publicly blacklisted as an “enemy.” She said an acquaintance was given a 50-year entry ban when trying to get back home.

Education is also being remade. Schools teach a Russian version of history and promote Russian identity and values, while “Ukrainian nationalism” — including sympathy for Ukraine or the use of the Ukrainian language — is punished as “extremist.” Parents who refuse to enroll children in these schools, or who choose online Ukrainian education, are fined and threatened with losing parental rights. The story says some schools can feed students directly into the Russian armed forces once they are old enough to be drafted.

Andrii Dikhtiarenko, a journalist from occupied eastern Ukraine, said the result is the erosion of visible Ukrainian identity and dissent. Even so, he said many residents still hide their Ukrainian passports under floorboards or in gardens, treating them as “a little ticket to freedom.”

What happened

The Biko family is asking the Gqeberha High Court to stop a reopened inquest into Steve Biko’s 1977 death, arguing the matter is not ready to proceed and needs more time for service, consultation and translation work before the hearing goes ahead.

The application, now set for Wednesday after first being expected on Tuesday, will be argued by Advocate Dali Mpofu SC. Ngqiqo Sakhela, who represents the family at the inquest, said the delay was needed so the legal team could place proof of service before the court and satisfy it that everyone had been served. The court papers list Nontsikelelo “Ntsiki” Biko and their son, Nkosinathi Biko, as the applicants, with Judge Isaac Madondo, National Director of Public Prosecutions Advocate Andy Mothibi, Justice Minister Mmamoloko Kubayi, and former apartheid security officers Daniel Petrus Siebert and Jacobus Johannes Oosthuysen Benecke among the respondents.

Why the family says the inquest should pause

At the outset of the reopened proceedings, the family said it believed the matter was not ready to proceed. On 25 August, Sakhela asked for a postponement, pointing to a “voluminous” record of thousands of pages that still needed to be translated from Afrikaans into English. The legal team also said it needed enough time to consult with Biko’s widow and prepare an affidavit.

Sakhela said that an affidavit had been filed and placed before the court, but that the court still had to be satisfied that service had been properly completed before hearing the matter. He said the postponement was initially set down for Tuesday and was pushed back to Wednesday for that reason.

The judge refused, and the inquest kept moving

Judge Madondo, who is presiding over the reopened inquest, dismissed the postponement bid on 26 August. Among the reasons given was the advanced age of the two surviving persons of interest in Biko’s death and the concern that further delay could allow memories to fade.

Siebert is 80 and Beneke is 76. Both have said they cannot remember certain events surrounding Biko’s death. Their evidence has also been marked by discrepancies between what they now recall, what they told the Truth and Reconciliation Commission in 1997, and what was said at the original inquest in November 1977. After the dismissal, Sakhela said the family would approach the Judge President of KwaZulu-Natal and the Acting Judge President of the Eastern Cape in an urgent attempt to halt the proceedings.

Nkosinathi Biko has argued that the reopened inquest must be handled carefully. He said there was “one shot” at dealing with it, and that if the choice was between scheduling and rigour, the family wanted it done correctly. He also said the inquest could be a possible basis for a prosecution.

Testimony continued in another courtroom

While the legal fight was unfolding in one courtroom, the inquest itself continued in another, with Beneke testifying virtually on Tuesday alongside Advocate Marius Van Zyl SC, who also represents Siebert. Beneke said his encounter with Biko on 6 September 1977 was “purely coincidental” and that he had had no previous involvement in Biko’s investigation, interrogation or activities.

He told the court he was working at the State Security Branch offices in the Sanlam Building, now the Steve Biko Precinct, when he heard a commotion from a nearby office. He said he entered out of curiosity and found security branch members including Harold Snyman, Siebert, Rubin Marx, Gideon Johannes Nieuwoudt and Biko. Beneke described a scuffle in which Biko appeared to push a chair toward Siebert, after which he intervened, struck Biko with his shoulder and saw other officers join in. He said he later saw Nieuwoudt strike Biko with a hosepipe, and recalled Biko sitting on the floor with his back against a wall before being handcuffed. Beneke said he later noticed Biko speaking unclearly, loosened his handcuffs and told Nieuwoudt to report the situation to Goosen, after which he was told a doctor would examine Biko. He denied intending to assault or kill him.

Evidence leader Devendran Govender also confronted Siebert with inconsistencies in his testimony, including differing accounts of the chair Biko allegedly pushed or threw and where officers realised Biko was wearing only underwear during his transfer to Pretoria North Prison on 11 September 1977. Govender put Dr Shakeera Holland’s forensic evidence alongside those accounts. Holland, who reviewed the original autopsy conducted on 13 September 1977, testified that Biko had 10 injuries consistent with a hosepipe and that the wounds appeared to have been inflicted at different times, not all during one incident. Siebert rejected the suggestion that the injuries pointed to violence during interrogation or that he and other officers assaulted Biko so severely that it caused his death.

The inquest is expected to continue on Wednesday.

What happened

A Houthi attack on southwestern Saudi Arabia wounded dozens, set off fires at energy facilities and pushed Brent crude higher, while Riyadh signaled retaliation and the size and form of its response remained unclear.

The strike came early Tuesday and, according to Saudi-led coalition spokesman Major General Turki Al-Maliki, hit “civilian and economic sites” in Abha, Khamis Mushait, Jazan and Najran. Markets reacted quickly

Oil prices rose more than 1% after the assault, with Brent crude trading close to $99 per barrel. The attack also fit into a conflict that had already been heating up again. The sky over Kherson: Fear from above
global_south_and_exploitedpolitics_and_powerwar_and_conflictpublic_healthmaterial_harmhumanitarian expansion
What happened

In Kherson, drone threats are reshaping daily life, forcing residents, volunteers and doctors to plan around attacks from the sky while shelters, netting and overnight underground stays become part of routine, and the city’s main road and humanitarian work grow harder to use.

Roads, shelters and the new routine

For people who remain in Kherson and surrounding communities, simply looking up has become part of the day. The sight or sound of a drone can mean taking cover, changing course or abandoning a trip altogether. On roads, anti-drone netting stretches above passing vehicles, residents warn one another of sightings, and basements and underground shelters are increasingly used not just for safety but for sleeping and gathering.

The danger is especially visible on the main road linking Mykolaiv and Kherson, a vital route for civilians and aid groups that locals call the “road of death.” Destroyed vehicles line parts of the highway, and anti-drone netting now covers sections of the route to protect against low-flying drones. Even so, insecurity has at times forced humanitarian workers to suspend or restrict movement on both the highway and alternative routes into Kherson, making access to people in need more difficult.

Volunteers, medics and the cost of staying

Vlad Voronov holds a drone detector that has become essential to his work in Kherson. He leads Ukraine Unity Bridge, whose volunteers evacuate civilians, provide transport and deliver humanitarian aid to some of the most dangerous areas of the city and nearby communities. Some detectors can intercept a drone’s video feed, giving him and his colleagues a chance to predict its path and get under cover.

But the device does not remove the danger. Voronov said his team works in conditions “that most people find difficult to imagine,” and he pointed to the pressure on the people they support. Over four drone attacks, he sustained multiple shrapnel injuries that required surgery to remove fragments from his back, with pieces still lodged in his elbow. The organization has also lost an evacuation vehicle in an attack in Kherson’s Ostriv, or “Island,” neighborhood, close to the frontline; the team and the civilians they were evacuating had to leave the vehicle and flee on foot. The evidence also says more than 25 volunteer vehicles have been damaged during humanitarian missions, and that volunteers have been killed or injured in Kherson.

The same pattern reaches Kherson Regional Hospital, where doctors are treating injuries caused by drone attacks. Serhiy Yushchuk, head of the anaesthesiology department and acting deputy chief medical officer, said the hospital’s work changed dramatically after the full-scale war began. Before then, he said, it was mainly a place for planned care. Now, he estimates, about 70 percent of wounded residents have blast injuries from drone attacks.

Across Ukraine, the wider toll is also rising. July was the deadliest month for civilians in more than four years, with at least 437 people killed and more than 2,600 injured. In Kherson, that violence is felt most sharply from above.

Life below ground

For civilians who stay, the choice is often not whether to move, but how to live with the risk. Svitlana’s home was damaged by a missile; she said the scene was loud, gray and frightening. Police and volunteers helped her evacuate, but she did not leave Kherson. Instead, she moved to the Mangust Centre for displaced people, which offers temporary accommodation and support to those whose homes have been damaged or destroyed, including people who arrive without documents or even clothes.

At Mangust, Raisa Maksymets helps residents replace documents, register as internally displaced people and regain access to pensions and other assistance. She said people stay because the city still needs them: “We are holding on because if everyone leaves the city, who will keep it going?” For older residents in particular, leaving is often impossible because of cost, physical limits or the wish to remain at home.

Others have shifted into collective sites inside the city. Liuba lives there with her husband Yurii, daughter-in-law Sofiia and their cat, Marharytka, after moving because of security risks in their neighborhood. The family has already lived through attacks: windows shattered in one strike, and in another they sheltered in a corridor for hours while rockets hit the area. They follow Telegram chat groups where residents warn each other when drones are nearby, another way people try to track danger overhead.

At one collective site, residents have only minutes to reach an underground shelter when an air-raid siren sounds, though artillery can hit in seconds, so some choose to sleep there through the night. Tetyana, a volunteer preparing hot meals for people across the Kherson area, is also sent underground when sirens interrupt her work. Her home has been damaged in four separate strikes. She and her 15-year-old son shelter beneath the same closed kindergarten used by other residents, where, she said, people have been coming for three years because they feel safer than at home.

The same underground pattern now reaches children’s lives too. What happened

The World Bank has started attaching reproducibility packages to its research outputs, trying to make findings easier to verify at a time when doubts about irreproducible social-science results have become harder to ignore. The move matters because the bank’s working papers and reports help shape policy decisions tied to loans and grants worth roughly US$120 billion a year.

For years, social and behavioural sciences have faced an “open secret” that some findings are difficult to reproduce. When independent researchers cannot get the same result even with the same data and analysis methods, confidence in the original finding weakens. Earlier this year, broader reproducibility analyses added to those concerns. In that context, the World Bank stands out because it is not just a research institution: it is a Washington, DC-based lender and grant-maker whose evidence base affects low- and middle-income countries.

Since 2023, the bank has taken concrete steps to improve reproducibility, and since 2025 every published finding has been paired with a reproducibility package. These packages document how conclusions were reached from the original data, and include code, data and other supplementary material. They are citable, and where appropriate they include instructions for rerunning code. Before some materials are released publicly, a dedicated internal team checks whether the study can be reproduced; packages that pass receive a badge marking the research as reproducible.

The approach is beginning to spread. The Inter-American Development Bank has started releasing similar packages, and staff at other international-development organizations told Nature they sometimes check provenance internally but do nothing as systematically as the development banks. One unresolved issue is how to handle artificial intelligence tools that may scan released code or data and misinterpret them; the Inter-American Development Bank says it is discussing that risk internally.

What happened

Reports that two children died after receiving gene-editing treatments in separate clinical trials in China have put the country’s fast-track investigator-initiated trials, or IITs, under fresh scrutiny, especially as new rules are meant to tighten oversight without slowing biomedical innovation.

IITs are a popular way for researchers and biotech companies to test new therapies, and they move much faster than China’s drug-approval system, which is supervised by the National Medical Products Administration and is roughly equivalent to the US Food and Drug Administration. They have become a major part of China’s growing biomedicine sector, especially for cell and gene therapies: the number of annually registered IITs in this area rose 11-fold to 207 between 2015 and 2023. Similar trials also exist in the United States, but there they require FDA approval.

Order 818 and the push for tighter control

The deaths were reported only months after the government introduced stricter rules for IITs involving new biomedical technologies. Order 818 took effect on 1 May and covers trials using cell therapies, brain implants and gene-editing treatments. Under the order, only sanjia, or high-level, hospitals can conduct these trials.

Researchers said earlier rules already existed but were not strictly enforced. A lawyer at Han Kun Law Offices in Shanghai said much cell-therapy activity took place in non-sanjia institutions, private clinics and hospital-affiliated ventures, creating a grey market in which unauthorized or fraudulent therapies could be sold for large sums and, in some cases, lead to medical accidents and deaths.

The new system gives the National Health Commission power to carry out a second assessment, suspend, alter or cancel trials over technical or ethical concerns, and fine violators millions of yuan. For the first time, companies can also initiate IITs themselves, rather than working only through clinical researchers.

Scientists observe Einstein’s gravity in the quantum world for the first time

What happened

An international team led by Ben-Gurion University of the Negev, the University of Ulm and the University of Oxford says it has directly observed, for the first time, a gravitational effect in a falling quantum object that matches Einstein’s equivalence principle under the conditions tested.

Published September 2 in *Science Advances*, the work uses ultracold rubidium atoms and a device called the Quantum Galileo Interferometer to split an atom’s wave into two paths: one held in place, the other allowed to fall under gravity. By recombining the paths, the researchers measured a tiny phase difference that had built up while one wave fell and the other stayed fixed. They say the result matches the prediction for a quantum object when Einstein’s equivalence principle is extended into the quantum realm.

The experiment was carried out at Ben-Gurion University using clouds of rubidium atoms cooled to just above absolute zero and manipulated near a specially designed atom chip. The team says the setup allowed them to explore the overlap between quantum mechanics, which describes the behavior of very small objects, and Einstein’s theory of gravity, which explains falling bodies and large-scale structure.

What the result does and does not mean

The researchers say this does not amount to a unified theory of quantum mechanics and gravity, and it does not show that gravity itself is quantum. It does, however, support the idea that Einstein’s equivalence principle remains compatible with quantum behavior in the regime they tested.

The experiment also does not rule out a proposal associated with co-author Professor Sir Roger Penrose that quantum mechanics could break down for sufficiently massive objects held in superposition long enough. The team says the next goal is to extend the method to heavier objects, including nanodiamonds, and that related experiments are already underway at Ben-Gurion University.

What happened

University of Maryland researchers say they have found a powerful antivenom strategy in western diamondback rattlesnake blood, using the snakes’ own toxin-blocking proteins to protect against venom from several species and potentially point to safer, broader treatments for a major global health problem.

The study, led by Distinguished University Professor of Biology Sean B. Carroll and published in the *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*, builds on a problem long familiar to snakebite medicine: existing antivenoms save lives, but they are costly, can vary in quality and effectiveness, may trigger immune reactions, and do not always cover the full mix of toxins in different venoms. The World Health Organization estimates venomous snakes kill 80,000 to 140,000 people each year, with hundreds of thousands more left disabled, often in rural areas where treatment is hard to get.

How the rattlesnake proteins work

Carroll’s team traced the protection to FETUA proteins, which western diamondback rattlesnakes evolved to shield themselves from venom. In 2022, his laboratory identified one of them, FETUA-3, as a blocker of metalloproteinase toxins. In the new work, co-authors including Elda Sánchez at Texas A&M University-Kingsville tested what individual FETUA proteins could do and found they could blunt some venom effects, such as bleeding or enzyme activity, but none could fully prevent death on its own.

The result changed when the researchers combined several proteins. Those mixtures were far more effective than single proteins, and optimized combinations were about 10 times more potent than the current sheep-derived rattlesnake antivenom. They completely neutralized rattlesnake venom in lab experiments and offered broad protection against venom from multiple viper species.

Carroll said the team is now applying the same approach to other toxin families, with early commercial use potentially coming first in veterinary medicine and human treatments later.
