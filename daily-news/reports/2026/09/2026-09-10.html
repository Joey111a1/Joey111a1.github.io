What happened

Russian pressure around Kramatorsk is steadily eroding one of Ukraine’s last strongholds in Donbas, with bridges destroyed, drone and bomb attacks multiplying, and a recent Kyiv visit by Donald Trump’s envoys ending without a major breakthrough.

Kramatorsk under daily attack

Iryna Rybakova, a press officer with Ukraine’s 93rd mechanized brigade who is based in Kramatorsk, said the front line is inching toward the city and that the past few weeks have been “insane.” She said she has watched her units being pushed from one strategic hub after another during years of service in eastern Ukraine.

Kramatorsk remains one of Ukraine’s last strongholds in the Donbas region, but Rybakova said Russian forces are now dangerously close. Nearly all of the city’s bridges have been destroyed, and guided bombs and drones have become a daily reality. “The attacks happen dozens of times a day,” she said, adding that cars, utility buildings, residential buildings, administrative buildings and garages have been hit and set on fire.

She described a city where movement has become restricted by fear and routine threat. “Toward the evening and in the morning, almost no one is out and about,” she said. “During the day, there are gunmen everywhere … everyone walks around with a rifle, goes out for a coffee with a rifle, heads to the store with a rifle and so on because drones are everywhere now and you never know. They can strike at any moment.”

Ukraine’s eastern line is under pressure

The strain around Kramatorsk is unfolding as the first visit to Kyiv by Witkoff and Jared Kushner, Donald Trump’s envoy and son-in-law, came and went on Sunday without a major breakthrough. Against that backdrop, Ukraine is increasingly on the back foot in several places along the eastern front line, a marked change from the spring, when Kyiv was taking more land than it was losing.

Jennie Olmsted, a Russia analyst at the Institute for the Study of War, said a quick breakthrough is unlikely but that there has been a shift. She said Russian forces are carrying out infiltration missions and making slow progress toward Sloviansk and Kramatorsk, while also advancing in Kostyantynivka. Russian gains remain slow, but they are steadily reshaping the front.

The battlefield pace remains glacial by territory, but costly. ISW said Russia advanced or infiltrated about 124 square kilometers of Ukrainian territory in August, roughly a quarter of what it seized in the same month last year. The institute has also calculated that, at the current pace, Russian forces would not take over Donetsk until 2032, if they can seize it at all. Western intelligence services estimate Russia is losing about 40,000 troops a month.

The “fortress belt” is wearing down

Russia’s gains matter because they are chipping away at the so-called “fortress belt” in eastern Ukraine, a chain of four industrial cities that form the backbone of the country’s defenses in the east. The belt — Sloviansk, Kramatorsk, Druzhkivka and Kostyantynivka — has been heavily fortified over the past few years because it helps defend the rest of Donbas and also protects eastern and central Ukraine.

Its geography is part of its importance. The cities, roads and rail lines create a natural barrier before the flatter and less defensible territory to the west. Olmsted said the concern is that if Russia breaks through there, it could move deeper into Ukraine much faster.

The belt has eroded over the past four and a half years, and Kostyantynivka, its southernmost anchor, has now been effectively lost as a stronghold. Russia spent almost a year trying to seize it, and although pockets of Ukrainian resistance remain in the north of the city, Olmsted said its operational value for Kyiv is gone. She said the pattern is familiar: Moscow cannot usually take towns quickly while leaving infrastructure intact, so it wages slow, grinding warfare that leaves places uninhabitable.

Civilians are still there, but movement is shrinking.

According to the Ukraine-controlled Donetsk regional military administration, nearly 93,000 Ukrainians, including more than 4,800 children, are still living in the areas under Kyiv’s control. But daily life is getting harder. Hennadiy Yudin, a police officer with the “White Angels” unit that helps evacuate civilians from frontline villages, said officers can no longer drive into Dobropillya and nearby villages.

He said the military is evacuating people on foot and his unit is picking them up about 10 kilometers from Dobropillya because the area is mined and drone activity is extreme. In Kramatorsk, Rybakova said many civilians are still present and life continues, but she expects that to change. She said there is not yet a curfew, although authorities have said one may soon be imposed, and she warned that shelling has become a daily fact of life.

Rybakova also said the house where she used to live was bombed just the night before. Olmsted said Russian forces appear to be preparing the ground for attacks farther west, especially against Sloviansk, by degrading Ukrainian logistics and defenses and making it harder to move supplies to forward positions.

The system couples a heterogeneous model pool with intelligent routing, and records for each turn the predicted capability demand, the service tier selected, and the interaction that followed.
