ICE Deportees Are Vanishing Into Salvadoran Prisons
global_south_and_exploitedmaterial_harmpolitics_and_powermajor supplement
What happened

Deportees returned from the United States are being detained in El Salvador, with families often unable to learn where they are or whether they are alive. The detentions affect people who have not been charged with crimes, while at least five deportees have reportedly died in Salvadoran custody.

On a sweltering day in May, a woman identified as Ana waited at San Salvador airport for her 25-year-old son, who had been detained in Texas and deported. She expected to drive him home, cook dinner and work out what came next. But when other men emerged in ICE-issued sweatsuits, her son was not among them.

A police officer searched his name on a tablet and told her that he was being taken to prison. Ana was allowed five minutes with him at a police station before he was transferred. He wore a white shirt and handcuffs. She was later told he would be sent to a maximum-security prison that has become notorious for deaths in custody. She has not seen or heard from him since.

Ana’s son had no charges in the United States or El Salvador, according to the account. A document from El Salvador’s justice department states that he has no criminal history. His public defender has not been able to meet him, but told Ana that he was charged with “illicit association,” a generic charge used against people detained during the government’s gang crackdown. The charge carries a maximum sentence of 60 years.

Detentions begin after deportation flights land

On most days, deportees are flown to San Salvador. Salvadoran officials board the aircraft and read names from a list; those people are escorted away in handcuffs and placed in police vehicles. Other passengers are reportedly pulled aside on the tarmac after security officials judge that they look like gang members.

A person familiar with the flights estimated that about four people from each flight of 120 passengers are jailed. Some deportees said the number was higher. El Salvador’s migration ministry recorded 9,829 deportees arriving on at least 83 flights from January through June, as the number of deportations to the country roughly doubled in the first half of the year.

Those detained are held without regular contact with relatives or lawyers and are often not told what crimes they are accused of committing. Many have been sent to CECOT, a maximum-security mega-prison; others have been moved among prisons. Families say officials will not confirm where their relatives are being held or whether they are still alive.

Cristosal, a Salvadoran human-rights organization, says at least five U.S. deportees have died in custody. In three cases, it said, bodies showed signs of violence or torture. The evidence also describes families finding detainees only indirectly: one brother recognized his relative in two frames of a Salvadoran government video showing prisoners eating lunch and working inside a prison.

The government responded to requests from the Inter-American Court of Human Rights by describing the detainees as held through “collaboration and sovereign cooperation” between El Salvador and the United States. It called El Salvador a “receptor state” while saying the United States retained “penal jurisdiction.” Neither government has explained how that arrangement operates.

A prison policy extended to Salvadoran nationals

The detentions grew out of a 2025 agreement in which President Nayib Bukele offered to accept people deported by the United States and incarcerate them in Salvadoran prisons for a fee. The United States paid nearly $5 million. Bukele described the arrangement as an opportunity to “outsource part of its prison system,” while then-Secretary of State Marco Rubio called it an “act of extraordinary friendship.”

The first flights of Venezuelan deportees arrived in San Salvador in March of the previous year. The United States later stopped sending non-Salvadorans to El Salvador and began using about 30 other “third country” destinations. El Salvador, however, continued imprisoning deportees, including its own nationals.

The policy operates alongside Bukele’s “state of exception,” announced in 2022 and extended for more than four years. His government has made more than 90,000 arrests—about 2 percent of the adult population—on suspicion of gang ties. The crackdown has sharply reduced the homicide rate, but it has also produced mass trials, detention without charges and allegations of abuse. Some detainees are as young as 12.

Families interviewed about deported relatives say that people are being detained even without convictions or outstanding warrants. Sarah Bishop, a Baruch College professor who has interviewed relatives of 30 Salvadoran men detained after deportation, said only one had been released. In one case, she warned a U.S. immigration judge that a man was highly likely to be jailed if returned to El Salvador. He was deported and detained soon afterward.

Responsibility disputed as families search for answers

ICE says it has no responsibility after deportees leave the United States. An ICE spokesperson stated that once a person is removed, they are no longer in ICE custody or under the agency’s purview.

The evidence describes a different practical outcome for families. Ana has spent months taking soap, food and clothing to the prison where she believes her son is held, but officers provide no information about his legal status. Other relatives travel across El Salvador, submit care packages and exchange fragments of information through a WhatsApp group called “Families in search of justice.”

The United States and El Salvador have shared deportees’ criminal-history information under a 2014 memorandum of cooperation. ICE originally said the arrangement would help El Salvador categorize and monitor repatriated criminals. Under Bukele’s government, the information appears to be used to justify detention even when deportees have no active arrest warrants.

El Salvador’s justice minister, Gustavo Villatoro, has identified some deportees as gang members and said they must go to jail. He wrote that deported gang members do not return to the streets but are detained as terrorists. Families of those imprisoned say they have been left without clear charges, access to counsel or confirmation of their relatives’ whereabouts.

Gaza’s Open Horizon
global_south_and_exploitedmaterial_harmpolitics_and_powermajor supplement
What happened

Gaza’s Mediterranean shoreline remains a place of refuge and connection even as war has turned its beaches into contaminated, crowded spaces where displaced families live in tents, fishing livelihoods have been devastated, and children continue to swim, walk and fly improvised kites.

Gaza’s sea has become both an escape from confinement and a record of how the war has changed daily life. During the long summer, children went to the beach early in the morning or before sunset, stepping around garbage and tents to reach water described as contaminated by sewage. The heat remained severe; humidity sometimes reached 86 percent in July and August.

A resident’s daily walk illustrates the shoreline’s continuing pull. Seeking to reach 10,000 steps, the writer walks from home to the seafront road and back—a distance that happens to match the target. After a month, the step count has become secondary to reaching the Mediterranean, especially before sunset, when the heat eases slightly.

The shoreline as refuge—and displacement

Before the war, Gaza’s 25-mile coastline was a shared public space. Families swam, ate watermelon, drank tea, played backgammon and took wedding or family photographs. Vendors sold grilled corn, lupin beans and cotton candy, while children rode camels or horses along the water. Tourist facilities, including chalets, amusement parks and restaurants, lined parts of the shore and drew thousands of visitors in summer.

That landscape has largely disappeared. The stretch of sand where the writer’s family once played is now a makeshift camp for people displaced from northern and southern parts of the Gaza Strip. More than 2 million people live on a strip of land described as about 25 miles long and seven and a half miles wide. About half of Gazans now live in tents, which provide little protection from the heat and, as the season changes, may offer limited shelter from the cold.

The account describes more than 60 percent of Gaza as inaccessible as the so-called yellow line expands westward, pushing people closer to the sea. Before the war, leaving Gaza in any direction required a permit, while the sea offered a horizon that could be viewed without a document, checkpoint or drone in sight. As available space has contracted, contact with the water has taken on greater importance as a way to escape the feeling of being trapped.

Children’s kites and a narrowing freedom

A few children still fly kites along the beach. Kite-flying was common in Gaza before and during the war, but the practice has become an act of defiance after Israelis said kites would be treated as a hostile act because they had sometimes been used to launch incendiary devices into Israel.

Children have adapted by making kites from whatever materials they can find. Instead of brightly colored paper and wooden sticks bought from stationery stores, they use dry palm fronds, plastic bags, flour and food-aid sacks, and scraps from old notebooks. One boy carved a frame from a long piece of wood. The improvised kites preserve a familiar childhood activity while reflecting the scarcity and danger surrounding it.

The sea’s symbolic openness therefore exists alongside physical and political restrictions. Children can still reach the beach, but they do so around refuse, displacement camps and polluted water, while even flying a kite has acquired a potentially hostile meaning.

Fishing livelihoods lost to the war

The shoreline is also an economic lifeline. Hasan Shehada, a fisherman, said Gaza’s port services and many fishing boats had been destroyed. He told the writer that going onto the water remains dangerous because of Israeli naval fire. Before the war, he and other fishermen—including siblings and cousins—could catch enough fish to feed his eight-member family and earn an income.

Now, he said, he lacks the boats and equipment that made that work possible. “The war has changed our sea too,” he said, describing sitting on the beach and crying while thinking about the difference between the past and the present.

For families living along the shore, the sea is no longer simply a place of rest. Windblown sand enters the tents, humidity weighs on the air and the shelters are being patched against whatever the coming weather brings. Yet the water remains the widest visible space available: a place to look when the surrounding tents, restrictions and destruction become unbearable.

As the US pulls its forces out of Iraq, what happens to Iran-backed armed groups?
global_south_and_exploitedpolitics_and_poweranchorregional expansion
What happened

The departure of the last few hundred US troops from Iraq is ending the military phase of a coalition mission against ISIL, but it is also testing Prime Minister Ali al-Zaidi’s plan to bring Iran-aligned armed groups under state control. The outcome could shape Iraq’s security, sovereignty and relationship with both Washington and Tehran.

Iraq is marking the withdrawal with four planned “Sovereignty Days”, presenting the end of the US military presence as a restoration of national authority after more than two decades of foreign intervention. The remaining US personnel had served mainly in advisory roles under Operation Inherent Resolve, the coalition mission launched in 2015 to fight ISIL.

The withdrawal plan predates al-Zaidi, but it fits his stated effort to ensure that the Iraqi state controls decisions on war and peace. Speaking at the United Nations General Assembly, he said Iraq wanted to move its relationship with strategic partners from military cooperation towards economic and development cooperation.

The end of the US deployment does not mean that all foreign forces have left Iraq. Turkey still maintains a military presence, although it has pledged to hand over its Bashiqa-Zilkan base in the north, according to Middle East Eye.

A deadline for disarming powerful groups

The government has set June 30, 2027, as the deadline for armed groups to disarm or come under direct state control. Al-Zaidi had extended the timetable from an earlier September 30, 2026 deadline, building in more time for what is likely to be a difficult political and security process.

The groups are not all alike. Many paramilitary forces were formed in 2014 after Shia religious leader Ali Sistani called for mobilisation against ISIL. They later became part of the Popular Mobilisation Forces, formally under state authority. The most powerful factions, however, are described as operating with considerable independence and maintaining political, military and financial links to Iran.

Kataeb Hezbollah, Harakat al-Nujaba and Kataeb Sayyid al-Shuhada have already indicated that they are unwilling to disarm, Middle East Eye reported. Hamzeh Hadad of the European Council on Foreign Relations said some factions might integrate into state institutions because their leaders have political ambitions, while groups committed to advancing Iran’s regional political vision have less reason to surrender their weapons.

The militias have long used the presence of US and other foreign forces, including Turkey, as a justification for retaining their own weapons and chains of command. Douglas Silliman, a former US ambassador to Iraq and Kuwait, said al-Zaidi was trying to build a political case that groups “more beholden to Iran” and operating without government authorisation no longer had a reason to remain armed.

The groups and their supporters have celebrated the US departure as a victory. At the same time, some have been involved in attacks on US and Israeli targets during the regional conflict, and experts cited by Al Jazeera said factions aligned with Iran’s “axis of resistance” could continue targeting US interests.

The risks of forcing the issue

The central danger is that an attempt to impose state control could produce a confrontation between Iraqi security forces and militias that are embedded in politics and public institutions. Hisham al-Mozani of the al-Amal Association warned that clashes could return Iraq to civil war, damaging security and the economy. He said some factions had existed for decades and were represented in political parties, parliament and ministries.

Ali al-Bayati, a former commissioner of the Iraqi High Commission for Human Rights, was also pessimistic about the state’s ability to restrain the groups. He attributed their strength partly to the failure of the US-led occupation to build effective democratic, military and security institutions after 2003. Middle East Eye reported allegations that armed groups had killed, kidnapped and tortured thousands of Iraqis and helped suppress the 2019 Tishreen pro-democracy movement.

Al-Zaidi’s government has sought short-term safeguards. He told The New York Times that Washington and Iran-aligned militias had assured him they would refrain from attacks for 90 days after the withdrawal. But Renad Mansour of Chatham House said the groups remained in “full-on war mode” and were working with Iran’s Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps in the region.

The withdrawal could therefore reduce one justification for militia weapons while also giving Iran greater room to influence Iraq. Silliman described the policy as a high-stakes attempt to politically weaken the groups; Al Jazeera reported that it could instead open the way to a dangerous domestic standoff.

A less visible US role, and new vulnerabilities

The operational consequences of the troop departure remain unclear. Iraq’s security forces—including the army, police, counterterrorism units and PMF forces—have gained substantial experience fighting ISIL. Silliman said Iraqi forces were confident they could handle most threats to the country’s sovereignty.

But Iraq still lacks a substantial air-defence capability, according to the experts cited by Al Jazeera. That is significant because pro-Iran militias have used drones, rockets and missiles against targets inside Iraq. Kurdish officials have particularly criticised the withdrawal after attacks on the semi-autonomous Kurdistan region were reportedly thwarted by US anti-missile and anti-drone systems. Masrour Barzani, prime minister of the Kurdistan Regional Government, said the region was vulnerable without an appropriate defensive system.

The United States and Iraq appear to be returning to a 2008 Strategic Framework and Security Agreement, which provides for continued but non-binding US security support. The future role of Washington is expected to rely less on a visible military presence and more on intelligence, training, technical assistance, capacity-building and institutional cooperation. Neither the Iraqi government nor the Pentagon has clearly explained how that arrangement will work, particularly in intelligence and logistics.

For Iraq, the immediate challenge is to balance competing pressures: prevent ISIL’s resurgence, avoid becoming a battleground in the regional conflict, maintain ties with Washington and manage Iran’s influence without provoking a militia confrontation. The government has also promoted deeper economic cooperation with the US, but experts said continuing regional conflict could make that difficult.

Iran has lost considerable leverage in the Strait of Hormuz. It can’t go on like this forever
global_south_and_exploitedpolitics_and_powereconomy_and_businessmajor supplement
What happened

Gulf oil producers are moving large volumes of crude through the Strait of Hormuz with US military support, reducing Iran’s ability to use the chokepoint as economic leverage. But the operation depends on costly, risky workarounds while inventories decline and fuel prices remain high for consumers and businesses.

Oil and petroleum-product flows through the Strait of Hormuz averaged 13.1 million barrels per day last week, according to Kpler, a marine data-tracking service. That was just under 80% of the 17.1 million barrels a day that had passed through the strait before the war began. Total Middle Eastern crude flows, including oil moving through alternative routes, had recovered to 98% of prewar levels, JPMorgan said.

Matt Smith, Kpler’s director of commodity research, said the volume showed that “Iran is losing its influence over” the strait. Gulf producers have restored much of the traffic through a complex operation involving military-escorted shuttles and so-called “dark” transits, in which vessels move surreptitiously. The arrangement has enabled significant oil flows despite the risks around the chokepoint.

A costly operation is keeping oil moving

The recent increase in traffic also includes Saudi oil returning to the strait. Saudi Arabia had primarily diverted some shipments to the Red Sea after Iran-allied Houthis attacked a major Saudi oil pipeline earlier this month. After the attack temporarily shut down roughly 7 million barrels of oil headed toward the Red Sea, the Saudis shifted some crude back through Hormuz.

Oil prices approached $110 a barrel after the pipeline disruption, but later fell as Saudi Arabia restored alternative flows. Satellite imagery on Sunday showed all seven berths open at two important Saudi ports on the Red Sea coast, Yanbu and Al Muajjiz. Kpler said the imagery indicated that the East-West pipeline had resumed operating at higher levels.

The recovery in crossings does not mean the route has become safe. Natasha Kaneva, JPMorgan’s head of global commodities strategy, said higher crossings reflected the industry’s growing ability to operate under sustained risk rather than improved security. Insurance costs remain extremely high: insurers are valuing the largest class of five- to 10-year-old oil tankers at $150 million, compared with $135 million to build a new tanker.

The United States is also expending substantial military resources to keep oil moving. Iran is unable to send its own oil through the strait because of a US naval blockade, according to the account, and has been losing a major source of economic leverage. As a result, Smith said, attacks on tankers have become more common as Iran tries to deter vessels from transiting the Gulf. He expected that pressure to continue as Tehran seeks to regain control over the waterway.

The market is absorbing the shock—for now

Oil supplies have been sustained through more than military-escorted shipping. Pipeline diversions, increased production outside the Gulf and a significant decline in global demand have helped offset the disruption. JPMorgan estimated that global oil inventories had fallen by about 2 billion barrels during the Iran war, yet the market had avoided a return to the record crude prices reached in 2008.

That resilience may not last. With more crude being withdrawn from inventories than added, stockpiles could eventually become too low to meet demand. At that point, prices would need to rise sharply enough to reduce consumption and restore balance. The timing is unclear. Kaneva said JPMorgan no longer had a baseline forecast for the conflict’s outcome and did not know how to model the endgame.

The central issue, in her view, is not only how long the war continues, but how long the market can physically deliver the oil customers require. Without a genuine resolution over Hormuz, the system will depend increasingly on remaining inventories and on the ability of producers, shipping companies and militaries to maintain risky workarounds.

The pressure is already reaching households and businesses. Oil has remained above $90 a barrel throughout the month and spent most of September above $100. Gasoline prices are near their highest level of the war, while diesel has exceeded its previous record and risen above $6 a gallon. Refined petroleum-product flows from the Middle East remain at only 58% of prewar levels, according to JPMorgan, showing that crude’s partial recovery has not translated into normal supplies of gasoline and diesel.

Iran’s leverage has weakened, but the confrontation remains unstable

Market expectations of a possible peace agreement have also affected prices. President Donald Trump’s repeated comments about a potential deal with Iran to reopen the strait have had a larger influence on oil prices than some changes in actual physical supply, the account said.

Yet the current balance remains fragile. Greater tanker traffic reflects an increasingly expensive effort to operate under threat, not a settled reopening of Hormuz. The United States is sustaining the security operation, producers are redirecting shipments and the market is drawing down its reserves while fuel prices stay elevated.

Iran’s reduced ability to obstruct the strait has therefore not ended the confrontation. With its economic leverage diminished, attacks on tankers may intensify, while the oil market’s adaptations face limits. The longer the situation continues without a resolution, the greater the risk that depleted inventories and constrained refined-product supplies will force prices higher.

Slew of lawsuits over disability access frustrates US cafe and shop owners
global_south_and_exploitedpolitics_and_powereconomy_and_businessanchor
What happened

Rodrigo Nogueira’s Manhattan cafe became one of dozens of small US storefront businesses caught in a fight over private lawsuits enforcing disability-access rules, with owners describing costly, confusing cases and disability advocates arguing that litigation remains essential to civil rights.

In April 2025, lawyers contacted Nogueira about a summons accusing No More Cafe, his restaurant in Manhattan’s East Village, of violating Title III of the Americans With Disabilities Act (ADA). The lawsuit listed 35 alleged violations. One concerned an outdoor table, although Nogueira said the cafe had no outdoor tables. Other allegations involved the restaurant’s interior, while the plaintiff said he had been unable to enter.

Nogueira said he later found that the plaintiff and the attorney had filed complaints against numerous small businesses. He said the attorney alone had brought more than 100 ADA lawsuits against storefront businesses over nine years, while the plaintiff had been involved in 67 cases. Nogueira filed a motion to dismiss without first hiring a lawyer, but said the judge ruled that a company could not represent itself in court.

He then reviewed public court records and spoke with nearby business owners facing similar lawsuits. In a post on his website, Nogueira said every owner he spoke with had opened within the previous year or two and was an immigrant. He said several had already spent thousands of dollars on legal fees, while others had not realized they had been sued until their response deadlines had passed. Other owners interviewed by The Guardian, mostly off the record, also said they felt targeted by serial ADA litigants.

The ADA, enacted in 1990, is intended to prevent barriers to stores, restaurants, bars and cafes. Its standards allow businesses to make changes when they are “readily achievable” or possible without substantial difficulty or expense; businesses with greater resources are expected to remove more barriers. Yet owners and advocates say the rules can be difficult to interpret, particularly in older buildings where structural changes may require government approval.

The financial pressure can make challenging a lawsuit impractical. Business advocates said settling may involve $13,000 to $20,000 in legal fees, while going to court can cost $20,000 to $50,000 or more. In Nogueira’s case, the plaintiff offered to settle for $8,000, excluding Nogueira’s own legal fees. Nogueira said more than 75% of the lawsuit’s 35 listed violations were invalid.

Small-business advocates, including Manhattan Chamber of Commerce president Jessica Walker and Lawsuit Reform Alliance of New York director Tom Stebbins, said the system can make lawyers money from claims that may not produce meaningful accessibility changes. Disability advocates dispute that characterization. Michelle Uzeta of the Disability Rights Education and Defense Fund said most cases involve actual violations, while law professor Ruth Colker said private litigation is the enforcement mechanism Congress established.

A bipartisan ADA 30 Days to Comply Act proposed in the US House in December would give businesses 30 days to address violations. Disability advocates oppose that approach, saying a warning period could weaken enforcement of disability rights.

Strasbourg’s test for disruptive political expression

The European Court of Human Rights in Strasbourg has long helped define how far governments can go in restricting protest and political expression. Its judgments establish human-rights standards for the 46 countries of the Council of Europe.

Earlier cases recognized that environmental protest could be inconvenient, unconventional and disruptive while still deserving protection. In *Bryan and Others v. Russia*, the Court found that the rights of Greenpeace activists had been violated after they were detained during a protest against Arctic oil drilling. In *Friedrich and Others v. Poland*, it reached a similar conclusion after activists protesting a coal shipment were detained in Gdańsk.

The Court’s more recent approach was tested by *Ludes and Others v. France*. In 2019, activists from the non-violent climate movement ANV-COP21 removed official portraits of President Emmanuel Macron from town halls across France. They described the action as a temporary “requisition” intended to symbolize what they considered inadequate government action on climate change. French authorities treated the removals as theft.

The activists argued that their convictions violated Article 10 of the European Convention on Human Rights, which protects freedom of expression. In July 2025, however, the Court ruled by five votes to two that there had been no violation. It recognized that the action was non-violent, conveyed a political message and concerned climate change, a matter of public interest. But the majority held that removing the portraits had already communicated the message and that refusing to return them could therefore be punished as theft. It considered the suspended fines—between EUR 200 and EUR 500—relatively modest.

Two dissenting judges disagreed with separating the removal of the portraits from their continued absence. They argued that the portraits’ absence was itself part of the political message and warned against using criminal law too readily against peaceful political activists.

When formal protection raises the practical cost

The *Ludes* judgment did not remove climate protest from freedom-of-expression protections. But it allowed a criminal conviction to stand despite recognizing the action’s peaceful character, political meaning and connection to a matter of public concern. That tension is central to the question of what protection remains when protest deliberately disrupts ordinary rules.

Protest combines freedom of expression with freedom of assembly, but its democratic role also extends beyond those legal categories. It allows people to challenge those in power between elections, make dissent visible and bring issues into public debate when established political channels do not produce change. The source connects this tradition to advances in workers’ rights, women’s political participation, civil rights and environmental protection.

The consequences of criminalization therefore reach beyond the people arrested or convicted. Arrests, prosecutions, fines and lengthy legal proceedings can increase the personal and financial price of participation, potentially affecting whether others speak, assemble or join future demonstrations. The right to protest can remain on paper while becoming harder to exercise in practice.

International human-rights experts have urged a different approach. In October 2025, Michel Forst, the UN Special Rapporteur on environmental defenders under the Aarhus Convention, issued guidelines calling on states not to use civil disobedience to restrict civic space and to ensure that sanctions against peaceful protesters are necessary and proportionate. Several UN Human Rights Council special rapporteurs expressed strong support for the guidelines in March 2026.

The dispute is consequently not only about new anti-protest legislation. It also concerns how governments and courts interpret existing rules on public order, property and criminal conduct. Further processing, including Bayesian statistical analysis and simulations of the antenna’s response, will be used to separate the cosmic signal from radio emissions, particularly those from the Milky Way.

Over an expected two-year mission, the team hopes to collect about 1,000 hours of observations. The data could illuminate how hydrogen gathered into the earliest stars and galaxies and how dark matter influenced that process. Development is underway, with laboratory prototypes built and environmental testing in progress. The researchers hope to launch within five years; the project has received UK Space Agency funding.

‘Godzilla’ of weight-loss drugs could provide other important health benefits
science_generalglobal_south_and_exploitedpublic_healthanchorscience_journalism
What happened

An 80-week phase 3 trial found that retatrutide, an unapproved drug that targets the GLP-1, GIP and glucagon pathways, reduced body weight and improved blood-sugar and cardiometabolic measures in adults with obesity or overweight and type 2 diabetes. The randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled study enrolled 2,047 adults across Argentina, Australia, Brazil, India, Mexico, Romania, Spain and the United States; participants had a BMI of at least 27 and an average age of 55. Average weight loss ranged from 11.9% with 4mg to 18.8% with 12mg, compared with 5.1% for placebo. At the highest dose, 47% lost at least 20% of their starting weight and 31% lost at least 25%, versus 6% and 3% respectively with placebo.

Researchers also reported improved blood pressure, lipid profiles, inflammation markers and blood-sugar control: 72% of participants receiving retatrutide reached an HbA1c of 6.5% or lower, compared with 29% on placebo. Gastrointestinal problems were the most common adverse events, including diarrhoea and nausea. Seven deaths occurred during the trial, but investigators deemed them unrelated to the study intervention. The findings were published in *The Lancet* and presented in Milan; the study was funded by Eli Lilly, retatrutide’s manufacturer. The drug has not been approved by any regulatory agency.
