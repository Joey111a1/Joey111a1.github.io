Explosions in Kuhestak

At around 9pm, as the United States launched a new wave of attacks across southern Iran, people in Kuhestak heard a series of explosions. Initial reports said a telecommunications tower and facilities near the pier had been hit. The tower was less than 200 metres from the wedding venue.

Moments later, a projectile struck the house where the women and children had gathered, tearing through the roof. Majid, 41, was at the men’s gathering nearby. Residents and emergency workers rushed in before the formal rescue response arrived. The Iranian Red Crescent’s deputy for international and humanitarian affairs, Razieh Alishvandi, described it as a projectile striking a residential home during a wedding. Ebola’s toll on humanity
global_front_pageglobal_south_and_exploitedpublic_healthpolitics_and_powermaterial_harmhumanitarian expansion
What happened

A woman named Celestine was struggling to breathe in an Ebola treatment centre in eastern Democratic Republic of the Congo, and the scene left an OCHA communicator confronting both the disease’s human cost and the limits of care when patients arrive too late.

Celestine in the cube

Priscilla Lecomte, a Public Information Officer with OCHA on assignment in eastern DRC, said she “didn’t really meet Celestine.” She saw only Celestine’s head and feet beneath a blanket, and her frail shoulders from behind. What stopped her was the uneven movement of those shoulders: Celestine was struggling to breathe.

Celestine was being treated in a portable isolation chamber called the cube, inside the Ebola treatment centre. Lecomte said oxygen was being pumped from a ventilator placed next to her, while a doctor in full protective equipment later came by to give her palliative medication through an IV. Dr. Etienne, who had just finished his shift, told Lecomte that Celestine was in a coma, that she had been in the centre for a week, that she was 60 years old, and that she had been infected with Ebola.

The account is shaped by Lecomte’s memory of her mother’s final weeks with cancer. Watching Celestine’s breathing, she said it reminded her of the same uneven movement in her mother’s chest when she could no longer breathe properly. Lecomte said she was scared and thought Celestine could stop breathing at any moment. She also said Celestine was alone in the cube, with no relative and no daughter beside her.

Lecomte wrote that she had not come to visit Celestine, but to document the Ebola response so that people abroad would “care and pay attention” to a crisis that had slipped from the world’s focus. Yet Celestine’s condition became the scene that made the crisis personal for her. Lecomte said she thought about what it would mean if her own mother were in that cube, alone and scared.

A response under pressure

The treatment centre in Rwampara, about half an hour from Bunia in Ituri Province, is run by the non-governmental organization ALIMA, which Lecomte said was doing everything it could to save people like Celestine. She said ALIMA invented the cube as a treatment system that lets medical staff work through transparent plastic walls with gloves and tools attached, while relatives can see loved ones up close and doctors can monitor patients more easily.

Lecomte said the cube is used in Rwampara for the most serious cases and for pregnant women who may need to deliver while suffering from Ebola. In those cases, she wrote, it is very difficult for a doctor to stay in full protective equipment throughout the delivery, so the cube helps.

She also described the strain on health workers. A nurse named Danny told her how hard it was to see so many people dying, including people from his own community. Lecomte said thousands of health workers were doing the best they could, but they were also at great risk. More than 40 of them had died since the beginning of the outbreak, she wrote.

The scale of the outbreak, as Lecomte described it, is severe: more than 3,000 people had died from Ebola, the fatality rate in eastern DRC was 47 per cent, and nearly half of the people infected since the outbreak began in May had died. She said the outbreak was the largest the country had faced and could surpass the 2014-2016 Ebola outbreak in West Africa, which killed more than 10,000 people.

Lecomte framed the crisis as one that would have been stopped under different circumstances — if the health system had been strong enough, or if the disease had been identified early enough. Instead, she said, the virus was outpacing the response. The government, from the Ministry of Health to community health workers, was doing its best, and the international community had sent hundreds of aid workers and experts. But, she wrote, the virus was silent, deadly and unforgiving.

Lecomte said that as she finished the piece she had just learned Celestine had died, first writing that it happened on 16 June 2017 and later adding that she had passed away on 25 August 2026. She ended by saying hundreds of people were still receiving care at Ebola treatment centres across eastern DRC and still needed attention and support.

What happened

The Air Pollutant Index first went past 500 in Serian at 510 at 9am on Friday, then rose to 515 by 5pm after haze conditions worsened overnight. The Prime Minister’s Department said Malaysian King Sultan Ibrahim Sultan Iskandar had consented to the emergency declaration throughout Serian because the haze had reached levels “hazardous to public health and safety.”

The department said the decision was made after considering the advice of the prime minister, the Premier of Sarawak and relevant agencies on air quality in the area. It also said the government would continue to monitor developments and take required actions promptly, including mitigation measures and the dissemination of information and health advice to the public.

Why Serian crossed the threshold

Under Malaysia’s API scale, readings above 400 are classed as “very hazardous.” Sarawak Disaster Management Committee chairman Amar Douglas Uggah Embas had previously said a locality would be declared an emergency if its API reading reached 500 under the National Haze Action Plan. Such a declaration would close all public and private sector activities except essential services.

Serian has been the worst hit district, with “hazardous” air quality recorded since last weekend. The district is about 60km southeast of Kuching and has a population of just under 106,000, according to Malaysia’s Department of Statistics. The Sarawak government website says it covers 2,039 sq km, close to three times the size of Singapore.

Local media reported that readings in Serian began to rise from 6pm on Thursday after haze conditions had shown signs of improvement over the previous 24 hours. It was also the first district to announce school closure on Aug 13, when API readings briefly exceeded 200.

Residents were already feeling the impact

The haze was not just a number on a chart. Serian resident Yasmina R.C. Holland told CNA the current episode was the worst she had experienced in the last two decades, and said she had noticed the air quality deteriorating over the past two weeks.

She said the haze had affected her family, with some unable to go to work and others needing medical attention after breathing difficulties. Holland, a 26-year-old content creator, said she and several family members are asthmatic, adding that even walking outside had become difficult and that washing and laundry outside the house left them exhausted.

Her nephews and nieces have also been affected by school closures in Serian, leaving them unable to attend classes in person. At Farmasi Ting, a pharmacy in the district, all types of masks, including N95 masks, had sold out amid surging demand. Store assistants had limited the number of masks on shelves so more customers could buy them, and the pharmacy’s supervisor said people had been panic buying for the past two weeks, “like during the COVID season.”

A wider haze response

Officials had already launched a three-day cloud-seeding operation on Thursday to try to ease the hazardous haze choking Sarawak, while also aiming to replenish water supplies ahead of expected hotter, drier weather. The state capital, Kuching, recorded an API reading of 306, followed by Samarahan at 292 and Sri Aman at 241.

The emergency also comes against a backdrop of severe haze in Sarawak in recent years. The state’s most recent worst air quality level was in 2019, when the API reached 405, while its last haze emergency was in September 1997, when the API peaked at 860.

A worsening outbreak in eastern Congo

According to data from the National Institute of Public Health collected up to August 31, the epidemic had caused 3,007 deaths and 6,186 confirmed cases, with a case fatality rate of 48.6%.

Active transmission is continuing in 60 health zones across six provinces: Ituri, Tshopo, Bas-Uélé, Haut-Uélé, North Kivu and South Kivu. Ituri is described as the epicenter. The same data says 1,409 patients have recovered, while 830 remain hospitalized or in isolation.

The current outbreak is caused by the Bundibugyo strain, for which there are no specific therapeutic treatments. The World Health Organization is advancing Phase 1 trials for two vaccine candidates, and the DRC has launched a campaign with the Ervebo vaccine because of its cross-protection indications.

Even so, the response is facing several obstacles. The source cites insecurity caused by armed groups, strikes by healthcare workers over unpaid wages and community reluctance to undergo post-mortem testing. Ebola Treatment Centers are operating near capacity, and the WHO, the Africa Centres for Disease Control and Prevention, and the DRC government have all appealed for urgent assistance.

Cuba points to its earlier Ebola response

Cuba has already activated its National Plan for the Prevention and Control of Ebola, preparing the country’s 24 points of entry and monitoring international travelers. That places the Cuban response in a preventive posture at home even as Havana signals readiness to assist abroad.

For the DRC, the immediate need remains practical and urgent: more help to slow transmission, treat patients, support frontline doctors and strengthen monitoring as the outbreak continues across multiple provinces.

US immigration judges told to slash time given to people awaiting visas or green cards before being deported
global_south_and_exploitedpolitics_and_powermaterial_harmanchor
What happened

US immigration judges have been told to narrow or deny time for people waiting on visas, green cards or lawyers, raising the risk that some vulnerable immigrants will be ordered out of the country before their cases or paperwork are ready.

Continuances pushed to the sidelines

Training materials shared with the Guardian say the instruction came through a mandatory presentation in recent days about “continuances” — the legal tool judges have historically used to give immigrants more time to find lawyers, build their cases, or wait for approval of documents from another federal immigration agency.

According to one source familiar with the training, hundreds of immigration judges attended the virtual session. The presentation was led by Keith Hunsucker, a veteran immigration judge on the board of immigration appeals, the appellate branch of the immigration courts. Images of the slides shared with the Guardian show the presentation was titled “Motions to Continue: Efficiently Advancing Cases to Completion.”

The thrust of the training, as described in the materials, was to restrict continuances in favor of faster adjudications, even when extra time could allow someone to receive a legal protection that would let them stay in the US. The materials specifically say an approved visa petition should not automatically delay deportation proceedings if the visa itself is not yet available because of annual quotas.

One slide quoted in the materials said: “Where a party lacks diligence and ‘sleeps on their rights,’ a continuance is unwarranted.” Another warned that “Continuance requests solely for dilatory purposes should not be countenanced by Immigration Judges.”

The people most exposed

The training appears to target some of the people with the strongest claims for remaining in the US. The Guardian’s account says the government’s own materials identify abused and abandoned children and survivors of violent crime among those affected. It also says the guidance explicitly targets special immigrant juvenile, or SIJ, classification for abused, abandoned or neglected youth, and U non-immigrant status for survivors of especially egregious crimes.

The instructions also appeared to discourage judges from giving people more than 10 days to find an attorney before proceeding with a case. One source familiar with the training said: “I thought, who has time to find an attorney in 10 days?” The same source said: “There is no room to be kind; there is only urgency to make them leave [the US], one way or the other.”

That would be a sharp turn for immigrants trying to defend themselves, especially because representation makes a major difference in immigration court. The evidence supplied says that from 2019 to 2024, only about a quarter of people with attorneys were ordered removed from the US, compared with over 60% of those who were unrepresented. It adds that only about a fifth of immigrants were represented when they were ordered deported in June.

The materials also suggest that lawyers themselves could face punishment if judges decide they are taking on too many cases. One slide said that where a practitioner takes on more cases than they can responsibly and professionally handle, an immigration judge may consider referral to EOIR disciplinary counsel for further action and possible sanction.

Faster courts, heavier pressure

The training comes against the backdrop of a backlog of roughly 3.2 million pending immigration court cases nationwide. The materials say the logic behind the approach was to cut inefficiencies and avoidable delays.

That pressure is taking place inside the Executive Office for Immigration Review, or EOIR, which houses the immigration courts. The EOIR is not part of the independent judicial branch; it sits within the Department of Justice, where the US attorney general can make precedential decisions and otherwise exercise control.

In response to the request for comment, a Justice Department spokesperson said the administration’s priority is reducing the immigration court backlog and said the department is “restoring integrity to our immigration system by hearing cases fairly, expeditiously, and uniformly, in accordance with the law.” An EOIR spokesperson said the agency prioritizes timely completion of cases and that judges are obligated to follow current immigration law, regulations, precedent decisions and EOIR policies so cases do not languish.

The training materials, however, suggest a much harder line: one that treats delay itself as a problem to be stamped out, even when more time might mean the difference between deportation and a lawful path to stay in the US.

The network investigators say they traced

The mission said the assistance moved along interconnected air, maritime and land routes involving Bosaso in Somalia’s Puntland region, southeastern Libya and Chad. The mission publicly named only people already designated by the UN Security Council’s Sudan sanctions committee, including retired Colombian colonel Alvaro Andres Quijano Becerra, based in Abu Dhabi, and his wife, Claudia Viviana Oliveros Forero, whom it said coordinated recruitment inside Colombia. The brain also became more responsive to rewards, with stronger activity in the ventromedial prefrontal cortex and the precuneus, regions involved in reward processing and in regulating the timing between heartbeats. The researchers said the change seemed to come from greater attention to possible rewards rather than from a weaker response to losses, because participants still reacted to losses in a similar way.

That matters because fast breathing and an elevated heart rate are often linked with rapid decision-making, while pressure can make people more cautious as they try to avoid losses, whether they are investing, dealing with a tense workplace situation or choosing a meal quickly. In this study, slower breathing and a calmer cardiovascular state appeared to push judgment in the other direction.

“Our decisions are rarely determined solely by external information,” Park said, describing the research question. “Rather, our judgment emerges from the interplay between cognitive processes and our current bodily state.” Lead author Wenhao Huang said the findings underscore “the transformative role of breath-based interventions” and suggest that breathing and cardiac dynamics can make the brain more receptive to rewards.

The findings were published in Neuron, and the researchers said the approach could be useful as a simple, inexpensive tool for self-regulation. They also said future studies will need to see whether the same effects appear in broader clinical populations, including people with overweight, and whether breath regulation could influence food-related decisions.

Scientists reveal the hidden instructions that build the human brain

What happened

Two studies published in *Cell* and *Science* have narrowed the focus on radial glia, the stem cells that help build the human cortex before birth, by showing that these cells answer both metabolic cues and physical signals from the developing brain.

Before birth, the human brain is assembled through an enormous series of cellular choices, and radial glia sit at the center of that process. They produce large numbers of the neurons and support cells that make up the cerebral cortex, the brain region involved in thought, memory and language. Aparna Bhaduri of UCLA said the cells are “really key to making us human,” but also sit at the center of many neurodevelopmental and neuropsychiatric disorders, as well as cancer.

Metabolism and contact both shape cell fate

The *Cell* study built a detailed map of metabolism in the developing human cortex using donated human tissue and brain organoids grown from stem cells. The team, a collaboration between Bhaduri’s lab and Heather Christofk’s lab, found that metabolism is not just background support for development: it can actively influence which kinds of cells are produced.

The researchers reported that radial glia depend heavily on the pentose phosphate pathway, a glucose-based process used by rapidly dividing cells. When they lowered available glucose or interfered with that pathway, the stem cells changed what they produced and began generating more inhibitory neurons and other cell types that normally appear later in development. Bhaduri said that was surprising because metabolism can “really control how stem cells make decisions.”

The second study, published in *Science* and led by Claudia Nguyen, looked at a different source of information: signals from the thalamus. Using human stem cell-derived brain assembloids, the researchers found that thalamic projections physically touch radial glia while the brain is still developing. That contact changed the stem cells’ behavior and caused them to produce more excitatory neurons, especially upper-layer neurons that are particularly expanded in the human brain.

The team linked that interaction to NRXN1, a gene known for helping neurons form connections and previously associated with autism spectrum disorder. In assembloids made from patient-derived cells carrying an NRXN1 mutation, altered thalamic signals shifted the balance between stem cells and the neurons they generated.

Together, the studies suggest that radial glia are shaped by more than one kind of instruction as the cortex takes form, and that those same developmental pathways may help researchers understand disease vulnerability and, potentially, similar stem-cell programs in brain cancer.
