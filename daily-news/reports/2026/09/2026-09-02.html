What happened

Iran and the US traded fresh strikes around the Strait of Hormuz and beyond on Sunday and Monday, while President Masoud Pezeshkian offered a conditional opening to Donald Trump, leaving the question of whether the fighting will ease or intensify unresolved.

The exchange followed a weekend in which, as The Guardian put it, the two sides fired on each other for the first time in a month. The US military said it struck rocket launchers on Larak Island in the strait of Hormuz on Sunday, in its first strikes on Iran since July, after it detected that the Iranians were planning to deploy fresh mines in the waterway.

Tehran responded by launching missiles at US sites in Jordan, which were intercepted, and at the United Arab Emirates, which said it had stopped an Iranian drone over its territorial waters. On Monday, Donald Trump threatened further action, telling Fox News: “We’re going to hit them hard. There will be a response.” The strikes 24 hours later were presented as that response.

The Strait of Hormuz remains the center of the fight

The immediate military clash is tied to the Strait of Hormuz, a waterway that before the war accounted for a fifth of the world’s seaborne energy supply. Washington claimed Iranian control over the route had been loosened by repeated US attacks on Iranian missile launchers and radars, while Trump last week said the US navy had cleared mines from the strait for the first time since the conflict started on 28 February. Iran rejected that claim as lies.

Iranian state TV gave its own account of how the fighting escalated on Sunday, saying a tanker caught fire after hitting two Iranian naval mines while trying to pass through the southern side of the strait close to Oman. Officials later said Iran had targeted three ships trying to use the southern route, which Tehran says is unauthorised. The Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps warned that other ships violating its rules would face the same fate and said compliance with its regulations for passage through the strait was mandatory.

Pezeshkian offers a conditional opening

Against that backdrop, Pezeshkian tried to signal restraint. Middle East Eye said he offered an olive branch to Trump even after several soldiers and civilians were killed in a US strike on Larak Island. Speaking on Monday before travelling to Bishkek for a Shanghai Cooperation Organisation summit, Pezeshkian said Iran was “not looking for war” but would “never sit still in the face of any aggression”.

He also drew a line around any future response. According to the Fars news agency, he said Iran would give a decisive response if attacked again. At the same time, state media reported that he said Iran would immediately reciprocate if the US fulfilled its commitments under an interim deal signed in June. The Iran presidency’s website quoted him saying: “I state explicitly that if the United States returns to its commitments under the aforementioned memorandum of understanding, the Islamic Republic of Iran will immediately take reciprocal action.”

Diplomatic pressure and economic strain

Iran also turned to the UN after the strike. In a letter to UN Secretary-General Antonio Guterres, Tehran’s deputy permanent representative and ambassador to the UN, Gholamhossein Darzi, urged the Security Council to demand that Washington end its attacks. He described the strikes as “a clear violation of the sovereignty and territorial integrity” of Iran and accused the US and others involved of war crimes and crimes against humanity in the war on Iran. He also said Iran’s forces “would not hesitate” to respond to any further strikes.

The wider cost of the war is already showing up in shipping data. Middle East Eye said the number of vessels sailing through the Strait of Hormuz changed little on Monday, with preliminary Kpler tracking showing five commodity vessels passing through: four entered the waterway and one exited. One of the vessels that entered was an empty handy-sized gas tanker travelling via the Iranian route, while the other three were laden dry bulk carriers.

MLO defends the autopsy

Medico-legal officer Dr Osama Sheikh, who conducted the first autopsy, told the commission he had not been under any influence or pressure and had not mentioned suicide in his report. He also said that after completing his MBBS in 2016, he was appointed through the Sindh Public Service Commission in 2019, joined the police surgeon’s office in 2021 and was posted as an MLO at Jinnah Postgraduate Medical Centre.

Responding to questions, Dr Sheikh said he had conducted around 100 post-mortems so far and added that a sweeper used to be present during such procedures. Justice Sial asked why burn marks on the deceased’s hands and other parts of the body, as well as fractures, had not been mentioned in the report. Dr Sheikh replied that there were no burn marks and that the change in skin colour was due to decomposition, and he said he had found no visible fractures.

The commission, however, expressed serious displeasure over what it saw as unsatisfactory answers. It questioned how the body had decomposed so quickly and why the MLO had been able to identify only a bullet wound during the procedure. Justice Sial also asked whether the autopsy had been conducted by the sweeper and pointed out that the final report of the first autopsy differed from the initial report and that the handwriting in the two reports did not match.

Dr Sheikh maintained that he had conducted the autopsy and acknowledged that the handwriting in both reports was his. He said the final report of the first post-mortem was issued after the body had been exhumed and a second autopsy had been conducted, and after the police surgeon had raised 14 objections to the first post-mortem.

A case shaped by competing versions

The hearing also brought in more details from people linked to the recovery of the body and the police response. Nursery employees Yasir and Qaiser said they had informed police after spotting the body, called the police helpline 15, and saw two policemen arrive on a motorcycle about 15 minutes later, followed by a police van. They said police recorded their statements under Section 161 of the CrPC three days later and that officers used to be present at the nursery’s main entrance for around 20 days a month.

Two Edhi Foundation volunteers, Mohsin and Javed, told the commission they reached the site at around 1pm after being informed by their head office. They said the body was lying face down and that when they turned it over they noticed a bullet wound.

Police officials also testified. The case has been marked by disagreement from the start. The background to the commission included a Karachi court order on Aug 6 allowing exhumation and a second autopsy, followed by changes to the medical board, objections from the family, the restoration of the original board and the body’s exhumation on Aug 8. The next day, the investigating team was reconstituted and a murder charge was added to the FIR.

On Aug 19, Ali’s parents wrote to Chief Minister Murad Ali Shah seeking his direct supervision of the murder investigation and asking for an inquiry into the previous team. The commission has now summoned more people, including the victim’s business partner, friends and police officials, to appear before it on Sept 3.

What happened

Nelson Mandela Bay's water and sanitation crisis has deepened into a major repair and funding problem, with leaks, outages and sewage spills affecting residents, rivers and beaches while the metro, civil society and human rights officials point to a large bill and no quick fix.

The metro is facing up to 1,000 water-related complaints a week, a backlog of more than 5,000 leak complaints and a network in which 4,700km of pipeline needs replacement. Officials have put the cost of fixing leaks at R426-million and the pipe replacement programme at another R495-million as they try to curb water losses that run up to 60%.

Water outages have also become more common, with some residents left without water for weeks. On the sanitation side, malfunctioning pump stations and wastewater treatment plants are sending raw sewage into beaches, estuaries and riverine areas. The crisis drew scrutiny from the South African Human Rights Commission in July, after an investigation report described it as a material public-health risk with human-rights implications.

A repair bill measured in hundreds of millions

The size of the problem is not limited to a few broken pipes. The city’s water boss, Barry Martin, said stopping the 60% water loss would require R426-million for leak repairs and R495-million for a pipe replacement programme. The metro’s political head for infrastructure and engineering, Buyelwa Mafaya, said the leaks and water losses needed urgent attention and that contractors had been appointed to work across six clusters.

The city is also dealing with vandalism and theft that undermine the system. According to the evidence, criminals steal electrical cables, which affects pumping. Bloom, during an oversight visit to the Nooitgedagt and Groendal water treatment plants, said huge volumes of treated water were being lost through leaks and raised concerns about sewage seeping into rivers.

Sewage spills, failing plants and polluted waterways

The Nelson Mandela Bay Civil Society Coalition released its CSC Metro Rough Guide on 25 August, and the metro’s wastewater system is failing. It describes continual spills polluting the environment and threatening public health, livelihoods, natural ecosystems, the local economy and what it calls the environmental inheritance of future generations.

The report’s description is blunt: in townships, it says, people see stepping-stones across algae-infested sewage waters streaming down roads, homes and factories flooded, stormwater drains dumping sewage into rivers and across beaches, and air that continually smells of sewage. It also says the Driftsands Wastewater Treatment Works, once considered the metro’s “gold standard” plant, has been largely non-operational for three to four years after thieves stole its underground electricity supply cable. Internal cables serving plant equipment were also stolen, despite 24-hour security.

As a result, raw sewage flows through a 3km sand canal and is discharged into the sea from rocks between the Willows and Willow Grove resorts. Additionally, the Cape Recife plant, which previously discharged raw sewage into the sea, has improved after recent upgrades, but its settlement ponds are now threatened by an advancing sand corridor. A water-recycling project meant to supply one megalitre a day to Pine Lodge, Nelson Mandela University and Humewood Golf Course is also inactive because of failures at the plant and in the conveyance system.

The city’s largest pump station, the Creek, receives wastewater from Summerstrand, Humewood, Central, Korsten and Westering. It has been hit by regular cable theft, and a recently installed perimeter fence is repeatedly damaged and breached by traffic failing to make the turn on the highway.

Pressure for fixes and a new water source

The response now includes both repairs and broader questions about where future water will come from. In August, municipal spokesperson Sithembiso Soyaya said oversight visits were being planned and that the media would be informed once they were completed. Bloom suggested that coastal municipalities should consider desalination at scale as an alternative source of water, saying the city needed “a new source of water” and that lack of water was “a job destroyer”.

Martin said his two-year-old proposal for the city to look into desalination had been shot down. Mafaya said the National Treasury had not supported the proposal when it was submitted, but she welcomed Bloom’s mention of desalination. She said the municipality was addressing water issues on the R75 and Mission Road, where it would draw water from the Orange River to Nooitgedagt, but added that the budget remained a major obstacle to doing the work needed.
