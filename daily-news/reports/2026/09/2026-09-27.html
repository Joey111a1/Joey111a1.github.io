What happened

In Spain, older tenants facing eviction are fighting to remain in homes that hold decades of family and community life. Their cases illustrate a housing crisis that continues despite evictions reaching historical lows, with rent arrears, investment funds and rising property values placing households under pressure.

Spain recorded 67 evictions a day, with Catalonia accounting for 18 daily cases, according to the figures cited in the account. That rate is three times Madrid’s and nearly twice the eight daily evictions recorded in both Andalusia and the Valencian Community. Seven out of every 10 evictions involve unpaid rent, while the remainder concern unpaid mortgages.

The General Council of the Judiciary is the only source of eviction data cited, and its court records do not specify the ages of those affected. Many proceedings take place without activists, witnesses or public attention. In numerous cases, the court secretary and locksmith enter after the occupants have already left.

The cases of Mariví Abecia, Rosario Castelló and María Jesús Plaza show how older tenants experience that pressure. They have followed the eviction struggle of Maricarmen, an elderly woman in Madrid whose case has become a reference point for others facing the loss of their homes.

Mariví’s fight in Erandio

Mariví Abecia, 78, lives with her husband, who is six years older, in the Los Ochos urbanization in Erandio, a Biscayan town near Bilbao. She pays just under €1,000 a month for their home, but her rental contract expires this week. Mosaic, the investment fund that owns the urbanization, has sent her a notice ordering her to leave or face eviction.

Mariví has packed her suitcases and placed her belongings in boxes, while insisting that she intends to resist. “When they knock on the door, I don’t know how I will react,” she says. “I hope no one throws us out of here.”

She is one of 120 families trying to remain in the development. The fund bought 135 apartments there last year from La Caixa’s real-estate portfolio. According to the account, Mosaic now wants to empty the properties and sell them for substantially more than it paid. Neighbors say the fund paid about €70,000 per apartment and is now asking as much as €340,000 in some cases.

Fourteen families face the possibility of losing their homes because their contracts expire before December 31. Joseba, who lives there with his six-year-old daughter, has a contract lasting until 2029, but says that offers little reassurance because residents believe the evictions will come “one after another, in a constant drip.” He pays €1,032 a month for a 90-square-metre home, compared with €800 when he began renting it 15 years ago.

A home in Barcelona after 28 years

In Barcelona, Rosario Castelló, 64, has spent four years fighting to stay in the apartment where she has lived for 28 years. She began the week by learning that another neighbor in L’Eixample had been evicted, then watched Maricarmen’s eviction on television. Rosario says the process has been damaging to her mental health.

Rosario’s apartment was once part of a building owned by a private individual who left the estate to the Sagrada Família. She says the property was later sold to Renta Corporación and then to Vandor. Although her contract had not expired when Vandor acquired the building, she says the company told her she would have to leave.

She is now the last remaining neighbor in the building. The other apartments have been converted into rooms rented to foreign students, many of whom stay for only two or three months. Rosario pays €1,040 a month, an amount she says falls within Barcelona’s regulated rental band, but argues that the company can earn more by renting the apartments by the room.

Rosario, who lives alone, says she has maintained and carefully decorated the home where she raised her children and once operated a shop. She has support from the Renters’ Union and continues to challenge the eviction. Her case is now before the Provincial Court. She says that appearing before a judge made her feel treated “like a criminal,” and that the uncertainty continues to wear her down.

Lower totals, lasting upheaval

María Jesús Plaza, 60, represents another version of the same struggle in Valencia. Her legal battle began after she returned to her apartment in the city’s old town and found her belongings thrown into the street through the stairwell opening. The home had been vandalized. The supplied account ends while describing her case, without stating its outcome.

Evictions are now at historical lows and have fallen almost 36% since 2022. That remains far below the worst period between 2013 and 2016, when 187 families were evicted each day. In 2025, courts carried out 25,540 evictions: 6,814 in Catalonia, 3,782 in Andalusia, 2,992 in the Valencian Community and 2,267 in Madrid.

The lower figures do not remove the disruption for those affected. For older people especially, losing a long-term home can mean rebuilding daily life in a place that feels unfamiliar. Activist groups such as PAH and the Renters’ Union sometimes accompany elderly tenants, but most evictions occur quietly, without witnesses.

Why Pakistan’s endless war on militants fails to break the cycle
global_south_and_exploitedpolitics_and_powermaterial_harmanchor
What happened

For more than two decades, Pakistan’s military campaigns against Taliban and Baloch insurgents have produced repeated tactical victories but failed to end violence in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa and Balochistan, leaving soldiers, police and civilians exposed while cross-border tensions deepen.

Pakistan’s western provinces remain trapped in near-daily militant violence despite successive counterterrorism operations. In northwestern Khyber Pakhtunkhwa and southwestern Balochistan, scores of soldiers, police officers and civilians are killed each month, while attacks target security personnel, government officials and other prominent figures. Militants use roadside bombs, explosive-laden drones, ambushes, suicide attacks, raids, assassinations and kidnappings against patrols, checkpoints, police stations and administrative centres.

The military routinely presents its operations as successful, announcing that hundreds of perpetrators have been eliminated each month. Its statements often list militants “sent to hell”, with only a small number surviving for interrogation. But analysts say these battlefield results have not produced the strategic outcome Islamabad has sought: a durable reduction in insurgent activity or a break in the cycle of violence.

Cross-border strikes deepen the cycle

The latest escalation has centred on the porous Pakistan-Afghanistan border, which armed groups have used for sanctuary, smuggling and logistical support. Following months of heightened attacks, Pakistan launched air strikes against what its information ministry described as Tehreek-i-Taliban Pakistan (TTP) “terrorist camps and safe havens” in Afghanistan’s Paktika and Kunar provinces. The ministry said 28 militants were killed, including suicide bombers in training.

The strikes followed a September 18 assault on a mosque at a police compound in Kohat, in northwestern Pakistan, which left at least 31 people dead and more than 100 injured. Afghanistan’s Taliban government has repeatedly denied that terrorist camps exist on its territory. Its chief spokesman, Zabihullah Mujahid, said the Pakistani strikes killed three civilians and vowed revenge.

The confrontation then expanded. Security officials said eight “rudimentary” drones were launched from Afghan territory into Pakistani airspace, with all of them shot down, including four near Kohat. Mujahid denied responsibility. Pakistani officials nevertheless regarded the incursions as evidence that Kabul was actively supporting the TTP rather than merely tolerating its presence. Pakistan subsequently announced “precision aerial and drone strikes” on 10 locations in Afghanistan that it said were used to launch and store the drones.

The attacks have taken place alongside unsuccessful diplomatic efforts involving China, Turkey and Qatar. At a meeting in Belarus during the summer, officials reportedly considered a joint mechanism to monitor the cross-border movement of TTP militants. Islamabad-based security analyst Iftikhar Firdous said the talks failed to produce a breakthrough because Kabul would not compel the TTP to stop attacking Pakistan or move its fighters away from the border.

Qatar’s National Security Council deputy secretary general Hamad Khamis al-Kubaisi and Turkish intelligence chief Ibrahim Kalın also visited Kabul in an attempt to renew discussions between Afghan and Pakistani officials. Pakistani officials have said the Taliban government was only pretending to engage with those efforts.

Tactical gains without a strategic answer

Analysts describe Pakistan’s approach as largely reactive: militants attack, security forces conduct intelligence-based operations or cross-border strikes, and insurgents respond with further violence. Riccardo Valle, a researcher tracking militancy in South and Central Asia, called the strategy “incomplete and limited”.

He said local grievances and poor governance weakened the impact of military operations in violence-hit areas. Local security forces, particularly police in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa and Balochistan, were ill-equipped to confront “full-fledged insurgencies”, while reactive operations did not address the conditions that allow militant groups to regenerate.

“Thus, tactical success cannot compensate for a broader strategic deficiency, which fuels a circle of instability” in the affected provinces, Valle said. He described the twin insurgencies as a “multigenerational issue” that continues to draw in new cohorts of young militants. A broader response, he argued, would have to address both Pakistan’s domestic problems and the conflict’s international dimensions.

Farwa Aamer, director of South Asia initiatives at the Asia Society Policy Institute, similarly said force alone could not resolve Pakistan’s security problems over the long term. “Force will continue to meet force, keeping the loop alive,” she said, arguing that a political solution and dialogue were necessary. Such a solution would take time, she said, but without it—or a viable alternative strategy—continued instability could deter foreign investment, including in the critical minerals sector.

Two insurgencies, overlapping risks

The security picture has also become more complicated in Balochistan, where signs have emerged of increased tactical cooperation between the Islamist TTP and ethno-nationalist Baloch separatist groups. The province recorded a marked rise in attacks over the summer. In July, the TTP and the Balochistan Liberation Army carried out separate attacks in Pishin and Ziarat districts within hours of each other.

South Asia security researcher Muhammad Faisal said reports of joint training and logistical links showed that coordination was increasingly documented. He doubted, however, that the groups would form a durable strategic coalition. The TTP seeks to overthrow Pakistan’s government and establish an Islamist political order, while Baloch groups are primarily concerned with independence. Their different ideologies, aims, languages, cultures and areas of operation may limit cooperation to shared tactics and targets.

Pakistan’s regional relationships add further pressure. Relations with Iran improved after the two countries exchanged strikes against militants based on each other’s territory in January 2024, but residual mistrust and wider regional conflict continue to complicate cooperation. Islamabad has also accused India of covertly supporting Baloch separatists as proxies. Faisal said Pakistan would be better served by closely monitoring separatist networks than by treating every attack as proof of foreign backing.

The more immediate risk, he said, was that conflict involving Iran could create a power vacuum exploited by militants or foreign intelligence operatives. That danger, he argued, would be best managed through intelligence cooperation and sustained diplomacy rather than another cycle of retaliatory attacks.

What happened

Digital tools, renewable energy and ecological restoration are changing how herders in Inner Mongolia manage cattle, pasture and desertified land, with potential gains for animal health, household incomes, electricity reliability and the productivity of northern China’s grasslands.

In Horqin Left Middle Banner, Tongliao, ethnic Mongolian herder Zhang Weijian manages more than 30 cattle through a smartphone mini-program. Electronic ear tags monitor each animal’s activity and body temperature; when a reading exceeds 40 degrees Celsius, Zhang can identify a possible problem and check the animal. The platform also stores breeding, calving and vaccination records. “Before, we kept everything on paper,” Zhang said.

Zhang is among the farmers served by a national modern agricultural industrial park established in 2019. One of four such parks in China focused on beef cattle, it has collected traceability information for more than 90,000 cattle—over 90 percent of the animals in the park. Its stated aim is to follow cattle through the production chain and make livestock production more standardized and digitalized. Zhang said better herd management helps improve cattle quality, reduce losses and increase income for families that have long depended on cattle raising.

Technology is also being applied to the land that supports those livelihoods. In Horqin Right Front Banner, Hinggan League, herder Sainbayar uses three quadcopter drones in rotation to fertilize pasture along preset routes from June to July. He said higher pasture yields have provided a steadier supply of feed, while mixers, silage machines and feed conveyors have reduced manual labor.

Infrastructure improvements are addressing another constraint. A wind-solar-storage microgrid operating in a village in Chen Barag Banner, Hulunbuir, supplies more reliable electricity to 122 herder households, where distant grid connections can experience voltage fluctuations and outages during severe weather. Liu Xuebin, who manages more than 200 cattle and sheep, said the stable supply allows his family to operate livestock equipment throughout the day. Inner Mongolia’s April 2025 implementation plan sets targets for big-data services, smart farms and wider agricultural digitalization through 2027.

Restoring land while creating income

In Horqin Right Middle Banner, Hinggan League, desertification-control efforts combine fencing, irrigation, replanting and new industries. A 4,600-mu yellowhorn base has planted 308,200 trees, while researchers are exploring oil, tea and medicinal products from the species to extend its industrial value.

A photovoltaic project at another site combines solar generation with grassland restoration and livestock farming. Its 300-megawatt capacity is expected to produce about 530 million kilowatt-hours annually, equivalent to saving 159,000 tonnes of standard coal. Residents participate through employment, land leasing and seasonal grass harvesting. Villager Li Jincai said he earns 200 yuan a day from restoration work.

From 2021 to 2025, China treated 152 million mu of desertified land, according to the National Forestry and Grassland Administration. Across areas covered by the Three-North Shelterbelt Forest Program, forestry and grassland industries generated 800 billion yuan in output in 2025, while more than 1.5 million farmers and herders received 10.24 billion yuan for participating in related programs.

What happened

Andy Burnham has announced “Your First Home”, a proposed scheme for first-time buyers in England who can afford mortgage payments but cannot save a large deposit or rely on family support. It would combine a 2.5% deposit with a 20% equity loan on a new-build home.

The prime minister unveiled the plan on the eve of the Labour Party conference in Liverpool, presenting it as a response to the difficulty many young people face in buying a home. Burnham said too many people were struggling with housing costs and losing hope of owning a home, particularly those without access to the “bank of mum and dad”.

Under the proposal, eligible first-time buyers would provide a minimum deposit of 2.5% and receive a loan worth 20% of the value of a new-build property. The equity loan would initially be interest-free. The Guardian reported that the scheme would be aimed at households with regular incomes that have been unable to accumulate a large deposit, rather than those with the highest salaries or substantial savings.

A targeted replacement for earlier help-to-buy schemes

The programme is intended to be more tightly targeted than previous help-to-buy initiatives. Household-income and deposit limits are expected to exclude people with the greatest financial capacity, while price caps would restrict purchases to more modest homes. Ministers have not yet confirmed whether there will also be an age restriction or exactly how any property-value cap will work.

More detail is expected in next month’s Budget. The Guardian reported that Housing Secretary John Healey would formally announce the programme there and that registration could open by the end of 2026.

The proposal resembles the coalition government’s Help to Buy programme, introduced in 2013. That scheme initially allowed purchasers to buy new-build homes with a 5% deposit and a shared-equity loan worth up to 20% of the property’s value, for homes priced up to £600,000. The loan was interest-free for its first five years. A later version, which ran until 2023, was restricted to first-time buyers and used regional price caps.

More than 387,000 properties were bought through Help to Buy, including 328,000 purchased by first-time buyers, according to an evaluation cited by the BBC. The evaluation found that it had helped some people into home ownership but had not removed affordability barriers in areas where prices were already high. The scheme was also criticised for assisting buyers who might have been able to purchase without government support and for contributing to higher house prices.

Burnham said “Your First Home” would incorporate lessons from those programmes while preserving their benefits. He also argued that the policy would give housebuilders greater confidence to deliver new homes.

Funding and the argument over prices

Funding is expected to come from reprioritising existing government budgets, although it was not immediately clear whether that would reduce money already allocated to housebuilding or require savings elsewhere. Developers would pay a fee linked to property values to participate, contributing to the scheme’s running costs.

The Conservatives opposed the proposal, arguing that it would make buyers take on more debt rather than address the shortage of affordable homes. Katie Lam, the Conservative shadow housing secretary, said taxpayers would acquire a stake in buyers’ homes and could be left paying the bill. She also argued that placing another levy on developers risked pushing up the price of new homes.

Burnham rejected the contention that the scheme would simply increase prices by stimulating demand. He said the government would also work to increase housing supply, which he argued would reduce the risk of prices being pushed higher. The Home Builders Federation welcomed the plan, saying a well-designed scheme could help households struggling to buy.

The supply question remains central because the government has a target of building 1.5 million homes in England by the next election. Housing Secretary Angela Rayner has said there is only a “slim chance” of meeting that target, although she has not abandoned it. Data cited by the BBC suggested construction was running below the pace required, with the decline continuing a trend that began under the previous Conservative government.

The scheme therefore combines help with deposits and borrowing with an unresolved question about whether enough homes will be built to meet demand. Its eligibility rules, funding arrangements and launch timetable are expected to be clarified in the Budget.
