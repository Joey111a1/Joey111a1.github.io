Africa faces tens of thousands of additional heat deaths as El Niño intensifies

What happened

A Climate Impact Lab analysis projects tens of thousands of additional heat-related deaths across Africa as an intensifying El Niño brings unusually high temperatures, with Nigeria, Sudan, Niger, the Democratic Republic of Congo and Chad facing some of the largest estimated increases over the coming months.

The University of Chicago-based researchers project that a “Super El Niño” could produce 66,800 additional heat-related deaths across Africa’s Sahel over the next six months. Nearly half of that projected toll would occur in Nigeria, according to the analysis.

For the period from September to February, Nigeria is estimated to record 31,400 more heat-related deaths than in a normal year. Sudan follows with 17,500, while Niger and the Democratic Republic of Congo are each projected to see about 10,000 additional deaths. Chad’s projected increase is 7,900.

The figures are estimates of excess deaths compared with a normal year, not predictions of the exact number of people who will die. They are intended to show where the effects of extreme heat could be greatest and where interventions might be targeted before deaths occur.

A global heat burden concentrated in the Global South

The lab projects 239,000 additional heat-related deaths globally between September and February, with an uncertainty range of about 11,000 above or below that estimate. Across the first nine months of the current El Niño, from June 2026 to February 2027, the projected global total rises to 451,000.

The analysis says the largest increases will be spread across countries in the Global South. In South-East Asia, it projects 19,400 additional deaths across the Philippines, Vietnam, Thailand and Cambodia, and 19,300 in Indonesia. India is projected to record 15,800 additional deaths and Brazil 13,300.

South Africa is among the 50 countries with the highest projected number of additional heat-related deaths between September and February, with about 1,200 estimated deaths above the normal-year level.

The timing varies by country. In Nigeria, about 15,400 of the projected additional deaths are expected between December and February. Indonesia is projected to experience about 10,400 additional deaths during the same period.

Forecast temperatures could resemble a warmer future

Global land temperatures are expected to be about 1.2°C above normal in the coming months, producing 44% more extremely hot days than would occur in a normal year. The researchers said these conditions would resemble temperatures that climate change was projected to deliver about 20 years from now.

“El Niño is effectively pushing us into the future,” the researchers said, arguing that the event is exposing populations to temperatures that are not expected to become normal for another two decades, before communities have had time to adapt and protect themselves.

The Climate Impact Lab also said the heat-related deaths would come alongside droughts, wildfires and flooding associated with hotter temperatures and shifting rainfall patterns. It warned that El Niño could become the strongest on record.

The projections were produced by examining the relationship between temperature and excess mortality across 24,378 regions worldwide, accounting for differences in local climate, vulnerability and adaptive capacity. Those relationships were then applied to seasonal temperature forecasts and compared with the 1996–2025 average. The researchers said uncertainty arises both from the seasonal forecasts and from estimates of the temperature–mortality relationship.

Forecasts point to preventive action

Current forecasts suggest El Niño could remain in place through spring 2027. The report said that, if it does, the projected figures could rise further, with higher-than-average temperatures and heat-related deaths potentially continuing into June and July.

The lab identified heat warnings, outreach to vulnerable people, cooling centres, hydration stations, protections for workers exposed to heat and expanded health-system capacity as measures that could reduce the toll. Michael Greenstone, a Climate Impact Lab co-founder and University of Chicago economics professor, said the projections could help decision-makers identify where emergency action might save tens of thousands of lives.

Fighting has returned to Ethiopia’s Tigray region nearly four years after a peace agreement ended a war that killed an estimated 600,000 people, with Tigrayan forces seizing three airports and former battlefield enemies now cooperating against Prime Minister Abiy Ahmed’s government.

Both sides also accused each other of starting the earlier clashes. ACLED said federal drone strikes and Tigrayan troop deployments followed, with Tigrayan forces moving tanks and artillery south this month.

How the 2022 agreement unraveled

The African Union-brokered Pretoria agreement, signed on November 2, 2022, required Tigrayan forces to disarm and federal authority to return to the region. It also envisaged the restoration of humanitarian aid and basic services. But disputes over territory claimed by Tigray and Amhara, the return of displaced people and the status of Eritrean forces—who fought alongside Ethiopia’s federal army but were not parties to the deal—remained unresolved.

The TPLF’s internal power struggle further weakened the process: a faction led by Debretsion Gebremichael ousted Reda, and Gebremichael’s faction took back the regional government in May after Abiy appointed a new interim leader.

Reuters reported that Tigrayan forces were fighting alongside Fano in Amhara and a local armed group in Afar.

The alliance’s durability is uncertain. Getahun described it as a temporary arrangement against a common enemy, noting that the TPLF and Fano still disagree over Western Tigray. Abiy’s spokesperson said the groups had already been working together with financial backing from Eritrea, whose government has repeatedly denied supporting Ethiopian armed groups.

Tigray is still recovering from the previous war, with many displaced people unable to return. Ethiopia is also fighting in Amhara and Oromia, while ACLED says its federal military was already stretched.

What happened

A preprint introduces WROP, a benchmark and training resource designed to test whether video-generation models can preserve hidden objects, respect solid barriers and predict physically consistent motion. Its authors report that a 16-billion-parameter model fine-tuned on the data ranked first among continuation systems, but differences in model architecture and interface limit what the result establishes.

The researchers frame object permanence and solidity as basic physical expectations: an object should continue to exist when hidden, remain associated with a container that moves, and not pass through an impenetrable surface. They ask whether current video-generation systems possess these capabilities or whether they can be trained using tasks inspired by cognitive science.

A benchmark built around physical structure

WROP—short for World Reasoning with Object Permanence—contains 150 hand-designed Blender generators organized into six task families. Three test object permanence: dynamic occlusion, static-scene occlusion and container-based concealment or movement. Three test object solidity: obstruction, support removal or dropping, and collision.

The generators vary nuisance features such as lighting, camera angle, speed, object appearance and geometry while preserving the underlying physical problem. This is intended to prevent a model from succeeding through memorized visual cues rather than tracking the relevant event. Examples include an object passing behind an opaque barrier and reappearing with its identity and motion intact; a concealed object moving with its container; an object being stopped by a barrier whose opening is too small; and a collision in which the number and identity of objects must remain consistent.

The resulting corpus contains 1.5 million samples, with more than 10,000 samples per generator. Each sample is a 120-frame physically consistent animation split into a 60-frame input and a 60-frame target, accompanied by a prompt, object trajectories and scene-state metadata. The authors also release a fixed 300-question exam, model answers, scores, weights and their PWM native-PyTorch training stack for AWS Trainium2.

The exam evaluates 14 video models across three interface classes: four continuation models, three reference-to-video systems and seven edit or transfer models. Continuation systems extend a source video beyond its final frame. Reference-to-video systems regenerate the event from a visual reference, while edit and transfer systems modify or repaint the source interval. The distinction matters because these systems face different temporal demands, particularly when the correct event occurs after an object disappears behind an occluder.

PWM-WROP leads continuation models

The researchers fine-tuned PWM-WROP, an open-weight 16-billion-parameter continuation model, on the WROP training corpus. Its architecture and tokenizer remain those of the base model; the reported intervention is the WROP training signal.

In a blind pairwise study, 20 raters made 361 completed comparisons among the 14 models. Judgments considered alignment with the description, natural motion, physical plausibility and object permanence, including whether objects vanished, appeared spontaneously, passed through barriers or changed identity, shape, colour or count. On the resulting Bradley–Terry Elo scale, PWM-WROP scored 1679.5 and ranked third overall. It ranked first among continuation models, ahead of the other continuation systems evaluated.

Two reference-to-video models, Wan 3.0 Prime and MiniMax H3, shared the top overall Elo score of 1723.6, described by the authors as a statistical tie. PWM-WROP’s result was obtained at a native output resolution of 320 × 192, lower than the native resolutions of the competing systems; all outputs were resized to that resolution for the human comparisons. Against reference videos, the model also recorded the best LPIPS and MS-SSIM among the evaluated systems at matched resolution, although the paper cautions that these metrics measure visual similarity rather than object-permanence or solidity reasoning directly.

Performance varied by task family. PWM-WROP ranked first in static object occlusion and second in object drop, but fifth in obstruction and eighth in collision. The authors report that some systems tracked hidden objects effectively yet struggled to predict the consequences of barriers or collisions. A recurring failure involved a ball passing through a barrier without accounting for whether its size matched the opening.

What the result does—and does not—show

The authors interpret the findings as preliminary evidence that concept-specific synthetic data can improve physical reasoning in video models. They argue that WROP offers a way to evaluate these abilities with hand-authored physical ground truth and human judgments rather than relying only on multimodal language-model evaluators, which they say may themselves have weaknesses on intuitive physics.

However, the comparison does not isolate training as the sole cause of PWM-WROP’s ranking. The evaluated systems differ in architecture, interface class, output resolution and other properties, and the paper explicitly notes that the performance gap cannot be attributed to training alone. The benchmark and results are presented in an arXiv preprint and have not been peer reviewed.

science_cs_aipolitics_and_powertechnology_and_aieconomy_and_businessscience anchorpreprint_not_peer_reviewed
What happened

AI agents increasingly combine untrusted web pages, repositories, tool outputs and messages with privileged instructions, persistent memory and powerful tools. A research paper from the DeepKernel Lab proposes AgentKernel, an operating-system-style security layer intended to control those trust crossings across identity, perception, memory and execution.

The paper, posted to arXiv on 29 August 2026, argues that current agent security is mainly provided by application-level middleware: orchestration frameworks, runtimes, governance platforms and execution sandboxes that operate alongside the agent. In the authors’ assessment, these components often remain in a shared process trust boundary with the agent’s business logic. Identity checks, input filters, memory stores and tool controls can therefore be fragmented, with a weakness or bypass in one component undermining assumptions made elsewhere.

AgentKernel is presented as a mandatory security substrate beneath existing agent applications, rather than as another orchestration framework. Its central premise is that security should be built into the operating environment and enforced outside the manipulable language-model context. The proposed layer would mediate how an agent receives information, forms and retains beliefs, delegates authority and produces external effects.

Four pillars for the agent lifecycle

The architecture is organized around four pillars: Identity, Perception, Cognition and Execution. The paper adapts classical operating-system ideas such as reference monitors, least privilege, mandatory access control and defense in depth to the semantic operations of autonomous agents.

The Identity pillar uses a Global Agent Registry and an Agent Identity Card, or AIC. The proposed credential would cryptographically bind the developer, code artifact, operator and deployment context, with Ed25519 signatures used in the identity architecture. AgentKernel would use these credentials for mutual authentication, delegation and capability restrictions. When several skills are combined, their permission envelopes would be intersected with the agent’s maximum capability set, meaning that composition could narrow permissions but not expand them.

Perception is designed as a four-layer input defense. It would begin with source tagging and deterministic rule filtering, then apply semantic filtering and jailbreak detection. The stated aim is to stop web content, files, tool outputs and agent-to-agent messages from entering the model’s context as undifferentiated text. Their origin, trust level and warnings would remain available to later controls.

The Cognition pillar treats persistent memory as a labeled and auditable information-flow system. AgentKernel would divide conversations into provenance-aware segments, identify the material supporting candidate memories and assign security labels through a lattice-based model. The authors argue that this could preserve useful retrieval while limiting the effect of poisoned memory entries on later sessions. The design seeks minimal labels that preserve an item’s validity under a stated utility constraint, rather than assigning every memory the most restrictive possible classification.

Execution connects an agent’s stated intent to the operations a tool actually performs. Before a tool runs, a privileged guardian daemon would calculate an allowlist for resources such as file paths, network endpoints and DNS queries. The proposed system would install those restrictions through eBPF probes or other system hooks, monitor the entire process tree and pause processes that violate the policy. Permissions from multiple skills would again be intersected rather than combined by union.

A proposed kernel beneath agent harnesses

The integration model provides three narrow interfaces for the LLM, tools and storage. The paper argues that the agent and its orchestration fabric should have no parallel access to the same model endpoints, tool runtimes or data stores. This arrangement is intended to make policy-relevant traffic pass through a single auditable boundary.

AgentKernel combines semantic checks of tool calls, memory access and plans with kernel-level syscall enforcement. In the proposed two-stage execution process, semantic mediation would evaluate an action using identity, input provenance, memory taint and declared permissions. The resulting allowlist would then be installed at the system level before the tool’s process tree is started. The authors’ argument is that policy should not merely describe what an agent may do; the underlying system should constrain what the resulting processes can actually execute.

The paper places AgentKernel between agent applications and the traditional operating-system kernel. Commercial coding assistants, open-source harnesses, orchestration libraries, governance systems and execution sandboxes are described as complementary layers. The authors contend that these systems address parts of the agent lifecycle but do not, in their assessment, provide one integrated and non-bypassable boundary spanning identity, perception, cognition and execution.

They describe structural security as a “capability multiplier.” If operators can rely on cryptographic identity, provenance tracking, memory taint and execution controls, the paper argues, they may be able to grant agents broader privileges without relying only on restrictive application policies. This is the architecture’s stated intended effect, not a demonstrated deployment result.

Limits and unresolved engineering questions

The paper presents AgentKernel as a research architecture and security argument. It identifies residual risks involving adversarial memory segmentation, the accuracy of the semantic firewall, centralization of the Global Agent Registry, the assumption that the mediation boundary cannot be bypassed, eBPF portability and the lack of full formal verification. It also acknowledges that an overly permissive or misconfigured policy can still grant excessive access.

The proposal therefore does not replace existing runtimes, governance tools or sandboxes. Instead, it argues that those systems could operate above a mandatory kernel layer coordinating identity, provenance, memory and execution controls across the agent lifecycle. Whether that non-bypassable model can be implemented across different deployment environments and validated formally remains part of the research agenda described by the authors.

What happened

Researchers have demonstrated a universal set of quantum operations using non-Abelian anyons, unusual collective states created from entangled qubits. The result could help quantum computers run arbitrary algorithms while reducing reliance on resource-intensive error-correction procedures, although the experiment did not yet include active error correction.

A team from the University of Chicago Pritzker School of Molecular Engineering, Harvard, Stony Brook University and Quantinuum created and tested the operations on Quantinuum’s H2 trapped-ion processor. Using 54 entangled qubits, they built a system based on the S3 symmetry—the rotations and mirror-image flips that preserve an equilateral triangle—and encoded information in “topological qutrits,” which have three possible levels rather than the two levels of ordinary qubits.

The researchers combined two kinds of operations. In braiding, one anyon is moved around another; because the order of these movements matters, braiding can manipulate the encoded information. In fusion, two anyons are brought together and the resulting state is measured. Together, the team demonstrated an entangling gate produced through braiding and two measurements produced through fusion. These operations form a universal gate set, meaning they can in principle generate any quantum operation.

Ruben Verresen of UChicago PME, a co-author of the study published in *Nature*, described the anyons as emergent versions of quarks whose movement can enable arbitrary quantum computations. The objects are not ordinary particles found independently in nature. Scientists create them inside quantum circuits by entangling conventional qubits into collective states with particle-like properties. Their information is distributed across many qubits, which can provide protection against some disturbances.

The advance builds on a 2024 experiment involving Verresen that created non-Abelian anyons associated with the D4 symmetry, describing the rotations and reflections of a square. That work showed the anyons could be created and manipulated, but braiding alone did not provide enough operations for universal computation. The new S3 system required fusion to complete the set.

The approach may also address one of quantum computing’s costly error-correction steps. Protected information is typically spread across many physical qubits, but those codes often lack every operation needed for universal computation. Engineers therefore use specially prepared “magic states,” commonly produced through a resource-intensive process called distillation. The researchers demonstrated that topological operations could directly produce a magic state, potentially avoiding that process.

However, the experiment was a proof of principle rather than a fault-tolerant quantum computer: it tested the individual building blocks and did not include active error correction. The next step is to combine the operations with error-correction techniques.
