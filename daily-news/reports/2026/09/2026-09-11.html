Deadly cost of Sasol's Secunda pollution
global_front_pageglobal_south_and_exploitedpolitics_and_powerpublic_healthclimate_disastereconomy_and_businessmaterial_harmregional expansion
What happened

Air pollution from Sasol’s Secunda coal-to-liquids complex is estimated to contribute to about 1,000 deaths and R19 billion in annual economic losses, with Crea also linking the emissions to adverse birth outcomes, childhood asthma and lost working time.

The estimates come from a health impact assessment by the Centre for Research on Energy and Clean Air (Crea), covering the 2024/25 financial year. It attributes an additional health burden to emissions from Sasol’s facility in Secunda, in the Highveld Priority Area, a designated air-pollution hotspot.

Crea estimates that the emissions contributed to about 1,100 preterm births, 820 low-birthweight births and 2,030 asthma emergency-room visits. About 930 of those visits involved children aged between zero and 17. The assessment also estimates 260 new cases of childhood asthma and approximately 23,800 years of life lost through premature deaths associated with the facility’s emissions.

These are statistical estimates of an additional pollution-related health burden, not a record of individually identified people whose illnesses or deaths were directly caused by the plant. The estimated economic cost includes the value of premature deaths, illness, disability, healthcare use, adverse birth outcomes and productivity losses. Crea puts the total at about $1 billion, or R19 billion, and estimates 468,000 work-absence days during the financial year.

How the emissions were assessed

Crea used emissions reported by Sasol in an atmospheric-dispersion model to isolate the facility’s contribution from other pollution sources in the Highveld. The assessment covers emissions from stacks at the complex, rather than associated activities such as transport.

Jamie Kelly, a Crea air-quality analyst, said the modelling followed the emissions from Sasol Secunda as they dispersed and underwent chemical transformation in the atmosphere. The researchers then estimated health effects associated specifically with that additional pollution, rather than assigning the Highveld’s entire pollution burden to Sasol.

The report says Sasol emitted 112,000 tonnes of sulphur dioxide (SO₂) in 2024, alongside significant quantities of hydrogen sulphide (H₂S), particulate matter and other pollutants. The modelling found that pollution concentrations were higher close to Secunda, while fine particles could travel considerably farther. The mortality estimate reflects exposure to PM₂.₅ and nitrogen dioxide, including secondary particulate matter formed from SO₂, H₂S and nitrogen oxides.

Daniel Nesan, the report’s lead author, said the study examined where pollution released by the facility travels, its effects on health and who bears its costs. He said the scale of the SO₂ emissions was significant, adding that Secunda emitted almost one-third more SO₂ than the United Kingdom as a whole in 2024.

Crea said the measured health impacts from H₂S-related odour episodes were additional to the PM₂.₅ and nitrogen-dioxide effects quantified in the assessment. Recurring “rotten-egg” odours have been reported around Secunda and, at times, across parts of Gauteng. The organisation cautioned that the presence or absence of an odour could not be used to judge the full extent of exposure.

Emission limits and regulatory dispute

The findings come as Sasol operates under alternative SO₂ emission limits that are less stringent than South Africa’s generally applicable minimum emission standards for the relevant boilers. Crea said the general standard is 1,000 milligrams per normal cubic metre, while Sasol’s approved alternative limits are 1,700mg/Nm³ for the west stack and 1,400mg/Nm³ for the east stack. Those alternative limits apply until March 2030.

Crea is calling for the limits to be reviewed and for binding annual reductions across the Secunda complex covering SO₂, nitrogen oxides, particulate matter and H₂S. Its recommendations also include stronger emissions reporting, independent monitoring with publicly available data, and greater involvement by affected communities in air-quality planning and enforcement.

The H₂S issue has also prompted regulatory action. After strong sulphurous odours were reported in Johannesburg and elsewhere in Gauteng in January and March, the Department of Forestry, Fisheries and the Environment said H₂S was probably responsible. The department acknowledged that Sasol was operating within its legal limits but said investigations had found that the limits were not sufficient to properly protect public health, particularly around Secunda. A proposed reduction of the H₂S standard for coal-gasification processes was subsequently sent for another round of public consultation.

Sasol said it was reviewing the Crea report and could not endorse its specific figures because it had not independently verified them. The company said health outcomes should be attributed to a single source with caution because the Highveld is affected by multiple industrial, domestic, transport and regional sources.

It also said its alternative SO₂ application was intended to regulate emissions from its steam-plant boilers on a load basis rather than solely through concentration limits. Sasol said this approach was expected to produce greater overall SO₂ reductions while also reducing particulate matter and nitrogen oxides. The company said ambient SO₂ concentrations in Secunda and surrounding areas were below national standards, that it had invested more than R9.5 billion in emission-abatement initiatives since 2015, and that its roadmap aimed to cut steam-plant SO₂ emissions by about 30% by 2030 from the 2019 baseline.

Sasol is challenging requirements for a 40% reduction by 2030 under the Highveld Priority Area air-quality management plan and related regulations. It said the case concerns whether that target applies collectively across industry or is binding on individual facilities. The company has submitted an emissions-reduction and management plan and is awaiting feedback from the relevant licensing authority.

The dispute sits within the broader “Deadly Air” litigation. In 2022, the High Court found that poor Highveld air quality breached residents’ constitutional right to an environment not harmful to their health and wellbeing. The Supreme Court of Appeal upheld that finding and confirmed the government’s legal duty to implement and enforce the area’s air-quality management plan.

‘I am trapped here’: Gulf-based Nepali workers agonise as devastating floods ravage home

What happened

Nepali workers in Dubai, Qatar and elsewhere in the Gulf are watching catastrophic late-August floods destroy homes, kill relatives and cut off communities in Nepal, while the cost of flights, damaged roads and uncertain leave policies leaves many unable to return.

Under Dubai’s harsh afternoon sun, 32-year-old construction worker Hari Krishna lays bricks at a luxury residential complex. His attention, however, is fixed on Nepal’s Bhote Khosi-Trishuli corridor, about 2,000 miles away, where floods have devastated his home district of Nuwakot.

For a week, Krishna has used every spare moment to call relatives through iMO and follow local news on his cracked smartphone. The floods swept through Bidur Municipality in Nuwakot, carrying away bridges, roads and hillside settlements. Middle East Eye reported at least 1,399 deaths across Nepal and nearly 5,400 people missing at the time of writing.

Krishna’s family lost their house, household supplies, money, gold and a shed containing their cows and goats. Their land was destroyed, and Krishna said he sees no possibility of farming there again. His 67-year-old grandfather was also killed.

“I planned to visit my family for the upcoming Dashain festival, but that wish will now be unfulfilled with my grandfather gone,” Krishna said. His family is sleeping in a holding centre, he added, frightened by the threat of further landslides while preparing his grandfather’s final rites.

He asked his supervisor for emergency leave but said he could not afford a sudden flight. Even if he could, roads to his village had been washed out. “It would have been better if I was home and killed by the flood,” he said. “I am trapped here.”

Distance turns disaster into confinement

Krishna’s situation reflects the dilemma facing Nepali migrants across the Gulf. Hundreds of thousands work in Qatar, the United Arab Emirates and Saudi Arabia to support families in Nepal. Their earnings are central to the country’s economy: remittances account for roughly a third of Nepal’s gross domestic product, while money sent from the Gulf represents 40 percent of all Nepali remittances, according to Nepal Rastra Bank figures cited in the report.

Rameshwar Nepal, an investigator with the human and labour rights organisation Equidem, said many workers urgently want to return to see whether their families are alive and what remains of their homes and communities. But they face the cost of travel, restrictions on leave and uncertainty over whether they would be allowed to return to their jobs.

“They don’t know whether they will be allowed to go, whether they will get paid leave, or, even if they are willing to go on unpaid leave, whether they will be allowed to do so,” Nepal said while coordinating relief efforts from Kathmandu.

The workers also need their jobs precisely because the disaster has taken away their property and livelihoods. Nepal said many fear losing employment if they leave, even temporarily, and do not know whether they would have a job to return to.

He linked the problem to the kafala labour sponsorship framework, which he described as restricting migrant workers’ freedom of movement even during emergencies. He compared the anxiety with the difficulties workers faced during the Covid-19 lockdowns, when some were unable to return to their families.

Families lose homes, property and relatives

In Qatar, 37-year-old driver Shiva Ujjwal experienced the disaster from another distance. He was beginning an early-morning shift after three years transporting cleaners and construction workers from labour camps outside Doha when fellow workers told him what had happened.

When his supervisor allowed him to use a phone, Ujjwal’s calls home failed because communications in the affected region had been cut off. Videos of the flooding later showed him the scale of the destruction. His wife, children and parents escaped, but nine relatives were killed, while many other people from his village remain missing.

“My family members ran with empty hands, with no chance to take important papers, money, or gold,” Ujjwal said. He described the flood as destroying all their property in less than five seconds.

Ujjwal had built his family home gradually, using savings from work in Saudi Arabia, the UAE and Qatar to add one floor at a time. Although his wages were modest, he said the work had kept his family afloat. The flood has now left him facing the prospect of rebuilding from nothing.

His supervisor is arranging for him to return to Nepal on unpaid leave. Yet the separation remains difficult, particularly after the initial communication blackout. “My body is here, but I am not here,” Ujjwal said.

A related update attributed to Nepal’s National Disaster Risk Reduction and Management Authority put the death toll at 1,367 confirmed bodies, with 5,133 people missing and more than 7,000 requiring urgent medical attention. It said more than 21,000 Nepal Army, police and Armed Police Force personnel remained deployed in affected areas, while DNA collection was being used to identify victims.

Relief efforts cannot replace being home

Nepali workers in the Gulf have begun organising grassroots assistance through WhatsApp groups, pooling limited savings to send relief to the worst-hit districts. For families who have lost homes, livestock, documents and livelihoods, however, financial support cannot resolve the immediate problem of separation.

Krishna and Ujjwal represent two versions of the same crisis: one cannot afford or physically reach home, while the other may be able to return only through unpaid leave and with no certainty about his employment afterwards. As Nepal begins confronting the destruction, the migrant workers who help sustain their families may also be expected to finance and carry out much of the rebuilding from far away.

What happened

Power cuts and fuel shortages are putting patients and households across Gaza at risk. At Nasser Hospital, one of the territory’s largest functioning hospitals, shortages of fuel, engine oil and spare parts have forced rotating outages between buildings. Ahmed Al-Nabris, an internal medicine specialist, said one of the hospital’s four generators was out of service and the largest two of the remaining three were under strain. Technicians have extended oil-change intervals from the usual 250 operating hours to about 1,000. Patients needing ventilators or nebulisers must wait for power to return, while some wards endure sweltering conditions.

Gaza has had no grid electricity since Israel’s military offensive began after the Hamas-led attacks of October 7, 2023. Israel halted electricity supplies and restricted fuel and engine-oil imports, describing the measures as punishment for Hamas; the restrictions continued after an October 2025 US-brokered ceasefire that ended full-scale fighting. The shortages have also made neighbourhood-generator electricity increasingly unaffordable, with residents reporting costs rising about 20-fold and engine oil reaching US$1,300 per litre, compared with about US$3 before the war. Israel’s COGAT agency said a monitored mechanism facilitates the entry of engine oil, spare parts and medical equipment, while seeking to prevent advanced equipment from reaching Hamas.

global_south_and_exploitedpolitics_and_powermaterial_harmregional expansion
What happened

Beneficiaries receiving funds from a deceased worker’s Afore will not have to pay income tax (ISR) under a mandatory criterion approved by Mexico’s Supreme Court of Justice of the Nation (SCJN). The ruling, adopted by six votes to three through a project by Minister Giovanni Figueroa Mejía, treats the funds as materially analogous to an inheritance or legacy. The court based its decision on Article 93, section XXII, of the ISR Law and Article 261, section II, paragraph a, of its regulation. Figueroa argued that the money belonged to the worker and accumulated during the worker’s lifetime, while the beneficiary designation allows a specific person to receive an individualized asset or right after the worker’s death.

The decision renewed disagreement among the ministers. In July, the plenary majority had rejected a proposal by Sara Irene Herrerías Guerra to tax the funds. Herrerías, Lenia Batres Guadarrama, and María Estela Ríos González voted against the new criterion. Herrerías argued that equating Afore funds with a legacy creates an exemption not established by lawmakers; Ríos called it a false analogy and an instance of judicial creation. Batres said the SCJN lacks authority to exempt taxes and argued that beneficiaries should pay ISR when workers earning more than 15 times the minimum wage would have owed it upon withdrawal.

The office of the Central Financial Commission formulated the plan with relevant financial authorities, Lu Lei, deputy governor of the People’s Bank of China (PBOC), said at a press conference on Sept. 10. China will retain its managed floating exchange-rate system, allowing the market to play a decisive role in exchange-rate formation while guarding against herd behavior and self-reinforcing irrational expectations.

Capital-market reform is another major component.
