Reports of violence against women in Gaza surge, UN agency finds

What happened

Reports of gender-based violence against women and girls in Gaza have risen sharply amid mass displacement, hunger, collapsed public services and prolonged school closures, UN Population Fund (UNFPA) reports say. The findings document increased early and forced marriage, sexual violence and exploitation, while warning that many cases remain unreported.

A UNFPA-led analysis of data collected by 10 organisations says the crisis has created conditions in which women and girls face escalating risks of gender-based violence. It describes a “severe escalation” in early and forced marriage during the second quarter of this year.

The UNFPA says reported cases of early and forced marriage rose by 123% from the first quarter to the second. The cases involved girls aged 12 to 17. The agency cautions that establishing the actual scale remains difficult because sexual violence and other abuse are acutely under-reported, largely because of fear of stigma and retaliation.

The findings are based on research carried out in late 2025 and early 2026. A separate report presents testimony from 230 women living in crowded displacement camps in Gaza City and Khan Younis, describing violence they had experienced or witnessed.

Displacement and poverty are increasing pressure on girls

The evidence links the rise in child and forced marriage to the conditions created by the war, including the deaths of parents, poverty, hunger, insecurity and the breakdown of social structures. Nearly all of Gaza’s population of more than two million people has reportedly been displaced, with about 1.2 million living in tents or makeshift shelters.

Public services have largely collapsed, and about 700,000 children missed formal in-person schooling for almost three years. Girls face heightened safety concerns while living among strangers without privacy; families also describe marriage as a way to protect daughters from harassment and preserve their modesty.

Six women in different displacement camps described cases in which girls were exchanged in marriage for food. One woman said a man married off his 14-year-old daughter in exchange for two bags of flour during last year’s famine so that he and his other children could eat. Another woman said rape victims were sometimes married to their rapists to avoid shame and scandal, describing weddings held at schools soon after rape cases were discovered.

Nestor Owomuhangi, the UNFPA representative to Palestine, said the circumstances of the past three years had eroded social structures and left “no system”. The Women’s Affairs Center in Gaza, a Palestinian NGO working with UNFPA, and other support groups have documented cases in which girls married in their early teens experienced abuse and became pregnant.

The reports say adolescent pregnancies are particularly dangerous in conditions of hunger, poor hygiene and limited healthcare. Owomuhangi said girls whose bodies are not fully developed face serious consequences when pregnancy follows child marriage. Support groups report that girls in their early teens are more likely than adult women to suffer miscarriages and have underweight babies.

Orphaned girls are described as especially vulnerable. One woman interviewed in a Gaza City camp said two girls from her family, aged 14 and 15, had been married off by their uncles after their parents were killed. She said the girls did not understand “what marriage or life is”.

Sexual violence remains under-reported

The UNFPA-led task force’s latest analysis says reported incidents of sexual violence, including rape and sexual assault, became “critical” in Gaza. They made up 2.7% of all reported gender-based violence incidents between April and June 2026, compared with 2.1% in the previous quarter. In absolute terms, the number of reported cases rose by 61%.

The increase is linked in the report to efforts to raise awareness and provide better ways to make confidential reports, rather than being presented as a complete measure of the violence occurring. Women aged 18 to 59 made up the majority of recorded survivors, followed by adolescent girls at 8% and adult men at 5%.

UN safety audits identified particular risks for women and girls at night. Some avoid camp toilets and bathrooms because they fear harassment or assault. Widows and women whose husbands are detained or missing are also described as facing increased risks of sexual abuse and exploitation.

One account in the testimony report describes a widow seeking a tent who was allegedly offered shelter in exchange for spending the night with a camp representative. Nearly one in four households is now reportedly headed by a woman, twice the proportion before the war, increasing the number of women responsible for securing food, shelter and safety in extremely difficult conditions.

Before the war, early marriage was already more common in Hamas-run Gaza than in the occupied West Bank, although it was declining. Gaza’s law sets a minimum marital age of 17 but allows exceptions with parental consent and a judge’s authorisation. Court officials can approve marriages for people as young as 14 years and seven months. A local Sharia expert said legal safeguards were weakened when Islamic courts handling family matters were closed for much of the war.

Aid agencies say improved humanitarian conditions could reduce the risks. They are calling for better-quality shelters, upgraded latrines and sanitation facilities, and more lighting, while longer-term efforts include education and healthcare programmes and campaigns against domestic violence and child marriage.

Trump’s Board of Peace proposes US$2.45 billion Gaza reconstruction plan

What happened

The US-backed Board of Peace has unveiled a proposed six-month, US$2.45 billion programme to begin rebuilding and governing Gaza, presenting 66 infrastructure, education and health projects while acknowledging delays and major political obstacles. The plan is aimed at a territory where basic systems remain severely damaged and where attacks, aid restrictions and disputes over Israeli withdrawal continue to threaten implementation.

Jared Kushner led the presentation on Wednesday as board members briefed donors on the sidelines of the United Nations General Assembly in New York. Kushner has no official role within the US administration but serves as one of its leading peace negotiators. He is an executive board member of the Board of Peace, while President Donald Trump is its chairman.

The proposal is described as a starter programme rather than a complete reconstruction effort. The board estimates that fully rebuilding Gaza will require more than US$71 billion over the next decade. The longer-term work would include restoring power lines, water pipes and other foundational systems, following the destruction caused by years of fighting. The plan’s 66 projects are intended to rehabilitate economic infrastructure and support education and health initiatives as part of Trump’s broader 20-point plan.

Little immediate detail was available about the projects’ sequencing or implementation. The board briefed donors while Trump’s ceasefire framework faced what was described as significant roadblocks, leaving the proposed programme dependent on political conditions that remain unsettled.

Reconstruction amid continuing violence

The plan was presented against a backdrop of continuing deaths in Gaza. The Trump-brokered ceasefire took effect on 10 October 2025, but Middle East Eye reported that Israel has carried out near-weekly air strikes in Gaza since then, with civilians killed in each instance according to local media. On Wednesday, Gaza’s Palestinian health ministry reported 1,402 known deaths from Israeli attacks since the ceasefire. It put the total number of deaths since October 2023 at just under 74,000.

Nickolay Mladenov, the Board of Peace’s point person for Gaza on its executive board, said the group wanted to give Palestinians in Gaza and Israeli communities across the border “a new chance”. He said the objective was to help both populations move beyond the fear of violence that had shaped their lives for years.

Kushner acknowledged that the board had experienced delays during the 11 months since it was formed. “Different delays on different things, for whatever reason,” he said. He added that the board was refining its preparations and hoped to act “very, very soon”. His remarks indicated that the proposal remained in preparation rather than describing completed reconstruction work.

Aid access and withdrawal conditions

Humanitarian access is a central dispute surrounding the board’s role. Athena Rayburn, executive director of the Association of International Development Agencies, told the UN Security Council last month that the organisation was failing in Gaza and had functioned to further entrench what she called Israel’s unlawful presence in the territory.

Rayburn alleged that Israel had rejected scissors, crutches, wheelchairs and prosthetic limbs as “dual-use” items, barred specialised medical staff and imposed restrictions on engine oil so severe that its price had reached 45 times the normal level. She also said Israel had rejected watermelon and onions under an unpublished humanitarian policy. Questioning how the board’s mandate was being used, she said: “This cannot be the new status quo.”

The board’s position on Israeli withdrawal has also become more restrictive. The Yellow Line, established under the October ceasefire agreement, divides areas of Gaza under Israeli and Palestinian control. After senior board officials met with Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu, the board said that an Israeli withdrawal beyond the line would take place only after Hamas had been completely disarmed.

That condition includes light and heavy weapons as well as tunnels, and abandons the phased approach the board had initially presented. The board said the requirement reflected Hamas’s commitment to mediators, but it ties the timing of withdrawal to a condition whose fulfilment remains unresolved.

The situation is further complicated by territorial changes described in the evidence. Israel has seized additional Palestinian territory and now controls more than 60 percent of Gaza, while Netanyahu has said the aim is to expand that control to about 70 percent. The proposed reconstruction programme therefore faces not only a vast financial gap but also continuing violence, contested humanitarian access and disagreement over the territory’s security and political future.

Ekurhuleni killings: ‘The onus cannot shift to women to keep themselves safe’

What happened

The discovery of nine women’s bodies in Ekurhuleni since July has intensified concern over gender-based violence in South Africa, while advocates say the response must address failures in policing, investigations, prosecutions and survivor support rather than place responsibility for safety on women.

Nine women’s bodies have been found in Ekurhuleni since July. Police are investigating possible links between some of the cases, but have cautioned that it is too early to conclude that all the killings were committed by one perpetrator.

The latest victim, Jabulile Ntimba, was found in Dawn Park on September 17. Two women appeared in the Boksburg Magistrate’s Court on Monday in connection with her murder. They face charges of murder and defeating the ends of justice, and their case was postponed to October 1 for a bail application. A man arrested alongside them was released without being charged after investigators concluded there was insufficient evidence to proceed against him.

The investigation has unfolded alongside the discovery of another woman’s body elsewhere in Gauteng. An unidentified woman, believed to be in her 20s, was found wrapped in a duvet in a dam in Moroka, Soweto, on Sunday. Police opened an inquest and have not indicated any connection between her death and the Ekurhuleni cases.

Advocates reject shifting responsibility to women

For people working on gender-based violence, the killings are not an isolated rupture but another expression of violence that has become embedded in everyday life. Advocate Bernadine Bachar, a board member of the National Shelter Movement of South Africa and director of the Saartjie Baartman Centre for Women and Children, said the response should not become another demand that women protect themselves.

“The onus should not be lying on South African women to keep themselves safe at all,” Bachar said. “The onus cannot shift.”

She said government departments responsible for public protection needed to do their work, while police required adequate training and resources for visible policing, arrests and investigations that brought cases to prosecution. Bachar also described the effects of violence as extending beyond individual attacks: women, she said, routinely assess threats in public and private spaces and remain on constant alert.

Bachar identified failures in the implementation of legislation, the processing of forensic evidence, prosecutions and sentencing. She also said shelters and organisations supporting survivors were being left to provide services that should receive adequate state support. Forensic kits could take months to process, she said, while under-resourced shelters and non-governmental organisations struggled to meet demand.

A system with laws but persistent implementation failures

Princess Eurika Mogane, a commissioner at the Commission for Gender Equality, said the sequence of killings had shaken South Africans into confronting violence that had become normalised. She said the country had become so numb that people could pass someone lying in the street without stopping to ask whether help was needed.

Police statistics illustrate part of the wider crisis. In Mpumalanga, 688 rapes were reported between April and June, including 332 involving children. Mogane said such figures did not include violence that was never reported or cases later withdrawn.

In July, the Commission for Gender Equality, the South African Human Rights Commission and the CRL Rights Commission launched a joint inquiry into the reporting, victim support, investigation and pre-trial handling of gender-based violence and femicide cases. The inquiry received such a large response that its submission deadline was extended to September 11.

Mogane said the central question was whether existing laws, policies and frameworks were being implemented. She rejected framing the crisis as a conflict between men and women, arguing that attention should instead focus on perpetrators, the wider community and failures across the system.

The Commission for Gender Equality is also examining the capacity of the forensic system, particularly DNA processing, which Mogane said could delay sexual-offence cases in court. Its commissioners and legal team monitor gender-based violence and femicide proceedings and can intervene when victims are not treated in accordance with the law.

Bachar said the response required more than another declaration of concern. She called for properly funded shelters and NGOs, visible policing, stronger investigations, prosecutions and a criminal justice system capable of preventing further violence. The shelter sector, she said, should not be expected to carry responsibilities belonging to the state without adequate support.

A British survivor told an Ontario court that Kenneth Law exploited her despair by selling her toxic chemicals when she was 23. She also said she was beginning to feel hopeful about the future.

Law, a former chef, has pleaded guilty to 14 counts of aiding suicide in Canada. Authorities say he met customers through online suicide forums and sold about 1,200 packages of toxic substances to people in dozens of countries. The packages included material containing lethal concentrations of sodium nitrite and were often sold for about US$80.

The reported scale of the UK deaths and shipments varies. Canadian prosecutors linked Law to 79 deaths in the UK, while another account described almost 80. One agreed statement of facts said 330 packages were sent there; authorities also said roughly a quarter of his packages went to the UK. The accounts place the sales across at least 40 countries, with one report specifying 41.

Families describe the losses

What happened

At GovTech 2026 in Durban, South African officials presented a national digital-public-infrastructure plan that closely echoes Estonia’s e-governance model, while stressing that South Africa must adapt foreign practices to its own institutions, skills, sovereignty concerns and technology market.

The plan concerns how government departments share data, deliver services and manage digital identity. Minister of Communications and Digital Technologies Solly Malatsi said Estonia was an important reference point for DPI development, but rejected “backroom deals” or blind copying. He said different environments and levels of societal digital skills meant that Estonia’s system could not simply be transferred to South Africa.

The technology at issue includes Estonia’s X-Road architecture, which allows government departments to exchange citizen data after authorisation is granted once. It is being considered alongside South Africa’s DPI and the Department of Home Affairs’ digital-identity framework, although the evidence does not establish that South Africa has formally adopted X-Road.

From fragmented departments to shared infrastructure

Sita managing director Magatho Mello said existing Estonian technology should not be rejected merely because it is foreign: a local vendor offering a less effective equivalent would be an “immediate non-starter”. But he warned that achieving Estonia’s level of e-governance would require investment, coordination and compromises over institutional autonomy. Departments that previously planned their information and communications technology separately would need to plan together.

Kule Duma of the Presidency’s Digital Services Unit outlined a roadmap under Phase 2 of Operation Vulindlela. The first six months would map existing capabilities, mandates and responsibilities across government. Months six to 12 would establish data-sovereignty controls, including rules for keeping sensitive information such as biometrics on state-owned infrastructure. During months 12 to 24, government would work with industry to expand services through the MyMzansi app while keeping core data under state custody.

Duma described the objective as a seamless, “one-touch” channel for public services, built around the “once-only” principle: citizens provide information to the state once, while internal data systems handle verification across departments. The Presidency’s Digital Services Unit would coordinate agencies, with Sita providing platform hosting, a structure presented as a way to limit vendor lock-in and protect sensitive data.

The notebook portrays this as evidence of a broader shift from departmental silos towards centrally coordinated digital infrastructure. It also argues that elements of Home Affairs’ digital-identity work and MyMzansi resemble Estonia’s approach. The author disclosed having attended an Estonian foreign-ministry-hosted e-governance conference where X-Road and other technologies were presented, and described the perceived influence as especially visible at GovTech. Claims linking the DPI programme to other government decisions were presented as the author’s speculation rather than established policy.

What happened

China’s AI community has largely treated the latest wave of American warnings about catastrophic risks as a geopolitical dispute rather than a reason to slow development, even as Chinese officials and safety specialists acknowledge dangers involving autonomous systems, cybersecurity, misinformation and political control.

AI is already being integrated into everyday life and public services in China. In Hangzhou, humanoid robots in fluorescent-yellow vests patrol tourist areas and admonish jaywalkers, while the chatbot Doubao has become so popular for medical advice that some citizens call it “Doctor Doubao.” The government is directing resources toward a globally competitive AI industry and encouraging adoption in manufacturing, energy and agriculture. Leaders have also used language associated with the country’s earlier drive to develop nuclear weapons to call for a nationwide effort to produce breakthroughs.

The result, the evidence describes, is a markedly optimistic public mood. A Stanford University industry report cited polls in which 84 percent of Chinese respondents said AI-enabled products and services excited them, compared with a global average of 53 percent and 38 percent in the United States. Chinese engineers and AI-safety specialists interviewed for the account said they recognized existential risks but viewed loss of control as primarily an American problem for now. Their immediate priority was catching up with U.S. models, which continued to lead overall benchmarks despite progress by DeepSeek and Moonshot AI.

That outlook shaped the response to Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei’s proposal for an intentional slowdown in AI development. Chinese state media and nationalist commentators portrayed the proposal as an effort to delay China’s progress, particularly because Amodei has supported export controls on advanced chips and restrictions affecting Chinese AI laboratories. Kwan Yee Ng of Concordia AI said many in China considered the call to “pace the frontier” hypocritical because export controls were already constraining the country’s development. A DeepSeek engineer, Liu Shengyu, separately called for frontier AI to remain open and affordable, though he later said his comments were personal.

Safety priorities reflect different systems

Chinese companies are generally described as more resource-constrained than their U.S. counterparts, with safety work focused largely on regulatory requirements such as data security and politically sensitive content. Zilan Qian of GovAI said Chinese authorities play the central role in setting the safety agenda, while companies comply.

That does not mean officials dismiss AI risks. Human skeletal evolution was shaped not only by changes in genes themselves but also by changes in when and where those genes are switched on. Researchers have mapped hundreds of thousands of human-specific regulatory variants and linked them to a major remodelling of cartilage chemistry that may help explain both human skeletal traits and vulnerability to joint disease.

The skeleton was central to the evolution of bipedalism, slender bones, large cranial vaults and other human characteristics, but the genetic basis of these differences has remained poorly understood. The study examined 561,410 single-nucleotide substitutions that distinguish humans from other great apes and are fixed or nearly fixed in the human lineage. These variants were located in promoters and enhancers—cis-regulatory elements that help control gene activity.

A genome-wide map of skeletal gene regulation

To test whether the substitutions alter gene regulation, the researchers used massively parallel reporter assays (MPRAs) in human fetal chondrocytes. These cells are important during bone development because they form the cartilage scaffold from which much of the skeleton develops. Each assay compared a human-derived sequence with the corresponding great-ape version.

The experiments identified 15,077 loci with human-specific regulatory activity. In total, 65,576 sequence pairs showed significant activity in at least one allele. The researchers then generated human–chimpanzee and human–gorilla hybrid cells and differentiated them into osteochondral progenitors. Because the human and ape genomes in these composite cells shared the same cellular environment, the comparison helped separate effects caused by DNA sequence differences—known as cis-regulatory effects—from broader cellular or environmental effects.

Together, the MPRA results and hybrid-cell gene-expression data produced atlases linking human-specific sequence changes to changes in gene regulation. The researchers report that these changes were distributed across the extracellular matrix (ECM), the network of proteins and carbohydrates that gives cartilage its structure and mechanical properties.

A major shift in cartilage chemistry

The strongest pattern involved glycosaminoglycans (GAGs), long sugar molecules that help organize the cartilage matrix. The study found widespread human-specific suppression of GAG biosynthesis. In the hybrid osteochondral cells, 17 of 21 differentially expressed GAG-related genes were downregulated in the human genome relative to the ape genomes, and the downregulated genes showed a greater average change than upregulated genes.

One example was CSGALNACT1, a gene involved in GAG production. A human regulatory sequence associated with the gene had 34% lower activity than the ancestral ape sequence in the MPRA. In the hybrid cells, the human allele was also downregulated relative to both chimpanzee and gorilla alleles.

The researchers examined cartilage samples from 42 humans and seven great apes, covering 139 samples and eight joints. Human cartilage contained approximately three times less GAG than ape cartilage, with the broader results described as an approximately three-to-fourfold reduction. The study therefore connects regulatory changes measured in cells with a physical difference in cartilage composition across species.

The authors say the human-specific shift carries signatures of accelerated evolution and selection, but the evidence does not point to a single simple evolutionary process. Some observations are also compatible with relaxed negative selection, under which lower expression and greater variation can accumulate. The results instead indicate that the GAG pathway underwent extensive remodelling in the human lineage.

An evolutionary change with a possible disease cost

The study proposes that reduced GAG production may have contributed to distinctive human skeletal biology while also leaving human joints more susceptible to degeneration. The authors estimate that the evolutionary reduction in human GAG content is about twice as large as the 25–38% reduction previously reported in patients with certain degenerative conditions relative to healthy controls. They suggest that humans may therefore carry a baseline cartilage deficit that makes joints more vulnerable, although the study describes this as a likely contributor rather than establishing a direct clinical cause.

The work also examines ACAN, which encodes a major cartilage protein and contains repeats that anchor GAGs. Analysis of long-read sequences from 316 human haplotypes and 26 non-human great-ape haplotypes found two human-specific pulses of repeat expansion. The researchers also connect ACAN with extensive methylation changes separating modern humans from Neanderthals and Denisovans, reinforcing the evidence for accelerated evolution in the human lineage.

The resulting atlas is intended as a framework for studying the genetic basis of human skeletal traits and disorders. It does not cover every regulatory element: the MPRA library included about 40% of putative human enhancers. Its findings nevertheless identify thousands of candidate changes and point to coordinated regulation of the cartilage ECM as a central feature of human skeletal evolution.
