The Israeli War on Lebanon Marks Two Years of Uninterrupted Violence

What happened

By September 2026, two years after Israel’s major September 2024 offensive in Lebanon, the conflict has moved through open war, ceasefires and renewed fighting. Israeli forces still hold positions in southern Lebanon, tens of thousands remain unable to return, and negotiations have yet to resolve the military and political dispute.

The latest escalation followed Hamas’ October 7, 2023, attacks in Israel, in which 1,200 Israelis were killed and 251 people were taken hostage. Hezbollah then began launching projectiles at Israeli positions along the border, turning the Lebanon front into a sustained theater of hostilities.

Tensions intensified on September 17, 2024, when thousands of pagers belonging to Hezbollah members exploded at locations across Lebanon. The operation killed 12 people and injured hundreds. Six days later, Israel launched large-scale airstrikes that killed more than 500 people in one day, according to the account. The strikes began an open war that continued until a cease-fire two months later.

By the end of that offensive, Lebanon’s Health Ministry had recorded nearly 3,800 deaths and 15,800 injuries since October 2023. In Israel, 47 civilians and 31 military personnel were killed during the same period.

A cease-fire reduced violence but did not end the conflict

The November 27, 2024, cease-fire between Israel and Hezbollah sharply reduced the fighting but left the central disputes unresolved. They included the presence of Israeli troops in southern Lebanon, Hezbollah’s disarmament and implementation of United Nations Security Council Resolution 1701, which calls for a complete cessation of hostilities.

Israeli attacks in southern Lebanon continued throughout 2025 despite the cease-fire. The United Nations verified at least 137 civilians killed in Israeli operations between the cease-fire’s entry into force and the end of 2025, while also documenting thousands of truce violations.

The situation deteriorated again in March 2026, when Hezbollah attacked Israel in response to the U.S.-Israeli war on Iran. Israel answered with a broad air and ground campaign in Lebanon. After a new cease-fire was signed in June, Lebanese authorities recorded more than 4,200 deaths, while more than 1 million people had been displaced at some point. During that period, Hezbollah killed 37 Israeli soldiers and two civilians, according to the account.

Since October 8, 2023, Lebanon’s Health Ministry has recorded at least 8,929 deaths and 30,614 injuries from Israeli attacks and incursions. Save the Children said Israel had killed an average of seven children each day in Lebanon since March. The account characterizes the overall war as genocide, while describing the civilian toll, village destruction and displacement as evidence of a continuing emergency.

Israeli positions and the failed return to normal life

Following the 2026 escalation, Israel launched a ground incursion into southern Lebanon and established what it calls a security zone extending several kilometers into Lebanese territory. The zone lies between the Blue Line, the de facto boundary between the countries, and the Litani River, an area described as strategically important.

Israeli forces destroyed most bridges in the area and seized Beaufort Castle in May 2026. The medieval fortress overlooks the Litani River and had previously been occupied by Israel during its military presence in southern Lebanon from 1982 to 2000.

Israeli operations have included the destruction of homes and basic infrastructure and daily attacks in rural areas and urban centers including Tyre and the Bekaa Valley. These operations continue to keep civilians in a state of emergency and prevent the safe return of tens of thousands of displaced people.

Talks begin as military issues remain unresolved

The first direct high-level talks between Israeli and Lebanese representatives in more than three decades began in Washington in April 2026, with U.S. mediation. A framework agreement signed by Israel and Lebanon in June provides for a gradual Israeli withdrawal, deployment of the Lebanese army and Hezbollah’s disarmament.

Implementation remains obstructed by the unresolved positions of the parties. Israel maintains military positions inside Lebanon, Hezbollah continues to reject disarmament, and Beirut is attempting to restore effective control over the south. The result is a shift from open warfare to fragile diplomacy rather than a settled peace.

What happened

A Sandton restaurant encounter in which a man allegedly threatened to shoot women after they rejected his advances has become the basis for a broader argument about gendered violence, wealth, race and power. Journalist and cultural critic Sandile Memela says the incident shows why women must be able to say no without fearing intimidation or death.

Memela describes the incident as taking place in a ladies’ room at a top restaurant in Sandton. He writes that a supposedly successful Black man, wearing an expensive watch and arriving with a German car and driver, threatened to shoot and kill women whose “sin” was rejecting his advances. The threat, as quoted in the column, was: “I will shoot you. I will kill you.”

The account says the confrontation did not happen in front of waiters or other diners, but characterizes the private setting as making it worse rather than less serious. Memela presents the episode as an example of a wider pattern, not merely the conduct of one rude or angry customer. The column says the man was later out on bail and had walked away with a warning, while asking where justice for the women involved could be found.

A threat over rejection becomes a question of bodily autonomy

The central message of the column is that women must continue to say no “loudly” — in Sandton as well as Pimville. Memela treats refusal as a basic assertion of bodily ownership and links it to the broader struggle for dignity and equality. His comparison with the 69 people killed in Sharpeville is presented as an invocation of historical resistance, not as a literal equivalence between the two events.

For Memela, the alleged threat demonstrates what happens when a man treats a woman’s rejection as an attack on his status. He argues that the man expected respect because of his money, clothing, car and social position, and that the refusal exposed how dependent his self-worth was on being admired or obeyed. The column repeatedly contrasts financial success with character, self-control and the ability to accept rejection.

Memela also says the incident should be treated as a matter for the law, arguing that a threat to kill should not be excused or softened by a person’s wealth or influence. He refers to attempted assault, criminal injuria and harassment in describing possible legal implications, while urging that no “Do you know who I am?” should shield the man.

Wealth, tenders and the “Big Man”

The column places the incident within a larger criticism of a small class of wealthy Black men whom Memela says acquired money quickly through tenders, political connections or business deals. He argues that some such men confuse access to cash with personal worth and use conspicuous wealth to demand deference from people with less power.

Memela’s language is explicitly interpretive and accusatory. He describes the man as having a deep-seated inferiority complex and as suffering from what he calls “Big Man Syndrome.” He argues that the phenomenon is not African culture, but an authoritarian pattern associated with rulers who accumulate wealth, demand submission and treat other people as instruments of their own importance.

The column connects this argument to inequality. It contrasts an affluent man in Sandton with a waitress earning R7,000, and contrasts exclusive estates such as Steyn City or Waterfall with communities where residents may lack water for days. It also refers to hypothetical or generalized examples of tender beneficiaries receiving very large state-linked contracts. These passages are part of Memela’s broader political critique rather than evidence supplied about the specific man in the restaurant.

Why Memela says the incident matters beyond one restaurant

Memela argues that the alleged threat is significant because it combines money, male entitlement and access to violence. In his framing, a woman’s refusal becomes unacceptable to a man who believes that wealth should guarantee attention, sexual interest and obedience. The gun therefore represents both a direct threat and the coercive power that he believes lies behind the man’s public persona.

The column also places the episode in the history of racial and gender domination. Memela says the struggle for liberation was not intended to enable wealthy Black men to threaten women, but to make Black women safe and free to refuse unwanted advances. He compares the logic of the threat — that a woman’s “no” can be overridden by someone else’s power — with earlier systems that denied Black people control over their own bodies.

His conclusion is that money cannot substitute for dignity, accountability or humanity. Real manhood, he argues, requires self-control and acceptance of rejection. For women, continuing to say no is presented as both personal protection and a contribution to a more just society.

What happened

Nearly seven months into the war in Iran, talks between Washington and Tehran are continuing while neither side appears ready to end the conflict. The immediate stakes extend beyond the battlefield: Gulf economies face disruption, Iran can threaten the Strait of Hormuz, and the next U.S. administration may inherit an increasingly difficult security crisis.

The conflict appears to have reached an impasse. Negotiations between the United States and Iran were still taking place during the week described, but The Atlantic analysis argues that Donald Trump has become disengaged, that his administration has produced no coherent strategy, and that it has not shown it can win the war. Iran’s leadership, meanwhile, appears unwilling to conclude it.

The analysis attributes part of Tehran’s position to the political consequences of peace. Once the fighting stops, it argues, Iran’s leaders would have to govern an angry and impoverished population. That makes an agreement difficult even as the costs of continued conflict rise.

Gulf states push for a way out

The strongest pressure for a settlement is described as coming from the Gulf states, whose economies are directly exposed to the conflict. Their leaders appear increasingly willing to accept some degree of Iranian control over the Strait of Hormuz—an outcome that would previously have been regarded as unacceptable.

That shift reflects the strategic importance of the waterway. The U.S. Navy’s Fifth Fleet, based in Bahrain, had previously helped deter threats to freedom of navigation. But the analysis says the Navy has failed to guarantee the passage of friendly commercial shipping through the strait, even though it has demonstrated an ability to impose a naval blockade on Iran. Iran’s expanded ability to restrict one of the world’s most important trade routes therefore creates a continuing risk for Gulf economies and international commerce.

The future of U.S. bases in the Gulf is also described as uncertain. In this account, Trump’s operation against Iran’s nuclear program damaged it but did not “obliterate” it, as he later claimed. Rebuilding a credible military deterrent would take months or years, leaving the next administration with limited options.

A conflict passed forward

Although Trump still has 27 months in office, the analysis argues that his administration is likely to leave the task of resolving the war to its successor. A future government might seek an agreement resembling the 2015 Iran nuclear deal, but the author says that may be impossible with Iran’s current leadership. Tehran may also doubt whether a future U.S. administration would honor any agreement, given that many of the figures who urged Trump to abandon the 2015 deal in 2018 later supported war with Iran in 2025 and again in 2026.

The result, according to the analysis, could be a successor forced to choose among “truly horrific” strategic options in the Persian Gulf. Iran’s ability to close the Strait of Hormuz could serve as a nonnuclear deterrent against future U.S. or Israeli strikes, the author hopes, reducing Tehran’s perceived need to develop a nuclear deterrent. But the same capability leaves the waterway—and the trade that depends on it—vulnerable to disruption.

The piece presents this as a recurring pattern in U.S. wars in the greater Middle East: one administration makes the defining decisions, while the next inherits the withdrawal or settlement. It points to Iraq, where the George W. Bush administration surged troops during the civil war and later negotiated a 2008 Status of Forces Agreement affirming Iraqi sovereignty and requiring U.S. forces to leave by the end of 2011. Implementation was left to the next administration, yet Republican critics later described the withdrawal as Barack Obama’s decision.

Lessons from Iraq and Afghanistan

The same pattern, the analysis argues, appeared in Afghanistan. The Trump administration negotiated a bilateral withdrawal agreement with the Taliban in 2020. By the time Joe Biden took office 11 months later, his choices were severely constrained. The author criticizes the Biden administration and the U.S. military for the chaotic departure in 2021, while also arguing that Republican commentary often treated the withdrawal as if Biden alone had created the underlying situation.

These precedents shape the article’s warning about Iran. It argues that Republicans may eventually blame the next administration—particularly if it is Democratic—for the consequences of a war begun under Trump. In the author’s view, that political narrative could obscure the decisions that produced the crisis and allow the officials who promoted earlier Middle East interventions to retain influence.

The central unresolved issue is therefore not only how the war ends, but who will be held responsible for what follows. The analysis contends that assigning the consequences to Trump’s successor would be politically useful, even if the successor is left to manage the conflict’s military, diplomatic and economic fallout.
