The work argues that multi-hop questions often compress several dependent reasoning steps into one sentence, while the supporting facts sit in documents at a finer retrievable level. How Hi-Q decides whether to stop or expand

Hi-Q is an evidence-conditioned framework for hierarchical query refinement. At each query node, a resolution operator checks whether the retrieved evidence supports the current query unit. If the node is resolved, the search stops there. If not, the node is expanded using a dependency-preserving binary operator, and the proposed split is checked by a semantic coverage verifier.

That design matters because Hi-Q does not assume in advance which query decomposition will fit the corpus. Instead, it grows a query tree whose shape is determined by corpus support signals rather than by a fixed decomposition template or a pre-built graph. The paper describes the split as ordered: one sub-query establishes a bridge fact, and the second uses that bridge to reach the parent answer. A verifier checks that the two-need split preserves the parent question’s intent without adding, omitting or reordering essential constraints.

The introduction gives a simple example: for a query about “the birthplace of the performer of III,” Hi-Q first resolves who performed “III,” then rewrites the downstream question using that resolved entity before looking for the birthplace. The aim is to ground abstract references in already resolved facts and reduce retrieval interference.

The authors evaluated Hi-Q on MuSiQue, HotpotQA and 2WikiMultiHopQA. The experiments establish performance on English, Wikipedia-derived, passage-based short-answer multi-hop QA, and the authors say the results should not be read as transfer beyond that setting. Hi-Q’s retrieval gains were also reported across embedding backbones. With text-embedding-3-large, Qwen3-Embedding-8B and NV-Embed-v2, the system improved average Recall@5 by 11.8, 14.3 and 8.2 points over dense retrieval alone.

Using second-trimester human cortex, the researchers combined gene expression, chromatin accessibility, DNA methylation and high-resolution 3D chromatin interactions to identify candidate cis-regulatory elements, or cCREs, and their likely target genes.

They isolated vRG and oRG from six donors spanning gestational weeks 15 to 18, and microglia and oligodendrocyte precursor cells from nine donors spanning gestational weeks 22 to 24. Across the four cell types, they identified 69,141 cCREs in vRG, 72,450 in oRG, 65,295 in oligodendrocyte precursor cells and 60,958 in microglia. More than 60% of the cCREs sat outside promoter regions, and the PLAC-seq data produced 136,389, 144,089, 140,297 and 135,123 significant chromatin interactions in the four cell types, respectively.

The data were used to highlight loci with epigenomic signals specific to vRG and oRG and to point to transcription factors that may help drive lineage specification. The paper frames this as a way to study human cortical development at the level of gene regulation rather than only cell identity.

A link between glial regulatory DNA and schizophrenia

The same framework was then applied to disease-associated variation. Using machine learning, the researchers prioritized 112 schizophrenia risk variants within glial cCREs. They also reported in vivo confirmation that the rs4449074 risk allele disrupted a predicted vRG enhancer.

The study places this result in the context of earlier work showing that chromatin looping can help explain how non-coding variants affect lineage-specific gene regulation. Here, the point was not only that schizophrenia-linked variants lie in accessible regulatory DNA, but that the 3D map could help connect those variants to specific glial cell types and, in at least one case, to a predicted functional effect in vRG.

Outer radial glia and human-accelerated regions

The evolutionary signal in the dataset centered on oRG. The researchers found that oRG cCREs were enriched for human accelerated regions compared with other cCREs. They also reported that a subset of these human accelerated regions showed activity differences from chimpanzee orthologues and interacted with genes involved in neuronal development.

That result sits alongside the paper’s broader view of radial glia as key drivers of cortical expansion. The authors describe radial glia as stem cells of the developing brain that form the scaffold supporting neuronal migration, and note that during mid-gestation the scaffold becomes discontinuous as oRG delaminate from the ventricular surface while vRG remain behind. Against that backdrop, the enrichment of accelerated regions in oRG-linked regulatory DNA suggests a possible role for these cells in human-specific gene regulation during corticogenesis.

The paper’s overall claim is that integrating 3D chromatin contacts with other epigenomic layers can reveal how glial populations help shape the developing human cortex, how some schizophrenia risk variants may act in that setting, and how regulatory DNA in oRG may relate to human cortical evolution.

What happened

A search at Jefferson Lab for a confirmed XYZ particle with a high-energy photon beam turned up two unexpected structures instead, adding new clues to a class of hadrons that still resists a simple explanation.

Researchers in the Gluonic Excitations, or GlueX, Collaboration at Experimental Hall D were looking for Y(2175), a strangeonium candidate first reported in 2006. Instead, they found two nearby signals created when high-energy photons struck a proton target: one at about 2.24 GeV, named Y(2240), and another at about 1.82 GeV, named X(1830). Malte Albrecht, a staff scientist at Jefferson Lab, said, “We went searching for a confirmed XYZ candidate with a photon beam but instead found two other structures. It’s new information.”

The result matters because XYZ states are particles that do not fit neatly into the conventional quark model. That model, developed in the 1960s to organize the growing “particle zoo,” describes hadrons as composite particles made from quarks held together by the strong nuclear force. But after the start of the 21st century, experiments began turning up hadrons with unusual quantum properties that did not sit comfortably in that framework.

GlueX was built to investigate hybrid mesons, exotic particles in which excited gluons may contribute directly to the internal structure. In this case, the new signals may help scientists sort out the strangeonium region, where particles contain strange and anti-strange quarks. The team said the findings could help clarify how one of nature’s fundamental forces contributes to the formation of matter.

Y(2240) was detected with 5σ significance, while X(1830) reached 3σ significance. The study also sets an upper limit on how likely Y(2175) is to be produced through photoproduction, which may help guide future work. For now, the researchers say the new structures open another path into a crowded and still poorly understood part of particle physics.

Earth may have lost the Sun’s protective shield millions of years ago

What happened

Two NASA-funded studies suggest Earth’s climate may have been shaped not only by the Sun’s light, but by the Sun’s journey through the Milky Way and by violent activity from its early years, raising a new question about how the planet stayed habitable.

One study from NASA’s SHIELD center examined how the heliosphere — the Sun’s vast protective bubble — moved through the galaxy over time. Using computer simulations, researchers reconstructed the heliosphere’s past path and found that the solar system may have passed through extremely cold, dusty regions at least three times in the last several million years. The modeled encounters occurred about 2 to 3 million years ago, 6 to 7 million years ago, and 13 to 14 million years ago.

In those simulations, dense interstellar clouds pressed on the heliosphere hard enough to compress it dramatically. At times, the bubble may have shrunk to smaller than Earth’s orbit, leaving the planet outside the Sun’s protective shield. The researchers said that if that happened, Earth’s atmosphere would have been exposed to a very different interstellar environment. They also said the timing lines up with geological evidence, including interstellar dust found in deep-sea sediment cores, Antarctic snow and Moon samples from those periods.

The study suggests those galactic encounters may have influenced conditions on Earth. In the simulations, exposure to a dense, cold cloud of galactic hydrogen increased atmospheric water vapor and altered the upper atmosphere, with effects that eventually reached closer to the surface. That raises the possibility that the solar system’s passage through colder parts of the Milky Way contributed to long-term climate change, including ice ages.

A second study tackles a different puzzle: how the young Sun, which was about 70% as bright as it is today roughly 3 billion years ago, could have left Earth warm enough for liquid water. That contradiction is known as the Faint Young Sun paradox. To test one idea, NASA scientist Vladimir Airapetian and collaborators recreated early Earth-like atmospheric conditions in a sealed chamber and bombarded the gas mix with protons to mimic particles from solar superflares.

The experiment produced nitrous oxide, a greenhouse gas about 300 times more potent than carbon dioxide. The researchers found that even if only 10% of that gas survived, it could have raised temperatures near Earth’s equator to about 41 degrees Fahrenheit, or 5 degrees Celsius, enough to keep water from freezing. The study also suggests that those conditions may have favored prebiotic chemistry, offering a possible link between the young Sun, Earth’s warmth and the chemistry that preceded life.
