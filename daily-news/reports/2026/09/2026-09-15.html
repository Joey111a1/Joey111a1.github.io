Houthi attacks expose gaps in Pakistan's defence pact with Saudi Arabia

What happened

The Houthi missile and drone barrage against Saudi Arabia has put Pakistan’s new defence agreement with Saudi Arabia and Turkey under immediate scrutiny, raising a practical question: whether the pact requires military action, or whether its members can respond through diplomacy and defensive support.

The Joint Defence Agreement was signed in Mecca on 7 August, after Pakistan had presented itself as a regional mediator, including by facilitating talks between Washington and Tehran. Its central provision says that an attack on one signatory will be treated as an attack on all, prompting comparisons with NATO’s Article 5. Analysts have cautioned that the wording should not be interpreted too literally.

That commitment was tested on 8 September, when Iran-backed Houthi forces launched what was described as a large drone and missile barrage into Saudi Arabia. The strikes hit military and energy infrastructure. China’s Foreign Ministry spokesperson Guo Jiakun later said Beijing was deeply concerned about reported civilian casualties and damage to energy facilities and other civilian infrastructure. Xinhua reported that attacks on four southern Saudi cities had injured more than 70 civilians, while the Houthis said they had also suffered casualties.

A defence promise meets operational ambiguity

Neither Islamabad nor Ankara launched military counter-operations alongside Riyadh after the attacks. Instead, both relied on diplomatic statements, exposing the difference between political solidarity and direct military participation.

Pakistan’s Defence Minister Khawaja Muhammad Asif said immediately after the attack that Pakistan was “bound by the terms and conditions of this pact” and would honour them if necessary. But Foreign Ministry spokesperson Sajjad Haider Khan said on 10 September that direct military action against the Houthis was not being discussed.

“Pakistan has signed a joint defence agreement, which is a fact,” Khan said at a weekly briefing in Islamabad. “But as of now, there is no discussion of this kind.” He later described the pact as an “umbrella agreement” and said its institutional mechanisms would take time to develop.

The arrangement is therefore still a general framework rather than an integrated military command. Specific procedures for air-defence assistance, intelligence-sharing, protection of Saudi military installations and naval operations, or participation in offensive operations against the Houthis have yet to emerge. A meeting of Saudi, Pakistani and Turkish ministers and military officials in Istanbul on 31 August pledged defence-technology cooperation and sought to establish a permanent secretariat in Saudi Arabia, but Qamar Cheema of the Islamabad-based Sanober Institute said substantial work remained on inter-military planning and interoperability.

The three countries also have different security priorities. Pakistan’s principal military concern is India, alongside tensions with Afghanistan’s Taliban regime over cross-border militancy. Saudi Arabia is focused on protecting its territory, energy infrastructure and maritime routes, as well as managing threats from Iran and armed non-state groups such as the Houthis. Turkey’s security concerns extend across Syria, Iraq, the Eastern Mediterranean and the Caucasus.

Pakistan’s balance between Riyadh and Tehran

Pakistan already has a military presence in Saudi Arabia and has expanded defence cooperation with Riyadh. Under a separate Strategic Mutual Defence Agreement signed in September 2025, Saudi authorities said in April that Pakistani troops, including Pakistan Air Force fighter and support aircraft, had arrived at King Abdulaziz Air Base to support regional and international security and stability.

However, protecting Saudi territory and infrastructure is different from joining an offensive campaign against the Houthis inside Yemen. One Pakistani official familiar with Gulf affairs compared the dilemma with Islamabad’s position in 2015, when Pakistan’s parliament voted to remain neutral rather than join the Saudi-led coalition in Yemen, severely straining relations with Riyadh.

Pakistan shares a porous 900-kilometre border with Iran and routinely seeks to contain tensions with its neighbour. A Pakistani offensive against the Houthis could worsen relations with Tehran, which the source describes as the group’s primary backer, although the nature and extent of that support remain contested. It could also create new security pressures along Pakistan’s western frontier and intensify domestic sectarian sensitivities.

Cheema said attacking the Houthis was not currently an option for Pakistan. He argued that Islamabad was seeking to use dialogue to contain the crisis, particularly because disruption around the Strait of Hormuz or the Gulf of Aden could threaten maritime routes and global supply chains.

Pakistan has joined the Saudi-led Multinational Maritime Defence Alliance, launched in Riyadh on 30 July, which aims to protect shipping lanes through the Bab el-Mandeb Strait, the Red Sea and the Gulf of Aden. Fourteen countries backed the initiative, while Pakistan also participates in maritime security operations through the US-led Combined Task Force 150.

Pressure could grow if strikes escalate

The immediate priority appears to be avoiding escalation. The Pakistani official warned that further Houthi missile strikes, or mass casualties among Pakistani personnel stationed at strategic sites in Saudi Arabia, could generate public and political pressure in Islamabad to act. That would turn the pact’s current ambiguity into a more direct test.

Saudi Arabia and Pakistan also appear determined, according to experts cited in the evidence, to avoid being drawn into a wider regional war. Cheema said the alliance was intended to deter state actors and provide Saudi Arabia with “strategic depth,” rather than automatically committing its members to every offensive operation.

China called on the relevant parties to exercise restraint, reduce tensions through communication and dialogue, and respect the sovereignty and security of all countries. Guo said political and diplomatic means were the only viable way forward.

The Mecca agreement’s practical meaning will therefore depend on whether its members can develop common procedures and decide what assistance is required when one signatory is attacked. For now, Pakistan is affirming its commitment to Saudi Arabia while ruling out active discussion of direct action against the Houthis—a position that may become harder to sustain if the attacks continue or Pakistani personnel are hit.

Posterior Label Correction in Noisy and Ambiguous Preference Optimization

What happened

Boryeong Cho, Sumyeong Ahn and Se-Young Yun propose a preference-optimization method designed for language-model training data whose labels may be reversed, weak or ambiguous. Their preprint reports higher average win rates than standard and robust DPO baselines across 57 evaluation cells, while using the model’s own preference margin to decide how each pair should influence training.

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) trains a language model from pairwise comparisons, treating one response as chosen and another as rejected. The authors argue that this assumption can fail when annotators disagree, model judges favor superficial cues such as length or confident wording, or two responses are similarly good or flawed. Applying the observed label as unquestioned truth can then reinforce the wrong update direction.

The proposed Posterior Label Correction DPO (PLC-DPO) treats every observed pair as having one of three latent states: clean, where the recorded direction should be reinforced; flip, where it should be reversed; or tie, where the pair supplies too little directional evidence to justify preferring either response. The method was presented by researchers affiliated with KAIST AI and KENTECH in an arXiv preprint dated 31 August 2026.

Routing noisy pairs instead of discarding them

PLC-DPO derives its routing signal from the same policy-reference margin used by DPO, but first detaches and calibrates that margin with an exponential moving average. A strongly positive calibrated margin indicates that the current policy agrees with the observed preference; a strongly negative margin indicates disagreement; and a value near zero suggests weak evidence.

That signal produces routing weights for the clean, flip and tie actions. The clean loss applies the normal DPO direction, the flip loss applies the opposite direction, and the tie loss penalizes a large absolute margin so that the model remains neutral. The routing weights are stop-gradient assignments, preventing the policy from changing its own label assignment through the same update.

Training does not immediately rely on these corrections. PLC-DPO begins with a warm-up period, then gradually increases correction strength and gates it by routing confidence. This is intended to reduce instability while the policy-reference margin and its moving-average calibration are still developing. The resulting objective mixes ordinary DPO with the correction objective rather than replacing DPO indiscriminately.

The authors describe the routing values as differentiable action weights, not calibrated probabilities from a fully specified data-generating model. They also caution that a high flip weight is not an oracle identification of a mislabeled pair, because apparently unmarked data may contain natural annotation errors, ambiguity or stylistic artifacts.

Reported performance across models and noise settings

Across 57 dataset–model–benchmark cells, PLC-DPO achieved a mean win rate of 60.5 against corresponding DPO baselines. The next-best method in that aggregate comparison, rDPO, scored 55.5. PLC-DPO’s worst-cell result was 41.2, the second-highest worst-cell score among the robust baselines; γ-PO reached 42.5.

The experiments used Qwen2.5-1.5B, Qwen2.5-7B and Phi-2-2.7B in the main comparisons, with additional generalization tests involving Llama-3-8B and Mistral-7B. Evaluations covered UltraFeedback, AlpacaEval, AlpacaEval 2, MT-Bench, Vicuna, Evol-Instruct and HH-RLHF. On clean UltraFeedback, PLC-DPO obtained a mean win rate of 60.7 across 21 model–benchmark cells, compared with 59.6 for ROPO.

The reported gains were not limited to naturally noisy data. In controlled tests that swapped chosen and rejected responses at rates from 5% to 30%, PLC-DPO was the strongest method at every tested rate on the Qwen2.5-1.5B Vicuna evaluation. At the 30% flip rate, it recorded a 61.88 win rate against the clean one-epoch DPO baseline, while the closest listed baseline, ROPO, scored 56.88.

Additional diagnostics supported the intended separation of states. Under increasing injected corruption, the cumulative clean routing weight fell from 0.676 to 0.495, while the flip weight rose from 0.265 to 0.470. In tests inserting equal-score pairs, the advantage of PLC-DPO over same-data DPO grew from 8.3 to 18.5 points as the injected tie rate increased to 30%; at that rate, PLC-DPO scored 68.5 compared with 51.6 for RE-PO.

What the evidence does and does not establish

The study also tested a commercial-model judge, Claude Sonnet 4.6, on selected Qwen2.5-7B outputs. PLC-DPO achieved pairwise win rates of 56.75 on AlpacaEval 2 and 61.25 on Vicuna against DPO in that validation, while its single-response scores were 5.795 and 6.900 respectively.

The findings remain the authors’ reported experimental results from a preprint rather than an established consensus. The routing mechanism depends on calibration, correction schedules and confidence settings; the paper’s ablations show that removing the warm-up or confidence gate can substantially reduce performance. Its broader claim is therefore that explicitly correcting preference direction and strength can outperform simply filtering suspicious examples under the tested datasets, models and one-epoch training setup—not that every noisy preference label can be reliably identified.
