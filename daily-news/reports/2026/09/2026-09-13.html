We know how to prevent black lung, so why are workers still dying?

What happened

Silicosis, a preventable disease caused by inhaling crystalline silica dust, is again killing workers across the United States, from coalminers in central Appalachia to Latino countertop fabricators, as contested protections, workplace exposure and regulatory decisions leave known hazards in place.

The disease is one of the oldest recognized workplace hazards, and public health experts have known for decades that reducing exposure can prevent it. Yet coalminers are developing black lung at younger ages, while workers who cut, grind and polish engineered-stone countertops are suffering severe illness and dying from respiratory failure.

John Robinson, who mined coal in south-west Virginia, was diagnosed with black lung at 47. His grandfather had also developed the disease, but in an earlier era when miners could spend decades underground before diagnosis and often died in their 60s, 70s or 80s. Robinson said the growing number of younger miners with the disease showed that “something’s causing it,” adding that the scale of the outbreak had become impossible to hide.

Around 2018, doctors began documenting outbreaks among countertop fabrication workers. Seven workers were diagnosed in Colorado and another 12 cases were identified in Texas. That year, a 38-year-old Hispanic worker in California who ground stone edges at a countertop company died of respiratory failure.

Public health experts attribute both outbreaks to inhaling tiny particles of crystalline silica. Prevention depends on limiting exposure through air sampling and measures that keep dust from becoming airborne, although the controls vary by occupation. Silicosis cannot be cured, but Dr James Brandon Crum, a Kentucky radiologist who helped identify the central Appalachian black-lung epidemic, said the proposed protections for miners could prevent new cases.

Coalminers face a delayed and contested rule

The Mine Safety and Health Administration’s 2024 silica-dust rule, developed over years and adopted under the Biden administration, was intended to address the hazard in mines. It reduced the permissible exposure limit from 100 micrograms per cubic meter to 50 micrograms per cubic meter over an eight-hour shift.

Industry groups challenged the rule within months of its taking effect. The Eighth Circuit Court of Appeals stayed it after the Trump administration failed to defend it, and MSHA notified the court that it would reconsider parts affected by the challenge.

The National Stone, Sand & Gravel Association led the legal challenge. Its senior communications director, Kerry Lynch, said the organization did not oppose lowering the exposure limit but did not explain why it challenged the rule. In an earlier letter to MSHA, the association objected to frequent air sampling and medical-surveillance requirements.

The American Exploration & Mining Association also joined the petition. Its executive director, Mark Compton, said the group supported a lower silica limit but argued that the rule’s implementation restrictions could undermine miner safety. He said the standard was too inflexible for different mining operations and that administrative controls, such as job rotation, and respirators should be permitted alongside engineering controls.

The Appalachian Citizens’ Law Center said the problem was not only historical. Rebecca Shelton reviewed MSHA silica samples from January through June and found that 17% of sampled operations had concentrations above the limit in the delayed 2024 rule. Most mines with the highest readings were in central Appalachia. The coal industry has argued that new black-lung cases reflect past exposure rather than current mining conditions, but Shelton said the samples showed that dangerous exposure was happening now.

A recent National Institute for Occupational Safety and Health study found black lung in one of every three coalminers in central Appalachia, including Kentucky, Virginia and West Virginia. Crum said he had identified hundreds of additional severe cases since first warning researchers more than a decade ago. “Nothing ever gets done about it,” he said.

Engineered stone brings another outbreak

Countertop fabrication workers are covered by a 2016 Occupational Safety and Health Administration rule, but industry groups also challenged that measure, arguing that its stricter exposure limit was not based on “sound science.” David Michaels, an epidemiologist at George Washington University’s Milken Institute School of Public Health and a former assistant labor secretary for OSHA, said the regulation was not sufficient on its own.

Artificial stone slabs can contain up to 90% silica, Michaels said, and many small employers do not comply with the rule. Even full compliance, he argued, would not eliminate the problem because the product cannot be made both economically and safely.

A study published in the New England Journal of Medicine identified nearly 600 cases of silicosis among California countertop workers, including 65 people who received lung transplants and 31 who died. Michaels described the coal-mining and countertop outbreaks as tragedies.

In January, he testified before a congressional subcommittee considering lawsuits brought by workers with silicosis. The hearing focused on alleged abusive litigation against stone-slab manufacturers and distributors; its chair, Representative Darrell Issa of California, is among 16 co-sponsors of a bill that would ban lawsuits against manufacturers or sellers of certain stone-slab products.

Michaels proposed a different response: prohibiting the sale of engineered-stone products with high silica concentrations. He said substitute products with comparable uses and costs were available without the same danger to workers.

California’s OSHA began rulemaking in May to prohibit the fabrication and installation of artificial-stone products containing more than 1% crystalline silica. Stone distributor Consentino opposed the proposal, calling it inaccurate, unnecessary, infeasible and beyond the agency’s authority. The company did not provide an alternative regulatory approach.

The dispute leaves workers exposed to a hazard whose prevention is well established but whose enforcement remains contested. Industry groups are challenging rules, employers may not comply with existing requirements, and regulators are reconsidering protections as workers continue to develop and die from silicosis.

The claim came as clashes increased between the Iran-allied Houthis and forces supporting Yemen’s internationally recognised, Aden-based government. Saudi Arabia has backed that government since entering the war on its side in 2015. Yemen’s civil war began in 2014 after the Houthis captured the capital, Sanaa; fighting had largely been dormant after a 2022 ceasefire before resuming in July.

Fighting spreads along the Red Sea coast

Yemeni government forces said on Saturday that they had struck Houthi fighters in Mocha, a Red Sea city, and attacked additional Houthi positions in southwestern Yemen. On Sunday, the Yemeni military said its air force had carried out three strikes against Houthi positions and barracks in the Taiz region.

The government news agency Saba reported that forces near Mocha had destroyed vehicles and weapons and killed Houthi fighters. A military spokesman also said Houthi targets outside the city had been attacked. The Houthis gave a sharply different account of the air campaign, saying on Saturday that Saudi aircraft had carried out 129 strikes over the previous 48 hours in areas under Houthi control.

The renewed clashes followed a week-long Houthi offensive. The group said it had captured the remainder of Yemen’s Red Sea coast and several strategic islands, consolidating its position around the Bab al-Mandab Strait at the southern entrance to the Red Sea. The Houthis have also declared a maritime blockade of Saudi Arabia’s Red Sea ports and resumed attacks on ships in the waterway.

The strait has become more important to Saudi oil exports after Iran largely cut off shipments through the Gulf by blockading the Strait of Hormuz, according to the supplied account. Saudi authorities therefore face pressure both from fighting along their southern border and from the Houthis’ stated threat to maritime traffic linked to Riyadh.

Displacement and damage in Saudi Arabia

The fall of Mocha to the Houthis on Thursday prompted residents to flee toward Aden, where the internationally recognised government is based. Displaced residents described being ordered to leave quickly and struggling to find housing or shelter after arriving.

The UN migration agency said on Saturday that 76,000 people had fled the fighting since July, with the figure quadrupling in a single week. Djibouti’s economy and finance minister said about 1,400 people had crossed there. Sources on both sides put the death toll from the renewed fighting at more than 500.

Saudi authorities said on Saturday night that a Houthi projectile had fallen near the border after alerts warning of possible attacks. The civil defence directorate said two people were injured and that a mosque, buildings and vehicles suffered material damage.

The escalation has also prompted reports of pressure for a wider Saudi response. Axios reported that Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman called US President Donald Trump twice the previous week to request strikes against the Houthis, but that the request was rejected. Trump acknowledged speaking with the Saudi leader on Saturday and said the Houthis had also contacted the United States, claiming they did not want to fight with Washington. He did not explain whether the US would take further action.

The United States conducted a seven-week bombing campaign against the Houthis in 2025 over repeated attacks on Red Sea shipping in support of Hamas during the Gaza war. That campaign ended with a negotiated ceasefire, although the Houthis continued missile and drone attacks targeting Israel. Their more recent maritime blockade declaration has now tied the renewed Yemeni fighting directly to Saudi security and the movement of vessels through the Red Sea.

What happened

Saudi Arabia temporarily shut the East-West Pipeline after authorities said several drones launched from Iraq caused injuries and damage, interrupting a vital route for exporting oil to the Red Sea. The strike occurred on Thursday, the Saudi foreign ministry said, and involved multiple drones targeting sections of the pipeline in the Riyadh and Medina regions. Saudi energy officials said the route was stopped as a precaution while teams secured it and assessed its condition. The authorities did not specify how much export capacity was affected.

The 1,200-kilometre pipeline runs from Abqaiq, near the Gulf, to Yanbu on the Red Sea. It has become increasingly important because Saudi oil can reach global markets through Yanbu without passing through the Strait of Hormuz, which has been effectively closed amid the war involving the United States and Iran.

Exports through Yanbu have more than doubled since the war began, according to the International Energy Agency. One account said the pipeline had been carrying between 4 million and 5 million barrels of oil per day, equivalent to about 4% to 5% of global supply. Another account said Saudi Arabia had rerouted about 5 million barrels per day toward Yanbu. The closure’s precise effect on exports remained unclear.

Responsibility remains disputed

Saudi Arabia identified Iraq as the launch point but did not name the group responsible. The Islamic Resistance in Iraq, an umbrella group of Iran-backed militias, denied involvement. Iraq’s government condemned the attack, said its territory had been used to launch it and ordered an investigation. It also said Iraqi land and airspace would not be allowed to serve as a platform for attacks on neighbouring countries.

On Saturday, Iraqi authorities said they had closed several borders as a precaution. The Iraqi government also dismissed the commander responsible for the southern province of Maysan, while another account said the province’s police chief was removed and its main border crossing was closed. The measures placed additional pressure on Baghdad, which maintains political and military ties with both Washington and Tehran.

Trump said in Dublin on Saturday that Iran was “probably” behind the drone attack. He said he had spoken with Saudi Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman, whom he described as a “good friend”. However, responsibility remained unresolved, and the Iraqi militia umbrella group rejected involvement.

Saudi Arabia said it reserved the right to take all necessary measures to protect its sovereignty, security, facilities and people. Bahrain, Jordan and Qatar condemned the attack, while the Gulf Cooperation Council called it a dangerous escalation and a threat to Saudi security.

A vulnerable alternative to the Strait of Hormuz

The pipeline’s importance has grown as threats have spread across the region’s energy and shipping routes. The East-West route offers Saudi Arabia a way to move crude to the Red Sea without sending tankers through the Strait of Hormuz. The pipeline therefore serves as an alternative not only to a blocked maritime passage but also to routes exposed to attacks in the Red Sea.

The attack came as Yemen’s Iran-backed Houthis expanded their presence along the Red Sea coast. The Houthis were reported to have captured the strategic island of Perim in the Bab al-Mandab Strait, a waterway linking Europe and Asia, and had announced a maritime blockade against Saudi Arabia. They also attacked Saudi-linked oil tankers in the Red Sea.

As separate background, Houthi fighters said on Sunday that they had used drones and missiles against a military base at Sharurah in southern Saudi Arabia. That claim concerned a different attack and came amid renewed fighting between the Houthis and Yemen’s Saudi-backed government forces. The date of the military-base operation was not specified.

The overlapping threats have unsettled energy markets. Oil prices rose above $100 a barrel during the week, while average US diesel prices exceeded $6 per gallon. Any extended interruption to the East-West Pipeline could further restrict Saudi export options, although officials had not announced the duration of the shutdown or the resulting volume loss.

Pressure on Riyadh and Washington

The pipeline attack added to Saudi Arabia’s security concerns as it supports Yemen’s internationally recognised government against the Houthis. A source cited in the accounts said the crown prince spoke twice with Trump on Thursday and urged the United States to strike the Houthis, but Trump declined. Another account said Washington would provide intelligence while avoiding direct military intervention for the time being.

Trump said the United States was in contact with Saudi Arabia and focused on protecting freedom of navigation in the Red Sea while leaving regional partners to manage their own security challenges. Asked whether he could help restore traffic through the Strait of Hormuz, he said that “everything will work out just fine.” No concrete US military action against the Houthis was reported.

The immediate next steps concern both the pipeline and the investigation in Iraq. Saudi teams were assessing the route’s safety, while Baghdad was investigating those responsible. Until responsibility is established, the attack remains a contested episode involving Saudi infrastructure, Iraqi territory and the wider confrontation among Iran, the United States and their regional partners.

What happened

A regional train carrying 186 passengers from Rouen to Caen derailed near Cléon in Normandy on Friday evening, injuring 44 people, including one woman reported to be in critical condition. French investigators are examining whether a fragment of rail found on the track points to sabotage or an accidental cause.

The derailment occurred at about 7:45 p.m. local time near a Renault factory in Cléon, in the Seine-Maritime department of northwestern France. The train was travelling between the Normandy cities when it left the tracks. An 18-year-old woman was evacuated by helicopter to Rouen University Hospital; the Rouen prosecutor said she was among those injured and in critical condition.

Why sabotage is being investigated

A fragment of rail was found on the track after the derailment. A spokeswoman for the French national police communications service, known as SICOP, said it was “probably” connected to the incident. A police source said the possibility of a malicious act had therefore become the main line of inquiry.

Investigators were still trying to establish whether the circumstances were accidental or involved sabotage. The prosecutor’s office remained cautious about drawing conclusions, according to police. The train driver’s drug and alcohol tests were negative, the prosecutor said.

The authorities’ response included a mass-casualty plan, with 130 firefighters, five medical teams and 80 vehicles deployed. Passengers who escaped unharmed were taken to a room provided by the mayor of Cléon while emergency operations continued.

Disruption on the regional rail network

SNCF Réseau, which controls France’s rail network, suspended traffic on the line and cut power to the tracks to allow emergency services to work. The derailment caused major disruptions in both directions between Rouen and Caen, and between Caen and Le Havre.

Services later resumed, but SNCF Voyageurs arranged replacement buses on the Rouen-Caen route. The line normally carries 10 return services per day and six at weekends. The derailed train was to remain in place until the public prosecutor authorized its removal, while the investigation into the cause continued. SNCF said it would cooperate fully and transparently with the inquiry.

Passenger account and investigation still open

One passenger, 24-year-old Louis, told ICI Normandie radio that the train first experienced several jolts, followed about three seconds later by increasingly forceful movement. He said he could no longer stay in his seat, windows partly shattered and track stones entered the carriage.

The investigation comes against the background of earlier serious rail accidents in France. A derailment at Brétigny-sur-Orge, south of Paris, killed seven people in July 2013, while an experimental high-speed TGV derailed near Eckwersheim, close to Strasbourg, in November 2015, killing 11. Those incidents provide context for the attention on the Cléon derailment, but authorities have not established that sabotage caused this one.

What happened

A preprint from researchers at the University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign and Amazon proposes World Model RL (WMRL), a way to train automatic research agents more cheaply by simulating experiments rather than running every candidate solution on real hardware, while using limited real executions to correct simulation errors.

Automatic research agents are language models that formulate research ideas, implement experiments, analyze results and iterate. Scaling their reinforcement-learning training requires many online trajectories, but each candidate solution must run in an isolated sandbox with data and real GPUs. The paper argues that this environment execution becomes the main cost and bottleneck as the number of trajectories grows, even though agent generation can share computation through batching.

WMRL replaces most real experiment execution with a world model that predicts the result of an agent’s proposed solution. The researchers model these predictions as containing two types of error: systematic bias, which shifts scores away from the true result, and zero-mean noise, which adds random variation. Because relying entirely on the simulated signal could leave a persistent error floor, WMRL uses two corrections: Online Debiasing recalibrates predictions against a small stream of real executions, while Inverse-Variance Denoising combines the simulated and real signals to reduce variance. The paper says these mechanisms have a strictly improved convergence guarantee in its theoretical analysis.

In practice, the real-execution “anchor groups” account for about 10% of all groups. Their paired real and predicted scores are used to fit a monotone recalibration function and to monitor how far the world model’s predictions have drifted from actual results. This preserves some costly ground-truth execution while allowing most training signals to come from the faster model.

Experiments covered Qwen3.5-4B and Qwen3.5-9B agents on MLE-Dojo and DSBench, as well as the MiniVLA-1B vision-language-action model on robotic tasks using the Robometer world model. The paper reports that WMRL reduced training compute by roughly three to four times while matching or exceeding reinforcement learning based entirely on real execution. On the reported benchmarks, the post-trained 4B and 9B agents also surpassed larger open-weight comparison agents of 48B and 120B.

These findings are claims from the arXiv preprint, dated 10 September 2026, rather than results established through peer review. The paper’s own formulation also makes clear that world-model bias and noise remain central technical problems; WMRL is designed to mitigate them rather than eliminate the need for real execution.

What happened

Robotic manipulation systems can lose information needed for later actions when it disappears from the current camera view, while storing every past observation makes execution slower and more memory-intensive. MaP-WAM proposes using long-term visual history to make compact plans, allowing robots to retain relevant context without feeding the full history into every control step.

The framework, introduced in *Memory as Plans: World-Action Modeling with Memory-Grounded Planning*, addresses manipulation tasks that are not adequately described by a Markovian assumption. In such tasks, the current observation or a short recent history may not reveal information needed for a future decision. The authors identify a trade-off in existing approaches: language summaries are compact but can discard fine-grained visual and spatial evidence, while growing visual windows preserve more history at the cost of increasing inference latency and GPU-memory use.

MaP-WAM stores completed task segments as episodic records containing their language instructions and sparse visual context, including a few frames from each segment. At each planning stage, a language planner predicts the next segment-level plan. A causal world model then uses the accumulated visual context and that plan to produce corresponding visual guidance. The two outputs form a memory-grounded language-visual plan that captures both the intended action stage and the expected visual evolution.

Progress-aware execution

Execution is handled by a World-Action-Progress (WAP) model. Rather than assuming that every segment lasts a fixed time, it jointly predicts action chunks, future visual dynamics and progress through the current plan. The predicted progress allows the system to determine when a segment has been completed and when to generate the next plan. MaP-WAM also aligns the robot’s current observation with states along the visual plan, using the best match to calibrate progress and reduce accumulated drift during long executions. New observations are sparsely added to episodic memory after a segment transition.

The authors report that the executor keeps a fixed context length, while structured attention enables key-value caching in both planning and execution. They report an 83.3% success rate on RMBench and 78.0% on real-robot tasks, with approximately constant executor inference latency as task history grows. What happened

Optical-flow estimation has often depended on specialized mechanisms for matching motion between frames; FreeFlow proposes replacing those components with a hierarchical transformer and a single feed-forward encoder–decoder, while reporting leading benchmark results and memory-efficient 1080p inference.

The work is presented in an arXiv preprint dated September 10, 2026, by researchers affiliated with the AI Center at Lomonosov Moscow State University and the MSU Institute for Artificial Intelligence. Optical flow is the dense, per-pixel motion field between two images, used in applications including video understanding, tracking, restoration and synthesis.

Modern high-performing systems commonly use task-specific inductive biases such as correlation or cost volumes, feature warping, pyramids, iterative refinement and specialized upsampling. These mechanisms can improve accuracy, but the authors argue that they also produce complex, hard-wired pipelines that are more difficult to modify, scale or reuse beyond optical flow. FreeFlow is intended to test whether a general-purpose, data-driven architecture can achieve comparable or better results without those components.

A transformer without flow-specific modules

FreeFlow uses a single end-to-end trainable, feed-forward encoder–decoder. It does not use explicit cost or correlation volumes, feature warping, iterative update stages, convex upsampling or a specialized recurrent flow head. Instead, paired images are split into non-overlapping 8×8 patches and processed by two shared-weight, Siamese transformer encoders. A decoder then alternates self-attention with cross-attention between the two views to produce flow tokens, which are converted into a dense optical-flow field by a lightweight prediction head.

The architecture is hierarchical because it combines three attention patterns. Window attention processes local regions; shifted-window attention moves information across window boundaries; and global attention operates after 2×2 downsampling to capture longer-range correspondence at lower computational cost. The resulting design repeatedly combines local detail, cross-region communication and global context rather than leaving different image tiles to interact only at a late stage.

The authors describe this arrangement as particularly suited to high-resolution processing. The model uses Rotary Positional Embedding and an attention scaling factor based on token count to support inference at resolutions higher than those used during training. Its final head directly predicts two flow channels and three uncertainty parameters, without iterative refinement or convex upsampling.

Training and reported benchmark results

Training begins with cross-view completion pretraining. One image view is heavily masked, and the model reconstructs the missing content using the second view, encouraging it to learn dense correspondences. The pretraining data consists of image pairs from ARKitScenes, MegaDepth and 3DStreetView, totaling 3.7 million samples. The model is then fine-tuned for optical flow using data from TartanAir, Sintel, Things, KITTI-2015 and HD1K, with separate fine-tuning stages for Sintel, KITTI-2015 and Spring.

The paper evaluates FreeFlow on Spring, a high-resolution real-world benchmark; Sintel, which contains synthetic scenes with complex motion and rendering effects; and KITTI-2015, which focuses on real driving scenes. The reported results improve as the model scales from the small (S) to medium (M) and large (L) variants.

On Sintel, FreeFlow-L records an endpoint error (EPE) of 0.68 on the Clean split and 1.48 on the Final split. On KITTI-2015, it reports a 3.23 Fl-all outlier rate. On Spring, it reports 3.192 for the 1-pixel outlier metric, along with an EPE of 0.278 and an Fl score of 1.048. The authors describe these results as state of the art on the three benchmarks. They also report that FreeFlow-L improves EPE by 9% and reduces Fl by 14% against WAFT-DAv2-a2 on Spring, while remaining competitive in other Spring measures and achieving the best WAUC among the two-frame methods shown in their comparison.

The largest FreeFlow model has 607 million parameters and, in the paper’s Spring comparison, uses 2.58 GB of GPU memory at 1080p on an Nvidia RTX 3090. Its listed runtime is 230.72 milliseconds under the stated evaluation setup. The paper argues that the model benefits from efficient standard attention kernels, whereas flow-specific modules require additional task-specific engineering for comparable efficiency.

What the evidence establishes

The paper’s ablations report that larger FreeFlow variants deliver consistent accuracy gains, while smaller versions retain strong performance. They also indicate that global attention helps with large displacements, while local attention handles smaller shifts. A comparison with vanilla vision transformers found higher prediction error and substantially slower processing for the vanilla models under the tested setup.

These findings are claims from an arXiv preprint rather than peer-reviewed results. The work therefore presents an architecture, training procedure and benchmark evaluation whose reported significance rests on the authors’ experiments. Its central proposition is that optical-flow accuracy can be obtained from a high-resolution hierarchical transformer without the conventional flow-specific machinery that has shaped most recent pipelines.

The benchmark therefore separates recognizing plausible content from locating it precisely in time.

What happened

Mercury’s surface contains far less silicon dioxide than previously estimated, suggesting that its volcanic rocks formed from deeply melted mantle material at exceptionally high temperatures. The finding offers a new clue to how the smallest planet developed its crust and why its geological history diverged so sharply from Earth’s.

Researchers from the Max Planck Institute for Solar System Research and the Universities of Münster and Göttingen estimate that silicon dioxide accounts for about 37 percent of Mercury’s surface material by mass—up to 25 percent below earlier estimates. The study was published in *Planetary Research*.

Mercury appears to have cooled relatively quickly after forming. Volcanic activity may have largely stopped roughly one billion years later, leaving a solid, continuous crust. Earth never reached the same state: its crust remains active through volcanism and plate tectonics, while Mercury has been largely frozen in place for billions of years. Scientists are still trying to determine how Mercury’s early volcanic processes produced the planet seen today.

The chemical result points to an unusually hot interior. As a planet’s molten mantle cools, the first rocks to crystallize contain relatively little silicon dioxide, while the compound becomes concentrated in the remaining melt. Lava reaching the surface later would therefore normally contain more of it. The researchers say Mercury’s low surface concentration could mean its volcanic rocks came from deeper mantle regions that underwent more extensive melting at very high temperatures. They also identify another possibility: Mercury’s crust may once have contained more silicon dioxide but gradually lost oxygen.

Because no rock has been collected from Mercury and no lander has operated there, the team relied on infrared observations. To calibrate the relationship between infrared signals and composition, researchers produced laboratory glass beads about half a millimeter wide, each containing a precisely controlled amount of silicon dioxide. They measured the beads’ infrared properties and used them as reference standards.

The technique was first tested on the Moon, where remote-sensing data from NASA’s Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter could be compared with lunar samples returned by crewed and uncrewed missions. After the results matched known lunar compositions, the researchers applied the method to Earth-based infrared observations of Mercury, including measurements from the Bok Telescope at Steward Observatory in Arizona.

The estimate could be tested more precisely by ESA and JAXA’s BepiColombo mission. Its MERTIS instrument, developed under the leadership of DLR with the University of Münster’s Institute for Planetology, is expected to collect higher-resolution infrared measurements of Mercury. Those observations could clarify the planet’s silicon dioxide content and the extreme conditions that shaped its crust billions of years ago.

Millions of people tackle Wordle’s hidden five-letter word with six guesses, but researchers at Binghamton University, State University of New York, say a strategy based on information theory can solve 99% of puzzles in simulations—by prioritizing useful clues over immediately guessing the answer.

The team, led by Assistant Professor Congyu “Peter” Wu, treated each Wordle guess as a way to reduce uncertainty. After every attempt, the game’s color-coded feedback narrows the possible answers. The researchers applied Shannon entropy, a mathematical measure of uncertainty, to identify the guesses expected to eliminate the greatest number of possibilities.

That approach can favor a word that is unlikely to be the solution. Its value may instead come from testing a combination of letters that divides the remaining answers into smaller groups. As Wu explained, some guesses can put the player on a faster information-gathering path even when they are not the most likely answers.

In simulations, the information-theory strategy solved 99% of Wordle puzzles, compared with 90% for a more conventional method focused on frequently used letters such as A, E and R. The strategy continually changes according to what previous guesses have revealed, rather than relying only on general letter frequency.

Using it during an actual game would require a separate script or program. After each guess, the player would enter Wordle’s color feedback, and the software would calculate the next guess expected to provide the most information.

The work began as a class assignment in which Wu asked students to apply information theory to a real-world problem. It later became the paper “Solving Wordle Using Information Theory,” published in the *Northeast Journal of Complex Systems*.
