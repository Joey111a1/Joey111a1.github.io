On the ground in China’s wiped-out border zone, the fate of hundreds is still unknown
global_front_pageglobal_south_and_exploitedpolitics_and_powerclimate_disasterpublic_healtheconomy_and_businessmaterial_harmmajor supplementregional expansionanchor
What happened

Gyirong Port on the China-Nepal border has been reduced to stones, gravel and sludge after an August 26 mudslide and flood, and 43 people were confirmed dead while 519 remained missing as rescuers kept searching for those still unaccounted for.

A border crossing erased in hours

Before the disaster, Gyirong was a busy gateway for tourists and traders moving between China and Nepal, with hundreds of people typically passing through each day to or from Nepal’s Rasuwa port across the river. That changed when the port was engulfed by a huge wave of water, which the reporting described as the first point of impact from a landslide and explosive flooding that then tore into Nepal.

By the time a CNN team reached the site on Sunday on a government-organized visit, the high-altitude hub had been almost completely washed away. Reporters described a moonscape of stones, gravel and sludge that emergency workers were still searching, along with hard hats and boots issued because the ground was still unstable and the risk of fresh landslides remained. A five-story checkpoint building that once dominated the area, along with travel agencies, a supermarket and a bridge linking China and Nepal, had been obliterated. A memorial of about 100 yellow flowers had been left for the dead.

Searching through mud, dust and collapse

The rescue work has been slowed by how much of the surrounding terrain was destroyed. The road to the port was completely wrecked and later covered with gravel so it could be used again, while the area itself was filled with heavy dust and fog. Rescue vehicles lined the site and officials directed traffic as teams worked in conditions described by one commander as the hardest part of the response.

Yang Qiqiao, commander of the ground assault team, said he had been working there since August 28, two days after the disaster. He said rescuers had to reach the core area on foot because the road had been washed away, forcing them to trek through the mountains and across them. Xinhua said a three-kilometer stretch of the G216 national highway near the border crossing was completely destroyed, blocking access to the core area until the road was cleared on Wednesday, seven days after the disaster, and thousands of rescue workers and heavy machinery could enter.

Searchers from the Chinese report said the operation continued along multiple routes: rescue boats searched the river, ground teams moved through rugged paths, firefighters rappelled from a village on the east side, and helicopters carried in rescuers, equipment and supplies. When heavy machinery reached areas where victims were thought to be buried, operators slowed down and rescuers with hand tools moved in carefully. In the People’s Daily account, workers poured a cup of liquor onto the ground before hand-digging and said softly, “We’re here to take you home.”

Families are still waiting for names

No survivors have been found at the port, which has become a focus for families in China and elsewhere who are desperate for information about relatives they believe were there when the disaster struck. Chinese authorities had not publicly released a list of names of people who were at the port or who were believed missing on that side of the border, and officials in Gyirong town faced repeated questions from international media about how the missing were being identified and how information would be shared.

The gap in information has stood out because updates in Nepal have been more frequent, according to the CNN account. At the same time, officials said more than 60 specialists and three laboratories were working on identifying bodies recovered during excavation. The People’s Daily report also showed the human scale of the loss from the site itself: a Chinese flag stood above the ruins, with bouquets at its base, while the joint inspection building that had once been there had vanished from sight.

The toll and the unresolved counts

The casualty figures have been updated as the search continued. Local emergency response and rescue authorities said 43 people had been confirmed dead and 519 remained missing as of 6 p.m. Saturday, according to reports carried on Sunday. Officials also told a press conference that 261 foreign nationals from 23 countries were among the missing. In an earlier account published on Friday evening, People’s Daily said the mudslide had claimed 31 lives and left 531 others missing.

The wider response has included a large deployment of personnel and equipment. Xinhua said China had sent more than 2,500 rescue workers and over 500 vehicles, carried out more than 1,000 drone flights and air-dropped 5.43 tonnes of supplies. Local authorities also said they had assisted 1,720 Nepalese border residents in Gyirong, while many Nepalese nationals who worked or did business there were stranded after the border port was destroyed. Officials said China has been giving Nepal daily updates on meteorological, hydrological and glacier data through a disaster information notification mechanism.

What remains unresolved is the same question that has driven the search from the start: who was at the checkpoint when the mudslide hit, and where are they now. Rescuers were still working through the ruins layer by layer, and the latest official numbers showed that the search was far from over.

At least 10 killed in central Mexico fireworks blast during festival

What happened

At least 10 people were killed and dozens injured when fireworks detonated during a patron saint festival in Temascalcingo, central Mexico, leaving the toll and the cause unclear as authorities opened an investigation and families searched through smoke and debris.

Fireworks effigy turns deadly

The blast struck on Saturday night in Santa Maria Canchesda, in the Temascalcingo municipality, next to the Nuestra Senora de la Luz church. Video showed revellers carrying a straw bull effigy with fireworks spouting from it, and AFP reported that the celebration was part of a local ritual.

What was meant to be part of the religious display appears to have escalated quickly. The burning bull apparently ignited a larger store of fireworks inside the village church, and the explosion knocked down walls and set a fire in the town square, according to the reporting cited in the ledger. Local media said some people were trapped under falling debris.

Hundreds of people were preparing to watch the fireworks display tied to the festival, South China Morning Post reported. The municipality is about 160km northwest of Mexico City, and the scene was one of smoke, rubble and people trying to understand who had been caught in the blast.

The casualty count was still shifting

The deadliest confirmed figure was consistent across the evidence: at least 10 people were killed. The number of wounded varied slightly between reports, with the local government cited by Al Jazeera English saying at least 60 were injured, while Mexican authorities cited by South China Morning Post put the number at 64.

The diocese of the nearby municipality of Atlacomulco said two priests were injured but are expected to survive. In a social media statement, the diocese said it was praying for those who died, their families, the injured and others experiencing “moments of pain and worry” after the explosion.

A local bishop also said two priests were among the injured. Social media video from the aftermath showed dozens of people walking through smoke and debris in search of survivors, underscoring how quickly the festive gathering turned into an emergency response scene.

Cause unknown, with an investigation open

Mexico’s Attorney General’s Office said the cause of the explosion has not been determined and that an investigation has opened. South China Morning Post also reported that the Mexico State Attorney General’s Office had opened an investigation. The evidence does not identify what sparked the initial detonation beyond the fireworks already in use during the festival.

The incident fits a pattern of dangerous pyrotechnic accidents in Mexico, where fireworks are commonly used in street celebrations and religious festivals. Al Jazeera English noted that fireworks mishaps have previously been deadly in the country, including an explosion in July 2018 that killed 24 people at a fireworks factory in Tultepec, north of Mexico City, and another deadly blast two years earlier in a market in the same city that killed 42.

What happened

The far-right Alternative for Germany surged to 44% in Saxony-Anhalt on Sunday, putting it on the brink of power in the eastern state while leaving open the question of whether it can assemble the votes to govern.

The result was a record for the AfD in a state election and more than doubled its 20.8% share in 2021. It finished well ahead of the center-right CDU, which was on about 17% to 18.5% depending on the projection, and marked the AfD’s strongest regional performance yet.

Ulrich Siegmund, the AfD’s 35-year-old lead candidate, celebrated the result as historic. Exit polls and interim counts suggested the party had emerged far ahead of its mainstream rivals, but not necessarily with a clean path to a governing majority.

The key uncertainty is arithmetic. BSW, the small populist party that has not ruled out working with the AfD, barely cleared the 5% threshold for parliamentary representation. Together, AfD and BSW would hold 43 seats in the 83-seat parliament, just one more than a majority, according to one count. But that may not translate into a workable coalition.

BSW co-chair Amira Mohamed Ali said on Sunday that her party would neither vote for Siegmund nor for CDU Premier Sven Schulze. She instead said she wanted a nonpartisan premier who would govern with BSW and AfD — a formula that would still require AfD support. Siegmund rejected that idea.

The vote carried wider political weight because no far-right party has governed a German state since World War II. Saxony-Anhalt, with a population of about 2.1 million, has also seen its AfD branch classified by domestic intelligence as right-wing extremist, a label Siegmund rejects.

The party’s leaders said the result showed growing strength well beyond a protest vote. But whether that strength becomes actual power depends on the final seat count and on whether any other parties are willing to work with it, something the CDU has already refused to do.

What happens next

Justice Ketanji Brown Jackson set Wednesday as the deadline for responses to the administration’s request. The dispute remains live as ballots are already being mailed in some states, including North Carolina, and more states are due to begin mailing them, with Alabama set for September 9 and at least five states in the week of September 13.

AI can now control fusion plasma faster than humans can react
science_cs_aiscience_generalglobal_south_and_exploitedpublic_healthclimate_disasterscience anchorscience_journalism
What happened

Researchers at Princeton say an AI framework can now steer fusion plasma in real time, acting faster than a human operator while keeping safety limits in place, after tests on a live tokamak showed it can handle changes that unfold in milliseconds.

The system, called PACMAN, was developed by researchers at the U.S. Department of Energy’s Princeton Plasma Physics Laboratory and Princeton University. It is designed for fusion devices such as tokamaks, which use strong magnetic fields to confine plasma, the electrically charged gas that must stay hot, dense and stable for fusion to continue. That stability is difficult to maintain because small instabilities can grow within milliseconds, while the plasma behavior is too fast for conventional human response and too slow for standard simulations to guide a live experiment.

PACMAN, short for Prediction And Control using MAchiNe learning, tries to bridge that gap by combining several machine-learning models in one control loop. The framework collects measurements from the tokamak, checks and packages the data, uses AI models to estimate what the plasma is doing or likely to do next, and then has controllers choose actions such as changing heating power. A final stage resolves conflicting instructions, applies hardware safety limits, and sends approved commands to the machine. The researchers said people still set the experiment’s goals and review results afterward.

Tested on DIII-D

The team tested PACMAN in five experiments on the DOE’s DIII-D National Fusion Facility tokamak in San Diego. In one tearing-mode test, a machine-learning model predicted an instability about 200 milliseconds before it would normally be visible to conventional controllers, giving the system time to adjust the plasma before the disturbance fully developed. In another demonstration, PACMAN coordinated all six of DIII-D’s gyrotrons at once, adjusting both power and mirror positions to meet predefined targets.

The researchers said the framework also sped up development itself: once the first model was installed, adding a second took only a few days rather than months. They described PACMAN as a modular system that could let different AI components work together without rewriting the whole control setup, potentially making it easier to adapt the approach to other fusion machines. Its design and initial results are reported in *Nuclear Fusion*.
