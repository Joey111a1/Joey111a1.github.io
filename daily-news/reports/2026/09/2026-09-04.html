What happened

Southern Lebanon’s border strip has become a landscape of Israeli flags, checkpoints and flattened villages, while residents of a few mostly Christian towns are allowed back only in escorted convoys, raising the question of whether a ceasefire and withdrawal will ever materialize.

A border road changed by occupation

Driving south along the coast, the Lebanese and Hezbollah flags that line the road suddenly give way to Israeli flags. Soon an Israeli checkpoint appears, with soldiers watching from the balconies of homes riddled with shrapnel. Beyond that, village after village has been reduced to rubble.

The territory being crossed is a band about 10 kilometers (6.2 miles) deep that the Israeli military has occupied to create what it calls a “security zone” for Israel’s northern residents. Israeli Defense Minister Israel Katz vowed to destroy all homes in villages near the border, and the destruction on the ground now matches that promise: most villages have been systematically flattened, and residents of the few Christian villages still standing say they hear daily controlled demolitions in nearby communities.

According to the Lebanese Ministry of Health, more than 4,300 people have been killed by Israeli strikes since March 2, and the United Nations says more than 350,000 remain displaced since Israel’s occupation began.

Three villages still standing, but cut off

CNN gained rare access by joining a convoy of residents permitted to move through the occupied territory to reach homes in three predominantly Christian villages: Rmeish, Ain Ebel and Debel. They have been spared the fate of surrounding Shia Muslim villages, which now resemble a wasteland, but their survival has come with isolation.

Residents of the three villages are cut off from the rest of Lebanon and cannot enter or leave without joining a UN-led convoy. Caritas, the Catholic non-profit that helps organize some of the trips, must request permission for each resident from the Israeli military through a US-led monitoring mechanism. Approvals usually come, but the convoys can be delayed or canceled, and the journey can take anywhere from a couple of hours to a full day.

That uncertainty shapes family life. Ronnie and Rita Alam fled Rmeish with their two daughters in March as the bombardment intensified. They rented a home in Beirut, straining their finances, then returned to Rmeish only after four months apart. Rita said she was glad to go home but upset that her husband would still have to live in Beirut for work, saying their family was being split apart.

Inside Ain Ebel, Yorgos Mitri described a different kind of survival: living in a village ringed by destroyed towns, where even his 2-year-old son recognizes the sound of fighter jets. He said he stays because he fears not being able to return, and because he sees little point in leaving a home that has already been surrounded by ruin.

Pilot zones, stalled talks and a fragile return

The Lebanese government has little ability to operate inside the occupied zone. Under a US-led framework agreement signed in June, Israel is meant to hand over small pockets of territory to the Lebanese army as so-called “pilot zones,” but there is little sign the plan is moving as intended.

The first pilot zones began in three villages that are all Shia Muslim majority, though none had been fully occupied by Israeli forces to begin with. In one of them, Zawtar al-Gharbiyeh, the Lebanese Armed Forces were shown trying to restore state authority: preventing armed non-state actors such as Hezbollah from entering, confiscating weapons and remnants of war, and preparing for displaced residents to return.

But the army is working with limited resources. Col. Raja Amer, assistant to the commander of the South Litani sector, said soldiers earn an average of $500 a month and often rely on outdated or scarce equipment. He said the army has recorded more than 5,000 Israeli attacks on Lebanon since the framework agreement was signed in June, and the Lebanese army says 34 soldiers were killed in 2026 alone.

The ceasefire also looks fragile from the ground. Amer said Israeli shelling continued even as soldiers spoke near Mansouri, and Israeli tanks and bulldozers remain visible only a short distance from Zawtar al-Gharbiyeh. Negotiations between Israel and Lebanon have stalled, and Israel’s defense minister has said the military would not withdraw from Lebanon even if the United States demanded it. For residents who have returned, the damage is already personal and permanent: Mahmoud Darwish came back to find rubble up to his knees, soot on the walls and the olive trees that had sustained him destroyed.

Judge rules 2,000 Catalina Island deer can be killed to save native plant species

What happened

A county judge has cleared the Catalina Island Conservancy to kill nearly all of the island’s 2,000 mule deer, escalating an emotional fight over a beloved population that supporters say is harming native plants and increasing wildfire danger.

For nearly a century, Catalina’s deer have been part of everyday life on Santa Catalina Island, with locals and tourists often spotting them around waterfronts, woodlands and even downtown Avalon. Now that familiar scene is set to change after Thursday’s ruling denied opponents’ request for a preliminary injunction and allowed the conservancy to move ahead.

Why the deer are being targeted

The island’s deer are non-native. Hunters first introduced mule deer to Catalina for sport about a century ago, and what began as a small population grew to a few thousand. The conservancy, which controls 88% of the island off the southern California coast, has been trying for years to eliminate them.

Statewide experts have backed the proposal, saying the deer have been eating a native shrub plant in ways that have helped invasive grasses spread. Those grasses ignite faster and carry fire more easily, adding to the island’s ecological problem. Anna L. Jacobsen, a plant biologist at California State University, Bakersfield, said the pattern is especially troubling when non-native deer eat shrubs already under stress from climate change, because native plants may not recover fast enough and can become locally extinct.

Jacobsen said the damaged habitat is then replaced by invasive grasses that burn faster than native species. In southern California, where wildfire seasons have grown more dangerous, Max Moritz, a wildfire specialist who advises California state and local governments, said prevention has shifted toward focusing on ignitions rather than fuel reduction alone.

The conservancy also said it explored other options. According to its director of communications, Pepe Barton, it examined contraception and sterilization but found them infeasible for such a large population. Barton said fencing would not be approved, four decades of regular hunting seasons had not reduced the herd enough, and introducing new predators would create new risks to native animals and local people.

That left, in Barton’s words, total extermination.

The fight over a familiar animal

The prospect of killing so many deer has triggered fierce and emotional opposition. Animal activists and hunters formed an unlikely coalition and tried to block the culling in court. An online petition against the move gathered more than 32,000 signatures.

Los Angeles County supervisor Janice Hahn, who has criticized the plan, asked Governor Gavin Newsom to intervene earlier this week, but that effort did not stop the judge’s ruling. On Thursday, the county judge issued a final ruling denying the preliminary injunction filed by opponents.

The issue has been especially difficult because the deer are so visible and so closely associated with the island. Barton said the conservancy’s decision was “never our first choice” and called it “a necessary step to protect the island, the native plants and animals that are only found here, as well as the residents and livelihoods that depend on Catalina.”

Max Moritz said the debate has become complicated because “people feel some strong attachment to the deer” and it can be difficult to see the science behind the decision. He added that, from a scientific, ecological and fire perspective, removal is the best way.

What the conservancy says happens next

The conservancy said it will now move forward with a five-year plan to eliminate the island’s deer population. Barton said an estimated 200 deer will be hunted with rifles over the next two months. Island residents will be allowed one last hunting season from late October to mid-December this year.

Only a small population near Avalon will be sterilized. The rest are to be killed, according to the plan approved in January by the California Department of Fish and Wildlife, after the conservancy filed for a restoration management permit.

Barton said the meat from the deer will go to the California condor recovery program or local tribal groups. He also said carcasses near trails or roads will be removed, while those in steep, dangerous terrain will be left to decompose naturally. People traveling or hiking on the island, he said, will not regularly see deer carcasses.

Formally known as Santa Catalina Island, the island is the last leg of the Channel Islands to remove non-native deer species.

The ballot rule Trump ordered

The fight stems from an executive order Trump signed in March that tightened federal control over mail-in voting. The order directed the Postal Service to check ballots against a federally approved voter roll before sending them, a shift from the agency’s traditional role of delivering ballots supplied by the states.

The USPS plan described in the filings would go further still. It would use voter-specific data and ballot-envelope standards, including an online portal that would track millions of mail ballots. Under the rule, the Postal Service could refuse to send ballots that do not meet the new standards or that are tied to voters who do not appear on the lists provided.

Supporters of the restriction, including the administration in its filing, say it is an “important federal policy to protect the mails from being used to commit voter fraud.” Critics, by contrast, say the changes could disrupt thousands of legitimate votes as November 3 approaches. Talwani has already tried twice to stop the system. In June she barred the Trump administration from using it before the midterm election. Last week she then set a 14-day temporary restraining order to prevent implementation while she considered the broader case.

The Supreme Court in late August lifted her June order, saying the challenge had come too early. That left room for another legal fight, which is now back before the justices as the administration seeks to move ahead and Talwani considers whether to keep the ban in place.

The Justice Department first asked the Boston-based 1st US Circuit Court of Appeals to pause Talwani’s latest order, but it did not wait for a ruling before going to the Supreme Court.
