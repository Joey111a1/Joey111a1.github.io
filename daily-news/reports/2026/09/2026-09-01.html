Iran’s economy in crisis as US launches new sanctions campaign
global_front_pageglobal_south_and_exploitedpolitics_and_powereconomy_and_businessmaterial_harmanchor
What happened

Iranians are facing record inflation, a plunging currency and fuel shortages just as the US launches Operation Economic Outcast, deepening a crisis that many households were already living through and sharpening the question of how far the country can absorb more economic pressure.

The new sanctions campaign lands in a country where the currency has kept sliding and basic goods have become far more expensive. On 23 August, the US dollar reached roughly 2 million rials on Iran’s open market, a record, and three days later Iran’s statistical centre said year-on-year inflation in August had reached 84.4%, with the rolling annual average at about 65%. The price of vegetable oil in August was 383% higher than a year earlier, while eggs rose by 294%, chicken by 177% and red meat by 148%.

Daily life is narrowing as prices surge

As purchasing power collapses, households are buying fewer goods. Diets are changing, clothes are being patched and repaired instead of replaced, and medicine is short in supply, though it can still be found on the hidden market. According to the Mehr news agency, demand for red meat fell by 50% in April 2026 compared with the same period last year, and there has also been a leap in potato consumption that has led to shortages.

Golshan Fathi, an Iranian feminist, said basic goods such as eggs are now unaffordable to middle- and working-class families. Mohsen Zavaar, a biomedical engineer, described the pressure on households as a shift from bigger ambitions to daily survival: cutting out meat, reducing food quality, postponing medical treatment and collection by until the end of the month. He said people do not want miracles; they want decisions.

Fuel queues, closures and political fear

The crisis has also spread beyond the grocery basket. Long lines of cars stretched through parts of Tehran and the north-eastern city of Mashhad on Tuesday and Wednesday as motorists queued for fuel, with some queues blocking entire streets. Many petrol stations, including some of the largest, were closed.

Officials blamed either isolated panic buying driven by rumours of a government-planned price increase or Israeli bombs that destroyed two of Tehran’s three main oil depots. Inside the government, there have been anxious discussions for weeks about raising the price of oil to reduce costly subsidies, but the political class knows from past experience that such a move can trigger a backlash. A sudden overnight petrol price rise of 50% in November 2019 sparked week-long violent protests.

Public anger has been sharpened by comments about fuel smuggling. Saqab Esfahani, deputy to Iran’s president, said both political factions in the country were involved in fuel smuggling and that shutting down the operation would provoke retaliation. Masoud Pezeshkian has been urged to investigate those remarks.

The government is also moving toward greater secrecy around the economy. Fatemeh Mohajerani, a government spokesperson, said future poverty statistics would only be published with permission from the supreme national security council, making the decline in living standards effectively a classified state secret.

A crisis now framed as a war

Iran’s leaders are presenting the economic strain as part of the confrontation with the US. Hojatoleslam Taeb, the new head of the Basij paramilitary force and a close ally of Mojtaba Khamenei, said it would be necessary to reduce petrol consumption by as much as 10% to cut the need for expensive imports, and suggested avoiding unnecessary travel as an act of patriotism. Mohsen Rezaei, the new secretary of the supreme national security council, urged young people to start producing needed goods at home, drawing a comparison that one economics editor likened to Mao’s “backyard steel furnaces.”

Amirhossein Hashemi-Javid, an energy expert, said the deeper problem was Iran’s inability to export oil because of the US blockade. In his view, oil that cannot reach customers brings in no revenue and only adds storage, production and risk costs.

What happened

A Trump executive order put birthright citizenship back in play for children like Liza’s newborn, turning a once-settled Fourteenth Amendment guarantee into a fresh legal fight over whether the right can weaken a constitutional rule long treated as definitive.

Early last year, Liza and her husband were preparing for the birth of their child while facing an unusual fear: that their baby could be stateless. The couple had left Russia for the United States. Liza was in the country on temporary legal status, and her husband was seeking asylum. In theory, they could have applied for Russian citizenship for the baby at the consulate, but Liza said that felt risky because of her husband’s asylum case. Before January 20, 2025, that worry would not have existed: their baby, born in the United States, would automatically have been a U.S. citizen under the Fourteenth Amendment.

That changed when Donald Trump signed an executive order on the day of his second inauguration. According to the order, after February 19 only children with at least one biological parent who was a U.S. citizen or green-card holder could be legally considered American. Liza worried that if she and her husband were deported, their child could be left without legal protection anywhere. On January 21, she and four other expectant mothers sued, and federal courts blocked the policy within days.

At the end of June, the Supreme Court ruled in Trump v. Barbara that the order violated the Fourteenth Amendment. But the decision was narrower than many expected: only five justices took that view, while four conservatives would have allowed at least some restrictions on birthright citizenship. That split mattered because administration officials and allies were already signaling they were not done. Vice President Vance said, “We have to keep fighting,” and a month later the White House issued a second, narrower order aimed at certain categories of people, including children born to members of legally designated foreign terrorist groups.

The broader strategy is not just another executive order. The evidence points to a longer campaign to get the Court to revisit Barbara, much as conservatives spent decades working to overturn Roe v. What happened

A survey of 259 works maps a fast-growing set of AI systems that do more than generate drafts: they build deliverables step by step, revise them as evidence arrives, and still struggle with whether the final artifact is coherent, repairable, and fairly evaluated.

What counts as agentic artifact creation

The survey defines agentic artifact creation as stateful construction in which an AI system materially constructs or revises a deliverable and intermediate observations redirect later work. It says the process links an operational representation of the artifact, a construction policy, and runtime verification whose feedback can redirect later actions.

To draw that boundary, the authors reviewed 259 works available through August 20, 2026: 230 systems that met the definition and 29 benchmarks of agentic artifact construction. They searched arXiv, Google Scholar, Semantic Scholar, ACM DL, and IEEE Xplore from January 2023 onward, and they also maintain a curated paper list on GitHub.

The survey organizes the field into six artifact families: textual, 2D visual, audio, video, spatial, and behavioral. It includes mixed-initiative systems in which human input changes later system-mediated artifact work, but excludes generic problem-solving agents and systems that only consume or evaluate artifacts. Across families, it argues that construction difficulty depends not just on modality, but on how tightly decisions are coupled and whether failures become visible while they are still repairable.

How the survey says these systems should be judged

A major focus is evaluation. The survey says evaluation can target the delivered artifact, the construction trajectory, or the agentic system itself. To support a claim, an evaluator has to match a target with an evidence channel, an evaluator type, and a metric or aggregation rule. The authors note that benchmark coverage is still sparse in audio, video, and spatial creation, so the landscape includes named protocols released with core systems as well as more ordinary benchmark setups.

Examples in the survey include MLR-Bench and DRACO for textual creation, AgenticVBench for video, AuthorBench for spatial creation, and SWE-bench for behavioral software repair. It also lists broader evaluation methods for textual and visual grounding, source quality, editability, and rendering, and for audio and video composition, playback, physical checks, and repair.

The survey also warns that learned judges may add little independent evidence when they share the generator’s preferences or blind spots. In its framing, a system may compile, render, or score well and still fail source evidence, intended use, or reproducibility.

Principles and open problems

From those cases, the authors propose four design principles: externalize commitments, define control boundaries, make feedback actionable, and revalidate affected state. In plain terms, later decisions should be tied to addressable artifact state; delegation and tool use should have clear boundaries; feedback should point to repairs a system can actually make; and any change should trigger selective rechecking of the affected state.

They contrast this with systems where commitments are omitted, observations arrive too late, or review feedback cannot be turned into a bounded fix. They also frame these principles as inspectable control relations, not as a maturity ladder or mandatory pipeline.

The survey’s open problems follow that same logic. It points to global coherence under decision interdependence, targeted repair when a system is diagnosing its own work, system self-evolution across episodes, human authority under agentic delegation, and reliable evaluation of open-ended artifacts. Its bottom line is that the field is moving from direct generation toward construction systems whose value depends on whether they can preserve intent, expose failures early, and revise the right parts of an artifact without losing the rest.

Code as Worlds: Agentic Discovery of Executable World Representations for Physical Reasoning
science_cs_aipolitics_and_powerscience anchorpreprint_not_peer_reviewed
What happened

The Code-as-World preprint proposes executable code as a way to represent physical worlds, aiming to give vision-language models a more explicit basis for quantitative physical reasoning from videos and descriptions than simple recognition or explanation can provide.

The report argues that modern vision-language models can describe physical events but often lack explicit representations of object states, physical parameters, and governing dynamics. Code-as-World is presented as a compact, quantitatively grounded, and controllable abstraction that encodes physical composition, dynamic evolution, and visual appearance as executable code.

Agentic discovery from partial observations

To build these representations from multimodal inputs such as natural-language descriptions or real-world videos, the system uses an agentic discovery loop inspired by abductive reasoning. In that loop, an agent proposes a world hypothesis, executes it, renders it, verifies it against the evidence, and then iteratively refines it.

The paper says this process is implemented as a propose–instantiate–execute–render–verify cycle, with an iteration budget of up to five rounds in the reconstruction experiments. It also says the loop was compared with one-shot generation and Best-of-5 sampling under matched evaluation budgets.

As a downstream use, verified executable worlds are used as scalable physical supervision for training vision-language models on quantitative physical reasoning. The task is to infer measurable quantities such as object size, displacement, velocity, and acceleration from monocular video rather than answer only semantic questions about what is happening.

The preprint reports that Code-as-World-VL achieved state-of-the-art performance on QuantiPhy and surpassed leading proprietary models. It also says Code-as-World-VL-9B outperformed substantially larger models, including Gemini-3.1-Flash, on QuantiPhy, which the authors present as evidence that executable world representations can serve as a scalable foundation for physical intelligence.

J-Zero: Unified Challenger--Solver--Judge Co-Evolution from Zero Data
science_cs_aipolitics_and_powerscience anchorpreprint_not_peer_reviewed
What happened

J-Zero is a new self-evolving language-model framework that claims to let a Challenger, Solver, and Judge improve each other from zero data, aiming to push performance in both verifiable and unverifiable tasks while addressing whether learning signals can keep pace without human labels.

The KAIST authors, Gyouk Chu, Myeongho Jeon, and Eunho Yang, present Judge co-adaptation from Zero data, or J-Zero, as a way around the limit they see in unverifiable domains, where a Judge scores answers in place of a verifier. In their setup, the Challenger and Solver co-evolve adversarially: the Challenger generates harder tasks, and the Solver learns to answer them better. At the same time, the Judge is not fixed. It is co-adapted during the loop so that evaluation improves along with the two policies it supervises.

The paper says the Judge learns from preference pairs whose ordering is known from how the answers were produced, rather than from the Judge’s own scores. One pair compares the Solver’s answer with the Challenger’s, since the Solver is meant to answer well and the Challenger is meant to make the task difficult. Another compares the Solver’s decomposed-and-recombined answer with its one-shot answer, on the idea that the subtask-based version should be easier to solve well. The authors describe this as a unified Challenger–Solver–Judge co-evolution framework for self-improvement across both domains.

J-Zero also claims measurable gains. The preprint reports average improvements of 4.2 points on verifiable domains and 8.0 points on unverifiable domains. It says the system keeps improving through at least ten iterations, while the baselines degrade after two. The authors further say their analysis identifies Judge co-evolution as the key component for sustaining improvement across iterative rounds.

Revisiting Local Context for Long-Horizon Streaming 3D Reconstruction
science_cs_aiscience anchorpreprint_not_peer_reviewed
What happened

Streaming 3D reconstruction from extremely long videos is being pushed toward a strictly local temporal window, with ABot-Recon trying to keep camera-motion and scene-geometry estimates stable under bounded memory and computation while avoiding the drift that grows as sequences get longer.

The model comes from AMAP CV Lab, affiliated with Alibaba Group, and it takes a different route from streaming systems that rely on persistent long-range memory. Instead, it keeps learned temporal context local and makes each prediction in local reference frames over fixed temporal ranges, so the per-step task stays the same even as the stream grows.

Local context instead of long-range memory

The paper frames the problem around a familiar failure mode: early streaming models can run causally with finite context buffers or compact recurrent states, but their estimates often deteriorate as sequences grow. More recent methods try to stabilize long-horizon reconstruction by coupling short-range context with persistent or multi-level long-range memory.

ABot-Recon drops that learned long-range state. It caches KV features from only the preceding 11 frames, giving it a fixed 12-frame temporal horizon, and predicts a point map in the current camera coordinate system together with an adjacent-frame relative pose. Global camera poses and geometry are then recovered by sequential composition of those local measurements.

Because the predictions stay reference-frame-consistent, the paper says they are equivariant under changes of reference frame. That is what lets the system assemble a global trajectory from local outputs rather than asking the model to carry an ever-expanding temporal memory.

How the model is kept stable

ABot-Recon is not just a local predictor; it also adds a small set of mechanisms to curb drift. A lightweight temporal refiner improves relative rotations using recent visual and motion context, and a composition-aware pose loss supervises multi-step pose composition rather than treating each adjacent pair in isolation.

The training objective also keeps the usual local geometric supervision: point-map loss, surface-normal loss, and confidence loss. In the method description, the authors say the fixed window means memory stays O(K) and temporal-attention computation scales as O(NK) over a sequence of N frames, which is part of the case for a simple streaming backbone.

The paper says this design can be trained on short sequences and applied to ultra-long streams. It also notes that the same local formulation is compatible with standard attention implementations, and that cached states outside the window are discarded rather than preserved in a persistent state.

What the benchmarks showed

The evaluation covers camera pose estimation and dense 3D reconstruction on long-sequence benchmarks including VBR, KITTI, Oxford Spires, 7Scenes, and TUM-Dynamic. On Oxford Spires, ABot-Recon reports an ATE of 4.35 m and an RPE-R of 0.12°, which the paper says is about a 40% reduction in both errors relative to the best prior results.

The discussion also says that adding a loop-closure backend further reduces Oxford Spires ATE to 4.02 m. In that framing, loop closure is an optional long-range refinement layer, while the learned streaming backbone stays local and sequence-length-independent.

For dense 3D reconstruction, the paper says the method preserves coherent global structure while maintaining aligned local geometry across scene scales, including compact indoor scenes, dynamic RGB-D sequences, and larger landmark-scale environments. It also says increasing the training clip length from 32 to 128 frames further reduces both ATE and relative-pose errors, while keeping the local pose-prediction window unchanged.

Taken together, the preprint argues that persistent learned long-range memory is not required for ultra-long streaming reconstruction. Its core claim is that long-horizon performance can come from a short-context model, as long as the local predictions are designed to be accurate and composable.

The leap second is dead. Long live the leap hour?
science_generalclimate_disasterscience anchorscience_journalism
What happened

World timekeepers are weighing an immediate end to the leap second, with a vote due in October, after researchers warned that the next adjustment could have to run backward — a first that could unsettle clocks, computers and critical infrastructure.

The leap second has been used 27 times since the 1970s to keep Coordinated Universal Time aligned with Earth’s uneven rotation, even though the practice was already set to end by 2035. But authorities are now considering scrapping it sooner because scientists say there is a 30% probability of a negative leap second by 2035, which would make UTC skip a second instead of add one.

That has pushed timekeepers to propose a leap hour instead. Under that plan, clocks would be realigned only when the two timescales drifted apart by 3,600 seconds. Member states of the General Conference on Weights and Measures (CGPM) will vote on the resolution at their 28th meeting outside Paris on 13–15 October.

The concern is not just theoretical. The last leap second was added in 2016, and researchers say such adjustments can disrupt systems that depend on precise synchronization, including electrical grids, telecommunications, financial systems and the Internet. Different organizations already handle leap seconds differently: Google “smears” the extra second across the day, others add it to a specific minute, and some exchanges alter trading times to avoid the 61-second minute.

Why are rich people so pessimistic? What the numbers say
science_generalglobal_south_and_exploitedpolitics_and_powerclimate_disastereconomy_and_businessscience anchorscience_journalism
What happened

People in richer Western countries often sound gloomier about the future than people in poorer places, and the numbers suggest wealth alone does not explain it: age, culture, income, and how much influence people think they have all shape expectations.

Why wealth does not map neatly onto optimism

Polls cited in the piece show how strong the mood can be. In 2026, two out of five US respondents said they thought the United States would not exist in 2276, its 500th year. In another poll, a majority expected that by 2050 the country would be less important, more politically divided, and economically weaker. The United Kingdom and France also ranked well below the global average in a 2025 survey of views on economic prospects.

But a UNICEF study spanning 55 countries found that people’s feelings about the future varied sharply even when they faced shared challenges. Optimism depends less on objective facts than on where people live, their income, culture and age. It also says optimism is not a rational forecast of coming reality; instead, it is a mood or attitude about the social or material future.

That helps explain why optimism is more common in low- and middle-income countries. Four in five young Indonesians, aged 15–24, said the world is becoming better with each generation, compared with 42% of young people in France. Age matters too: with each year, people are 1% less likely to think the future will be better, and 55 is presented as the point at which pessimists become the majority.

The pattern is not just about personal finances. People may feel happier as they get richer, yet also become more pessimistic about their country and the world. Optimism is strongest where people expect improvement and believe they have some agency over what comes next.

Order from disorder: do we need a new law of physics?
science_generalpolitics_and_powerscience anchorscience_journalism
What happened

A new book by Robert Hazen and Michael L. Wong argues that the natural world does not only run downhill toward disorder: it also has a recurrent tendency to build order, and that physics may need a second arrow of time to explain it.

The pair focus on the second law of thermodynamics, which says energy dissipates over time and entropy increases. In their framing, that is the familiar “arrow of time” behind events such as a snowman melting or organic matter decaying, as chemical bonds break and atoms spread into soil, air and water. But Hazen and Wong say that is only half the story.

Their case is that order has repeatedly emerged from simple, randomly distributed beginnings — in the creation of elements through nuclear fusion, the formation of chemical compounds and the evolution of intelligent life. They argue that this tendency is so widespread that there “must be a second arrow of time” describing “the universal tendency for certain systems to display increases in order”. They want guiding principles that explain complexity in evolutionary systems, both living and inert.

To measure that order, they propose “functional information”, estimated by comparing the number of forms that exist with the number that are theoretically possible. In their view, order rises when emergent forms occupy a smaller share of all possible configurations, as with human language, where particular sounds became useful for communication out of many possibilities.

The proposal is engaging and grounded in some peer-reviewed literature, but it is not settled. The authors acknowledge counterarguments and say they have not found “a satisfying solution” to all of them. A major difficulty is practical: measuring functional information in natural systems is not straightforward, even before deciding whether increasing order is really a new law of nature.

A “quantum bath” puts quantum entanglement on autopilot
science_generalscience anchorscience_journalism
What happened

Physicists at the Institute of Science and Technology Austria have shown a fully autonomous way to entangle separated qubits using a “quantum bath” of correlated light, a step that could matter for future quantum computers even though the method is still less efficient than active-control approaches.

The experiment, published in *Physical Review X*, is described as the first demonstration of a prediction proposed more than 20 years ago. It addresses a central problem in quantum computing: future systems may need to connect widely separated modules through distributed entanglement, but until now that has generally required active control and repeated measurements.

Previous attempts to entangle distant qubits usually followed one of two routes. One sent a single, actively controlled photon from one qubit to another. The other had each qubit emit a photon and then matched the two photons in an effort to generate entanglement. That second approach was recognized by the 2022 Nobel Prize in Physics, but it still depends on repeated measurements and post-selection, and it does not always produce entanglement successfully.

A bath that does the work

Alejandro Andrés-Juanes and professor Johannes Fink, working with international collaborators, developed a different setup. Their system uses a quantum bath — the qubits’ environment — made from correlated particles of light that automatically brings distant qubits into synchronization. In a prototype device, the researchers used a shared source of correlated light particles to entangle two separated qubits, experimentally realizing a concept that had remained theoretical for more than two decades.

The team says the approach is meant to bridge a gap between continuous-variable entangled states, which can be produced efficiently, and discrete-variable entanglement, which stationary qubits can use for practical applications. In Andres-Juanes’s words, the aim was to overcome the mismatch between what is readily available and what is practically useful. Because the entangled state is stabilized remotely, the method does not require active control or measurement.

To confirm that the qubits were synchronized inside the quantum bath, the researchers used quantum tomography, taking measurements lasting only 20-80 nanoseconds. Fink said the bath becomes the source of entanglement and creates a new ground state through a continuous stream of correlated photons, so the entangled qubit state remains available as a resource for further processing.

The result is promising, but the paper says it is not yet as efficient as actively controlled methods. The researchers report that their method currently transfers about 10% of the bath’s available entanglement. Even so, they say the prototype could provide new opportunities for quantum-optics experiments and help efforts to expand quantum processors.

Scientists discover two superconducting states hiding as one

What happened

In niobium diselenide (NbSe2), researchers at the Hebrew University of Jerusalem found that a material once thought to show a single superconducting gap actually contains two different superconducting orders that interact so strongly they appear as one. The study was led by PhD. student Shahar Simon and MSc. student Maya Klang under Prof. Oded Millo and Prof. Hadar Steinberg of the Racah Institute of Physics and the Center for Nanoscience and Nanotechnology. The work, published in *Physical Review Letters*, used highly sensitive tunneling spectroscopy measurements.

The researchers said the same concealed behavior also appears in the closely related material TaS2. By using a model with two distinct superconducting orders, they said they could explain the detailed superconducting energy spectrum seen in earlier experiments more accurately, as well as how the materials behave under magnetic fields. The findings also suggest that bulk NbSe2 may contain three interacting superconducting orders.

Scientists discover diamonds can generate electricity
science_generalglobal_south_and_exploitedscience anchorscience_journalism
What happened

Researchers at the University of Hong Kong said ultrathin, highly flexible diamond membranes can produce a measurable piezoelectric response, challenging a scientific assumption that had stood since the early 1900s. The work was led by Professor Zhiqin Chu of HKU’s Department of Electrical and Computer Engineering and Professor Yuan Lin of the Department of Mechanical Engineering, Faculty of Engineering. Diamond had generally been classified as non-piezoelectric and was usually used only as a structural support in microelectromechanical systems, but this result suggests it can generate electricity when mechanically deformed.

To test the idea, the team used a recently developed edge exfoliation method to make an ultrathin, flexible polycrystalline diamond membrane. When they deliberately flexed it, they observed stable voltage signals, and they carried out mechanical cycling experiments under controlled conditions to check that the output was genuine and not from environmental interference or triboelectric effects. The researchers also used first-principles calculations, which pointed to asymmetry at grain boundaries inside the membrane as the source of the effect.
