New attacks in Hormuz and Saudi Arabia test nerves as war’s spread worsens oil disruption

What happened

New strikes in Saudi Arabia and on vessels near the Strait of Hormuz have widened the security threat around two critical energy routes. Houthi attacks and territorial gains in Yemen, damage to Saudi Arabia’s East-West pipeline, and uncertainty over shipping access are increasing pressure on oil supplies and regional governments.

On Sunday, Yemen’s Houthi group said it had launched a ballistic missile and drone attack against the Sharurah military base in southern Saudi Arabia. Houthi military spokesperson Yahya Saree said the strikes targeted weapons depots and command-and-control centres involved in operations against Yemen, claimed they had hit their targets, and warned of larger attacks deeper inside Saudi Arabia if attacks on Yemen continued.

Saudi state media also released video showing damage to homes and a mosque in Jazan province after what was described as another cross-border Houthi attack. The claims came as fighting intensified between the Houthis and forces loyal to Yemen’s internationally recognised government, which is backed by Riyadh.

Yemen’s front moves toward Saudi Arabia

The escalation follows fighting around Mocha, the Red Sea coastal city, and elsewhere in southwestern Yemen. The Houthis have made significant territorial gains in western Yemen in recent days, while government forces have reported advances in northern al-Jawf province near the Saudi border. A related account said the group had also captured Perim Island at the mouth of the Red Sea and the port town of Mocha, in an effort to control the Bab al-Mandeb Strait.

Yemen’s military said on Sunday that its air force carried out three strikes against Houthi positions and barracks in the Taiz region. A government-affiliated website reported that further air raids killed several Houthi fighters. Those claims and the Houthi account of the Saudi attack come from opposing sides in the conflict.

The group’s advance has created a difficult choice for Saudi Arabia, according to analysis cited in related reporting: absorb growing economic and security costs or respond in ways that could draw the kingdom more directly into the wider regional war. A Yemen expert, Mohammed Al-Basha, said the signs pointed toward a possible large-scale Saudi retaliation, while other analysts said Riyadh did not want an all-out war.

Pipeline damage threatens an alternative to Hormuz

The Saudi attacks followed a drone strike on the kingdom’s 1,200-kilometre East-West oil pipeline, which carries oil to the Red Sea coast and allows Saudi Arabia to bypass the Strait of Hormuz. Saudi authorities said drones launched from Iraq struck the pipeline in the Riyadh and Medina regions, after which it was shut down as a precaution.

Iraqi security forces said they had found and confiscated a drone-launching platform allegedly used in the attack. Lieutenant General Saad Maan, head of Iraq’s Security Media Cell, said security and intelligence teams had located the equipment and were working to identify those responsible. Iraq’s commander-in-chief approved an Iranian request to join the investigation, while Baghdad remained in contact with Saudi authorities to share intelligence.

Iraq temporarily closed several crossings with Iran, including Shalamcheh, Chazabeh and Mandali, for security inspections before partially reopening them for humanitarian traffic. Maan said Iraq’s policy was not only to prevent attacks on its own territory but also to stop its land from being used as a launching pad against another country or becoming part of a proxy war.

The pipeline’s outage has immediate implications because it is a key alternative route when shipping through Hormuz is disrupted. Saudi Arabia has not disclosed the full extent of the damage or how long the line will remain offline. Traders and Saudi oil buyers told Reuters that supplies stored at the Red Sea port of Yanbu could sustain exports for only five to seven days if the pipeline remained shut. After that, they estimated that as much as 4 per cent of global oil supply could be at risk, in addition to oil already affected by disruption in Hormuz.

Repair estimates cited by Reuters ranged from days to weeks, while satellite imagery showed large columns of smoke from locations along the pipeline. Oil prices had already risen above $100 a barrel during the previous week, and the retail price of diesel in the United States reached more than $6.20 per gallon.

Attacks at sea and diplomacy put on hold

The pressure also reached shipping lanes. The British maritime security agency UKMTO said a vessel was struck by a projectile while transiting the Strait of Hormuz, causing a fire and forcing the crew to evacuate. Iran said one person was killed and four crew members were wounded aboard an Iranian commercial vessel struck off its coast.

The attacks came as Iran and Gulf states were expected to meet in Oman to discuss shipping routes through Hormuz. Iran said it had reached an agreement with Oman on future routes, but Oman’s foreign minister, Sayyid Badr Albusaidi, announced that the meeting had been postponed in the interests of consensus. Iran’s foreign ministry said the postponement was jointly decided by Tehran and Muscat at the request of some regional countries.

Iraq had planned to send a delegation, while Saudi Arabia and Qatar were expected to be represented by their foreign ministers. Bahrain said it would not meet Iranian officials. Iran’s foreign minister, Abbas Araqchi, said the strait would not reopen until the United States met Tehran’s demands, despite any agreement with Oman. The postponement leaves both the security of shipping and the future availability of Saudi Arabia’s alternative oil route uncertain.

The team used GAGE-seq to measure gene expression and three-dimensional genome contacts within the same individual cells, then combined those measurements with spatial transcriptomic maps showing where gene activity occurred in intact tissue.

To interpret how genome structure might affect cellular behavior, the researchers also developed Hicformer, a deep-learning model that combines DNA sequence information with genome-folding patterns and physical contacts between DNA regions. This approach allowed them to connect chromosome structure with disease-related gene programs across several types of brain cells.

Cells from people with Alzheimer’s showed less distinct boundaries between normally separate active and inactive genome regions, a pattern the researchers called “increased compartment mingling.” They also found fewer interactions between nearby genome sections, more contacts between some distant regions, and weaker interactions between genes and nearby regulatory elements. Greater compartment mingling was associated with lower overall gene activity.

These structural changes were associated with reduced activity in programs involved in neurons and synapses, alongside altered metabolism and cellular stress responses. In microglia, the brain’s immune cells, the researchers also found links to senescence-related programs. Mapping the changes across tissue connected genome reorganization not only to gene regulation but also to differences in how brain cells were arranged.

Jian Ma, who led and supervised the study, said Alzheimer’s cannot be understood through a single biological layer. The findings add higher-order chromatin organization—the DNA-and-protein material that packages the genome—to the disease’s molecular landscape. Further studies will be needed to determine which structural changes directly contribute to Alzheimer’s progression and whether affected regulatory regions could become therapeutic targets.

Scientists have observed since the 1980s that the relationship has weakened, with the Indian Ocean increasingly behaving differently from what Pacific conditions would normally imply. The researchers link the recent shift to climate warming and human-caused greenhouse gas emissions.

To place the modern change in a longer context, the team combined climate-model simulations with paleoclimate evidence from corals, tree rings and stalagmites, reconstructing conditions back to the early 1600s. The two oceans remained closely connected through most of the past 400 years, but their relationship weakened significantly between 1810 and 1850 after major tropical volcanic eruptions. Simulations covering the past 1,000 years supported the finding that volcanism can temporarily disrupt the link. The researchers describe the modern weakening as “exceptional” and driven by a different force: human climate forcing. Published in *Nature Communications*, the study warns that changing ocean interactions could make forecasts of rainfall and other climate patterns less reliable.

Physicists discover a hidden “curveball” in quantum light
science_generalscience anchorscience_journalism
What happened

An international team working at the Paul Scherrer Institute PSI has experimentally demonstrated the optical Magnus effect at the atomic scale by focusing laser light on a single calcium ion. The effect is analogous to the Magnus effect that makes a spinning table-tennis ball curve, but here it does not bend the ion’s path. Instead, when the laser is focused very tightly, the altered structure of its electromagnetic field shifts the point of strongest interaction slightly away from the beam’s center. The results were published in *Physical Review Letters*.

To detect the displacement, researchers held the electrically charged calcium atom nearly motionless in an electromagnetic ion trap, then moved it through different parts of the focused beam and measured its interaction with the light. The ion served as a sensitive probe capable of detecting a shift of just a few hundred nanometers. The measured sideways displacement depended on the light’s wavelength, not on how tightly the beam was focused. The finding could affect quantum computers, where lasers control qubits with high precision: ignoring the shift might contribute to errors, while the forces it generates could potentially help couple qubits.

NASA-backed scientists turn plastic waste into edible cookies
science_generalscience anchorscience_journalism
What happened

Researchers at Southern Illinois University Carbondale have engineered yeast to turn compounds derived from plastic and agricultural waste into food ingredients, including proteins, vitamins and flavorings. The work produced protein-rich, 3D-printed cookies that could eventually help supply food in resource-limited settings, from disaster areas to deep-space missions.

The research grew out of a NASA-led effort to develop food-production technologies for space exploration, where astronauts cannot rely on regular shipments from Earth. Associate Professor Lahiru Jayakody and graduate student Sandhya Jayasekara are targeting polyethylene terephthalate (PET), the plastic used in many soda and water bottles, along with discarded corn stalks, leaves and other biomass.

The team first processes these materials using a technique called oxidative hydrothermal dissolution. Developed by SIU Carbondale geology professor Ken Anderson, the process uses water and oxygen at high temperature and pressure to break resistant materials into smaller compounds. Engineered yeast—including baker’s yeast—then converts those compounds into proteins, fats, acids, vitamins and flavoring substances.

Researchers combine the yeast-derived ingredients with fiber, starch and sweetener, then use a 3D printer to make protein-rich cookies called µBites, pronounced “microbites.” The available data indicate that the cookies are safe to eat, but the team is still awaiting institutional approval for formal taste tests. Participants have so far assessed their aroma; most said they would be willing to eat the cookies when food was scarce.

The scientists are also developing yeast strains that can produce ingredients such as vanilla flavoring from plant biomass and beta carotene from PET-derived ethylene glycol. Their longer-term goal is for microbes to produce more of the cookie’s ingredients, reducing the need for separate additions. Jayakody hopes the product could be ready for public consumption within the next few years. The researchers presented the results at the fall meeting of the American Chemical Society; the work was funded by NASA’s Deep Space Food Challenge and an NSF CAREER grant.
