Deadly US strike on Iran school may have been a war crime, UN report finds

What happened

Two US strikes in Iran, including one that hit an elementary school and killed at least 156 people, may have constituted war crimes, a UN fact-finding mission concluded. The findings intensify scrutiny of the opening attacks, while Washington rejects the report and says its own investigation remains ongoing.

The school strike occurred on February 28 in Minab, a southern Iranian city. Iranian officials said at least 156 people were killed, including 120 children and 26 teachers. Although reported figures varied slightly, the incident would rank among the worst civilian casualty events in recent US military history.

The UN investigators also examined a second US strike that day in Lamerd, which hit a sports center and residential area and killed 22 civilians, according to the mission. In both cases, the report said investigators “found reasonable grounds to believe the United States committed the war crime of launching indiscriminate attacks resulting in the loss of life or injury to civilians or damage to civilian objects.”

How the Minab strike happened

The mission’s findings point to a failure to verify the school as a military target. US Central Command created coordinates for the strike on the Shajareh Tayyiba school using outdated information supplied by the Defense Intelligence Agency, according to preliminary findings of a separate US investigation described by sources.

Satellite imagery from 2013 showed the school and a neighboring Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps naval base within the same compound. By 2016, however, a fence had separated the school from the base and the school had its own entrance. Imagery from December 2025 showed dozens of people apparently playing in the school courtyard.

“No information indicates that the school was used for military purposes at the time,” the UN report found. It said the United States had failed to do everything feasible to verify that the school was a military objective. The strike appears to have resulted from an attack intended for the nearby IRGC base, according to early evidence cited in the investigation.

The report was prepared by independent investigators who reviewed satellite imagery, photographs, video, information from organizations and experts, and dozens of interviews conducted inside and outside Iran. The investigators requested unrestricted access to Iran, which was not granted. Iran answered only about a quarter of the mission’s information requests, the report said. Requests for meetings and information from US officials and the Israeli government also went unanswered.

Washington rejects the findings as its investigation continues

White House spokeswoman Anna Kelly said the only party in the conflict that had committed war crimes was “the Iranian regime,” which she accused of killing its own people, attacking Americans overseas, carrying out terrorism in the Strait of Hormuz and launching indiscriminate attacks on regional neighbors. She said the president was acting to ensure that Iran would never possess a nuclear weapon.

A State Department official said the Trump administration did not give credibility to the report’s findings. The administration had formally withdrawn the United States from the UN Human Rights Council in February 2025, accusing the body of antisemitism. The Department of Defense declined to comment beyond saying that an investigation was ongoing and that it had nothing to announce.

The United States has not published its own investigation. According to sources familiar with the matter, the first two stages of the Pentagon’s standard review were completed within a week: officials established whether the intended target had been hit and determined that the United States was responsible. A further review by Defense Intelligence Agency analysts, which would examine satellite imagery and other intelligence and assess the wider effects of the strike, had not yet been ordered.

A separate investigation involving interviews with service members was also launched, but sources said information from those interviews had been restricted within US Central Command. The UN report’s findings could be presented to the Human Rights Council and potentially used by international courts, including the International Criminal Court. The United States is not an ICC member, but it has its own war-crimes laws.

The report also accuses Iran of crimes against humanity

The UN mission’s report addressed events beyond the US strikes. It said Iran’s crackdown on anti-government protesters in late 2025 and early 2026, before the war with the United States began, killed thousands and involved torture and detainees being “disappeared” without their families being notified.

The investigators characterized those abuses as “a widespread and systematic attack on civilians” and said the violations amounted to crimes against humanity. The mission’s conclusions therefore assign alleged serious violations to both sides of the broader conflict, while responsibility for the Minab and Lamerd strikes remains contested in Washington pending the US investigation.

Trump announces an agreement with Denmark to assume the “permanent control of security” of Greenland

What happened

Donald Trump says the United States has reached a security agreement with Denmark and Greenland that would give Washington “permanent control” over Greenland’s security and other needs, while Danish and Greenlandic leaders describe a future pact that still requires formal signing and parliamentary approval.

Trump announced the deal on Friday in a post on Truth Social, saying the United States had worked with representatives of Denmark and Greenland to guarantee that it would “forever” have the ability to do whatever was necessary on the island. He said the agreement would address all of Washington’s concerns and would not cost the United States any money.

The announcement follows months of threats by Trump to acquire or annex Greenland, including remarks that the United States could take the territory by force. Greenland is a self-governing territory within the Kingdom of Denmark, and Denmark and Greenland have repeatedly rejected the idea that the island is for sale.

What Trump says the agreement would provide

Trump said the United States would immediately begin developing a large military presence in suitable parts of Greenland, although he did not specify the locations or the size of the planned footprint. He also said no US adversary would be allowed to establish a military base, station personnel or make sensitive investments on the island without Washington’s express written approval.

In his description, the arrangement would give the United States permanent authority over Greenland’s security and “all other needs”. He also described its duration as effectively unlimited. The BBC reported that an unnamed US State Department official said the agreement would not expire even if Greenland became independent in the future.

The same official said the United States would have unilateral authority to build additional military structures, as well as permanent rights of access, basing and overflight. The reported terms would bar non-NATO countries from building bases in Greenland and prevent adversaries from making investments in sensitive sectors. The official specifically referred to Russia and China.

However, the full text has not been released. It therefore remains unclear how the proposed arrangement would differ from existing US rights on the island, or whether all the provisions described by Trump and the US official are included in the final agreement.

Denmark and Greenland emphasize sovereignty

Denmark and Greenland issued a joint statement saying they expected to sign an agreement with the United States during the following week’s United Nations General Assembly session in New York. The statement said the pact would strengthen security in the Arctic and North Atlantic regions, but also made clear that it would need to pass the necessary parliamentary procedures in both Nuuk and Copenhagen before entering into force.

Danish Prime Minister Mette Frederiksen said the agreement recognized “the sovereignty and territorial integrity of the Kingdom” and the right of Greenland’s people to self-determination. She described it as a prospective agreement benefiting Greenland, the Kingdom of Denmark and the United States, rather than as a transfer of sovereignty to Washington.

Greenland’s Prime Minister Jens-Frederik Nielsen said the deal would recognize Greenland’s interests and its role in international cooperation. He said it would ensure and strengthen the security of Greenland, Denmark, the United States and the Western alliance.

Those statements are less expansive than Trump’s characterization. The Guardian reported that the Danish and Greenlandic leaders’ language suggested the pact would fall short of Trump’s stated desire for the United States to acquire Greenland. They did not describe the agreement as giving Washington ownership of the territory.

An unclear addition to an existing defense relationship

The proposed deal would build on an existing US military relationship with Greenland. Under a 1951 defense agreement with Denmark, renewed in 2004 according to El País, the United States already has broad rights to expand its military presence on the island. The United States also maintains a permanent base at Pituffik in northwestern Greenland, with more than 100 military personnel, according to the BBC.

The BBC reported that a US official said the new agreement would allow additional military structures without approval from Greenland or Denmark and would provide permanent access, basing and overflight rights. But without the text, the precise scope and novelty of those provisions cannot be established from the announcements alone.

The agreement comes as Greenland’s location and resources have become central to Washington’s stated security concerns. The island lies between the Arctic and Atlantic oceans and has strategic importance for defense and shipping routes. It also has largely untapped rare-earth mineral reserves. Trump has said Greenland is important to plans for a “Golden Dome” missile-defense system intended to protect the United States from attacks involving Russia and China.

For Denmark and Greenland, the immediate issue is whether the final pact can combine expanded US security and military access with their insistence on sovereignty and self-determination. The planned signing and subsequent parliamentary procedures will determine whether the announced arrangement takes legal effect and what its final terms actually are.

Doctors Without Borders Warns of Ebola Spreading to New Areas

What happened

Doctors Without Borders (MSF) says Ebola transmission in the Democratic Republic of the Congo (DRC) is shifting from initial hotspots into provinces with weaker response capacity, challenging official indications that the epidemic peaked in August and raising the risk of new, harder-to-contain outbreaks.

MSF issued the warning on Friday as Congolese authorities pointed to signs of improvement and identified the epidemic’s peak as occurring in the third week of August. Anthony Kergosien, MSF’s emergency coordinator in the DRC, said fewer patients at some Ebola treatment centers should not be interpreted as evidence that the outbreak is under control.

According to Kergosien, transmission appears to have slowed in Ituri, the original epicenter, at least temporarily. However, the emergence of new cases nationwide has not declined. South Ubangi in the northwest has become the seventh affected province, while North Kivu in the east accounts for nearly half of newly confirmed cases and is experiencing a sharp increase in its test positivity rate.

New areas face weaker response capacity

MSF warned that expansion into areas with more limited preparedness and response capabilities could make the epidemic significantly more difficult to manage. The organization is trying to identify and contain new hotspots before they develop into major outbreaks, but Kergosien called for substantially greater operational support from United Nations agencies, including the World Health Organization (WHO).

The DRC’s latest official figures record 7,475 cases and 3,605 confirmed deaths since May. MSF said the true totals could be higher because weaknesses in surveillance and infection detection may leave some infections unidentified. The outbreak was officially declared on May 15.

MSF has deployed more than 2,800 staff members and operates nine Ebola treatment centers, as well as several isolation areas, across Ituri, North Kivu, South Kivu and Tshopo. The organization’s work includes responding to known cases while trying to locate transmission in newly affected communities.

The Africa Centres for Disease Control and Prevention (Africa CDC), the African Union’s public health agency, also rejected claims on Thursday that the epidemic had already peaked. It warned that transmission remained uncontrolled, reinforcing the disagreement over whether the apparent slowdown in some locations represents a broader improvement.

Transport and access complicate the response

A logistics delivery illustrated the continuing effort to reach affected areas. On September 10, 2026, the United Nations Humanitarian Air Service (UNHAS) delivered 1.2 metric tons of cargo, including personal protective equipment and tents, to NiaNia in Ituri. The supplies were intended to help WHO and the DRC Ministry of Health reach affected communities.

The response is complicated by shortages of personnel, laboratories and healthcare infrastructure, as well as insecurity and limited access to diagnostic facilities. These conditions can delay detection and slow the deployment of medical teams. The DRC’s large territory, difficult-to-reach areas and long distances also make it harder to move equipment, supplies and staff to communities where transmission is concentrated.

The outbreak is described as the second deadliest and most contagious on record globally, behind the 2014–2016 West Africa epidemic, which caused approximately 11,000 deaths and 28,000 infections. Ebola also spread to neighboring Uganda; WHO declared that outbreak over on August 26 after 20 confirmed cases, including 15 imported from the DRC, and two deaths.

Reduced funding and international support

MSF said reduced global-health funding is weakening preparedness for new epidemics. The discontinuation of programs linked to the U.S. Agency for International Development, combined with budget cuts, has curtailed projects intended to strengthen outbreak preparedness. The organization contrasted the current situation with the 2014–2016 epidemic, when Sierra Leone received substantial support from foreign governments and organizations, including USAID.

China and Cuba have expressed willingness to support the DRC’s Ebola response. Cuba reaffirmed its commitment during a technical videoconference between the two countries’ health ministries and reactivated its National Plan for Ebola Prevention and Response, including stronger surveillance of international travelers and preparations at its 24 points of entry. China has announced emergency humanitarian aid, deployed medical specialists and continued cooperation with African health authorities.

For MSF, however, the immediate concern remains that falling patient numbers in established hotspots may obscure continued national spread. The organization says the response must find and contain transmission in new provinces before limited preparedness, difficult access and delayed detection allow those hotspots to become major outbreaks.

Najib’s house arrest puts Malaysia in ‘uncharted’ legal territory, poses political challenge for PM Anwar
global_front_pageglobal_south_and_exploitedpolitics_and_powereconomy_and_businessmaterial_harmregional expansion
What happened

Former Prime Minister Najib Razak has been allowed to serve the remainder of his current prison sentence under house arrest, conditional on paying a RM50 million (US$12.26 million) fine. The arrangement raises unresolved questions about Malaysia’s legal framework while testing Prime Minister Anwar Ibrahim’s anti-corruption credentials and governing coalition.

The decision on Friday, Sep 18, was made by the Federal Territories Pardons Board, chaired by King Sultan Ibrahim Sultan Iskandar. Najib, 73, had been serving a six-year sentence scheduled to end on Aug 23, 2028, after convictions for criminal breach of trust, money laundering and abuse of power involving RM42 million transferred from SRC International, a former 1Malaysia Development Berhad (1MDB) subsidiary, into his personal accounts.

Legal experts described the arrangement as unprecedented. Najib is likely the first convicted prisoner in Malaysia to have a sentence commuted to house arrest, but the country has no specific laws setting out how home detention should operate. Constitutional lawyer Lim Wei Jiet said he did not know the details or limitations that would apply, describing the situation as “uncharted territory.”

A new legal arrangement with unclear conditions

The decision follows a December 2025 High Court ruling that dismissed Najib’s judicial review application concerning an addendum order purportedly allowing him to serve his sentence at home. Judge Alice Loke said that order could not be executed under existing Malaysian law, noting that the statutory power to release a prisoner on licence belongs to the Commissioner-General of Prisons.

The new arrangement therefore leaves questions over who will supervise Najib and what restrictions will apply. Possible measures discussed by criminal lawyer S Sivananthan include prison officers stationed at Najib’s residence, electronic monitoring and limits on visitors. It is also unclear whether Najib will be permitted to participate in political activities.

Sivananthan said no Malaysian prisoner had previously served a sentence under house arrest and warned that the decision could encourage other prisoners, particularly those convicted of white-collar crimes, to seek similar treatment. Former economy minister Rafizi Ramli called on Anwar to publish the conditions so that breaches could be acted upon.

A separate issue concerns Najib’s 1MDB case. In December 2025, the High Court sentenced him to 15 years in prison and fined him RM11.38 billion after finding him guilty on 25 charges involving RM2.2 billion allegedly misappropriated from the state investment fund. Najib has appealed against that conviction and sentence.

Lim said that if Najib obtains a stay of the 1MDB sentence while his appeal proceeds, he could continue serving his current sentence under house arrest. If the conviction and sentence are ultimately upheld, however, the house-arrest arrangement would not automatically apply to the separate term, meaning he could be required to return to prison.

Najib’s release could revive UMNO

The arrangement also has immediate political implications. Analysts said Najib’s departure from prison could strengthen UMNO and its Barisan Nasional coalition because he retains influence among party supporters. Political analyst Azmi Hassan said the development could “rejuvenate” UMNO and give it momentum after electoral successes in the Johor and Negeri Sembilan state elections.

Before entering prison in 2022, Najib—known to supporters as “Bossku,” or “my boss” in Malay—drew large crowds at campaign events. Azmi said his return to public life could help UMNO ahead of the Melaka state election, expected before the end of the year, and Malaysia’s next general election, due by February 2028.

UMNO president Ahmad Zahid Hamidi said the party had begun a nationwide donation drive to raise the RM50 million fine. He described the king’s decision as a relief for UMNO members and Najib’s family. Political researcher James Chin said UMNO, which has more than a million members, should be able to raise the money, adding that Najib’s family could also raise the sum.

The fine may provide a political compromise, according to Azmi. Many UMNO supporters view Najib’s imprisonment as political persecution, he said, while people who believe Najib was treated too leniently over 1MDB could see the payment as a measure of accountability.

Anwar faces pressure inside his coalition

The decision puts Anwar in a difficult position because his administration has repeatedly pledged tougher action against corruption. Chin called Najib’s house arrest a “bad look” for Anwar’s government and said it could reinforce the perception that Malaysia’s political leaders enjoy impunity.

Anwar’s Pakatan Harapan (PH) coalition governs alongside Najib’s BN, but tensions between the two blocs have been simmering. BN is also working with the opposition Perikatan Nasional coalition under a new Malay-Muslim conservative pact connected to recent state elections.

The strongest internal opposition is likely to come from the Democratic Action Party (DAP), a major PH component. DAP chief Anthony Loke said the party respected the king’s power of pardon but would continue defending the rule of law and fighting corruption. The party’s central executive committee was due to meet on Saturday to discuss its position and next steps.

DAP politicians have previously opposed Najib’s release. Housing and Local Government Minister Nga Kor Ming said in July that he would resign from the Cabinet if Najib were released without serving a sentence commensurate with his offence. Analysts said Anwar must now manage those objections while maintaining cooperation with BN and preserving his government’s anti-corruption commitments.

Saudis scramble to solve ‘humiliating dilemma’ after US rejects Red Sea intervention

What happened

Saudi Arabia is scrambling to protect its alternative export route through the Red Sea after the United States declined to intervene militarily against Iran-aligned Houthi forces in Yemen. The standoff threatens Saudi oil and petrochemical shipments, raises the prospect of concessions to Tehran and the Houthis, and adds pressure to already strained energy markets.

Houthi control along Yemen’s Red Sea coast has turned the Bab el-Mandeb Strait into a strategic vulnerability for Riyadh. US President Donald Trump dismissed the Houthi seizure of Yemen’s entire Red Sea coast on September 12 as a problem affecting “only one country”, according to analysts cited in the evidence. That position reportedly deeply disturbed Saudi leaders, who had expected Washington to support them against what they regard as mortal enemies.

The immediate impact was on Saudi Arabia’s ability to bypass the Strait of Hormuz. Iran had already blockaded that main Gulf export route, while Saudi Arabia’s East–West Pipeline had allowed crude to move across the kingdom to Yanbu on the Red Sea. Drone strikes by Iran-allied Iraqi militias badly damaged the pipeline, prompting the suspension of Saudi oil exports from the Red Sea, according to the supplied account.

The attacks exposed the limits of a route Riyadh had developed as insurance against disruption in the Gulf. Middle East Eye reported separately that Saudi Arabia told European refineries on Friday not to expect crude deliveries the following month. The two accounts describe a wider interruption to Saudi exports as both the Gulf route and the Red Sea alternative came under pressure.

The Red Sea route is commercially vulnerable even without a formal closure

Houthi forces reportedly captured the Port of Mokha on September 10, Mayun—also known as Perim—Island inside Bab el-Mandeb on September 11, and the Greater and Lesser Hanish Islands on September 14. The islands lie on tanker and liquefied natural gas shipping lanes entering the Red Sea.

The strait has not been physically closed, but territorial control gives the Houthis the ability to disrupt traffic. Abdi Guled, editor of Horn Briefs and a former AP and Reuters correspondent, said the group did not need to close Bab el-Mandeb to gain strategic value from its position. War-risk insurance premiums have surged, and major insurers have reportedly withdrawn coverage for some transits.

Early indicators showed vessel traffic declining, with shipping companies diverting vessels around the Cape of Good Hope. That detour adds 10 to 14 days to journeys and increases costs across supply chains. The account also said that perceived threats alone could make routine voyages commercially unviable.

For Saudi Arabia, the problem is therefore not limited to whether ships can physically pass the strait. Riyadh must also make the route insurable and commercially usable while protecting its territory from Houthi drones and ballistic missiles.

Riyadh seeks a coalition, but not necessarily a war

With US military support not forthcoming, Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman’s administration is working to assemble a multinational coalition involving regional and Western states. Analysts said the likely strategy is to increase military pressure on the Houthis while maintaining diplomatic engagement with Iran in the hope that Tehran will restrain its Yemeni ally.

Saudi Arabia hopes Pakistan and Turkey, alongside Britain, France and Egypt, will provide defensive assistance. Possible contributions include air defence, counter-drone systems, intelligence, surveillance, training, munitions and personnel. Western powers could add naval escorts, mine-countermeasure capabilities, surveillance and protection for international shipping.

The coalition may nevertheless be designed to enable Saudi operations rather than conduct an offensive inside Yemen. Andreas Krieg of King’s College London said Egypt had a strong interest in Bab el-Mandeb because the strait’s security affects the Suez Canal, but no appetite for another expeditionary operation in Yemen. Pakistan and Turkey could support Saudi territory without invading Yemen.

Washington remains difficult to replace in intelligence, targeting data, early warning and command-and-control systems, Krieg said, even if it refuses to bomb Yemen itself. Saudi Arabia could therefore build substantial support around the conflict while remaining largely alone in any direct offensive.

Pressure to negotiate

The military choices are tied to a diplomatic and financial dilemma. A temporary framework agreement between Iran and Oman, due to be signed on Monday, was reportedly blocked by Saudi objections because it would have granted Tehran permanent privileges over the waterway without guaranteeing that commercial shipping could resume. Iran had linked reopening Hormuz to the lifting of a US naval blockade and other conditions under the Islamabad Declaration in June.

Analysts said Riyadh may eventually have to make concessions to both Iran and the Houthis. Giorgio Cafiero of Gulf State Analytics described the kingdom as having no good options, while Bernard Haykel of Princeton University said Saudi Arabia would sooner or later need to “pay off” both parties to stop attacks. Krieg suggested that some Saudi financial compensation could emerge, while distinguishing between payments to the Houthis and payments to Iran.

The standoff is also affecting Europe. Its oil and gas supplies are being disrupted by conflicts in the Gulf and Eurasia, while reserves are limited and dependence on spot markets leaves buyers exposed to price swings. Ukrainian strikes on Russian refineries and export terminals have constrained another source of supply, and Kazakhstan’s main export route runs through Russia’s Black Sea port of Novorossiysk.

Middle East Eye reported that reduced supply, higher shipping costs and refinery outages were feeding into diesel, electricity and food prices. Costantinos Stambolis of the Institute of Energy for South-East Europe said eurozone inflation had reached 3.3 percent and energy inflation 14.3 percent.

For Riyadh, the immediate test is whether it can organise partners with different capabilities while keeping control of the political strategy. Choosing an open-ended war could expose the limits of Saudi power; diplomacy and compensation could help restore shipping, but would require concessions to forces that have made the kingdom’s backup route its latest vulnerability.

What happened

Survivors say overcrowding and poor ventilation turned a detention cell in Minna, Nigeria, into a deadly struggle for air, leaving 37 suspected illegal gold miners dead. The deaths, many involving teenagers, triggered protests, a curfew, official suspensions and investigations into conditions inside the cell.

A struggle for air inside the cell

The victims were found dead on Thursday in cells operated by the Nigeria Security and Civil Defence Corps (NSCDC), a paramilitary organization that works with police. The agency had raided suspected illegal gold mines and detained miners in Minna, the capital of Niger state. The New York Times reported that 66 miners had been packed into one cell; officials said 37 people died in custody.

Dauda Shehu, a survivor who spoke to journalists at a hospital on Friday, said the detainees were “jam-packed” and had no ventilation. He said people suddenly realized they could not breathe, scrambled for fresh air, appealed repeatedly to those outside and banged on the cell door, but received no response.

Another survivor, who did not give his name, said he escaped because he was near the cell’s entrance, where he could breathe better. He said detainees screamed for help and told guards that people were dying one after another, but the guards did not believe them. The New York Times reported that survivors also described barely having food or water and said people began collapsing and dying.

A video shared online showed dozens of young men, most appearing to be teenagers, lying outside in only shorts. Volunteers wearing gloves stood over the bodies as veiled women tried to identify relatives. Voices in the background cursed those responsible.

Competing accounts of what caused the deaths

The NSCDC initially attributed the deaths to a disease outbreak. But an intelligence report seen by Agence France-Presse said preliminary information pointed instead to overcrowding and inadequate ventilation.

Niger state Governor Umar Bago said many of the dead were children aged between 14 and 18 who had been working in mines instead of attending school. “There are issues of illegal mining and it is taking a toll on our children,” he said. He later confirmed the death toll at 37 and described the incident as tragic.

The NSCDC said the detainees had been arrested during raids on suspected illegal mines in the Wushishi-Lukoto area west of Minna on 15 and 16 September. The raids targeted illegal gold mining in a state rich in minerals and home to thousands of artisanal miners.

The men were buried on Thursday according to Islamic tradition, without forensic investigations. Bago declared three days of mourning and postponed the start of campaigning for elections scheduled for January.

Protests, force and official action

Protests began in Minna on Thursday evening and continued on Friday, when dozens of young people, including miners and relatives of the dead, gathered near the NSCDC office. Police cordoned off the area and fired teargas to disperse protesters. A Reuters witness said security forces also fired live rounds as protesters, some carrying sticks, machetes and traditional hunting rifles, called for justice.

Some demonstrators tried to set fire to government buildings in the Tunga neighborhood. At least one body lay in the streets, while soldiers and police patrolled the city. A miner who joined the protest, Yusuf Auwal, said the demonstrators were not thieves but people seeking a way to make a living through mining.

Authorities imposed a curfew beginning at 6 p.m. on Friday, after which security forces appeared to have temporarily restored order. Nigeria’s Interior Minister, Olubunmi Tunji-Ojo, ordered the immediate suspension of Niger state’s NSCDC commandant, Suberu Siyaka Aniviye, pending an investigation. President Bola Ahmed Tinubu also ordered an investigation, after the government suspended some senior officials.

The deaths have exposed the risks surrounding Niger state’s informal gold economy. Officials and experts say criminal gangs involved in the sector tax miners and demand a share of extracted ore. The evidence supplied does not establish the final cause of death or the outcome of the investigations.

40 years of care at risk: Somali health centre faces closure

What happened

A nearly four-decade-old health centre in Laas Caanood, Sool region, is at risk of closure after severe funding shortfalls restricted its services. The facility provides free maternal and child healthcare to thousands, while drought, malnutrition and wider reductions in Somalia’s health network are increasing demand.

Assanti Abdi, a displaced woman of Ethiopian origin living in Laas Caanood, said she delivered all four of her children at the Central Mother and Child Health Centre. Her children were vaccinated there, and her family returned whenever they needed medical treatment.

“We don’t know where we will go if this facility closes,” she said.

The centre has served families in Laas Caanood for nearly 40 years. Its free services have been especially important for families with few alternatives, but Assanti said they have become increasingly limited since last year. Severe funding shortages have now pushed the facility closer to shutting down, threatening access to maternal and child health services for people with limited resources.

A functioning centre with shrinking resources

Maryam Abdinasir, the centre’s officer-in-charge, said funding cuts had severely affected operations. All 18 medical staff are currently working on a voluntary basis.

Despite those constraints, the centre treated 722 children in August, including 567 children under five. More than 450 pregnant and breastfeeding women received health services, 24 mothers delivered safely there and 155 children were vaccinated against preventable diseases.

The facility receives some vaccines through the authorities, allowing immunization services to continue. Other essential services face significant shortages. Nurse Kowser Abdirashid said mothers brought sick or malnourished children to the centre almost every day, but staff often had little to offer them.

The building was damaged during armed confrontations that affected Laas Caanood and surrounding areas in 2023. Parts of the centre still require rehabilitation before operations can fully resume and expand. Maryam identified three immediate priorities: repairing the infrastructure, replenishing medical supplies and providing incentives for healthcare workers so they can continue serving the community.

Without those basic needs, she said, it becomes difficult to provide quality care, and the consequences for mothers and children would be severe if the facility failed.

Part of a wider contraction in Somali healthcare

The centre’s predicament reflects a broader reduction in health services across Somalia. According to the World Health Organization, 380 health facilities in 11 regions either closed or operated at reduced capacity between January and June. Most were primary healthcare units serving thousands of Somalis.

At least 115 mobile health units have also closed this year, removing the only source of care for many remote and hard-to-reach communities. Thirty hospitals are operating below capacity, while at least 10 public hospitals in the northern districts of Laas Caanood, Badhan and Buuhoodle are struggling to remain operational.

The pressure comes as needs increase. In the first six months of the year, admissions of Somali children with severe acute malnutrition and medical complications to stabilization centres rose by 32 per cent compared with the same period last year, according to UNICEF.

The worsening drought is adding to the strain in Sool and other northern regions, including Sanaag, Bari, Nugaal and Mudug. Up to 1.9 million people in these areas are estimated to be acutely food insecure. More than 75 per cent of traditional water sources in Sool and nearby Sanaag have dried up, and more than 165,000 people across the two regions have been directly affected by drought.

In Sool, which is home to about 600,000 people, years of instability and underinvestment have limited access to basic services. The possible closure of the Laas Caanood centre would therefore come as the region faces overlapping pressures on food, water, nutrition and healthcare.

The framework’s Optimizer runs an iterative three-stage loop. First, Self-Diagnosis examines retrieval outcomes to identify where the current index is failing. A revised key might make a document’s specific knowledge more explicit, distinguish it from broadly similar documents, or connect an existing passage to the task described by a query.

The validation criteria include whether a key is faithful to the source document, specific to that document, and sufficiently separated from other keys.
