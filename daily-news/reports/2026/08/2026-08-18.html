Earthquake Leaves Thousands of Colombian Children Out of School

What happened

Thousands of Colombian children are still out of class after the Aug. 10 magnitude 7.4 earthquake, with Pereira alone reporting more than 58,000 temporarily sidelined and only 15 of its 165 schools able to reopen after urgent repairs, while rain keeps worsening conditions for displaced families and the question of safe return remains unresolved.

Authorities have recorded varying degrees of damage at more than 3,200 schools across the country, and the disruption is most severe where buildings, power, water and internet access were already fragile. In Pereira, the capital of Risaralda, Interior Minister Rodrigo Lara said on Saturday that the city’s schools were in critical condition and called the situation “an educational tragedy on a national scale.”

Pereira’s classrooms are still unusable

The scale of the school closures is easiest to see in Pereira, where Lara said 165 schools were damaged and only 15 could resume operations after urgent repairs. The city alone has temporarily left more than 58,000 children out of school, and that figure captures only one part of the wider disruption caused by the earthquake.

At Jesus Maria Ormaza School, students were beginning their second class period when the ground started shaking violently. Principal Luis Caicedo said the quake left the building badly damaged, with walls and roofs affected and the roof of the teachers’ room collapsed. He said there are “many damaged walls,” and the school is now setting up a relief and collection point for food, medicines and clothing while staff decide how to support students.

Caicedo said the school has experience with virtual learning, but that option is limited because many children are at home without computers and without power in many areas. He also said up to 120 of the school’s 800 students lost their homes and are now staying in shelters.

Rural students are waiting on inspections, not online classes

The educational emergency is even harder to solve in remote places. In La Encharca, a rural village in the municipality of Medio San Juan in Chocó, the school can only be reached by boat along the Suruco River and has remained without electricity, potable water or connectivity since the earthquake.

Community leader John Ibarbo said the children were able only to approach their teachers and hug each other “with that fear” after the shaking. He said some classroom walls and community halls have cracks, and the students have not had classes since then while they wait for a technical inspection by regional authorities to determine whether the building is safe and what repairs it needs.

Ibarbo also rejected the idea of shifting classes online as a practical fix. He said it would not benefit most families because signal coverage is poor in the area and many households do not have computers or cellphones. The municipality lies in a region where the Clan del Golfo drug-trafficking group and the National Liberation Army, or ELN, have historically operated, adding another layer of difficulty to the response.

Rain has made the shelters harder to live in

The earthquake’s aftermath has been made worse by heavy rain. On Sunday night, downpours in Pereira worsened conditions for families staying in improvised camps after losing their homes, particularly at Olaya Herrera Park, one of the main refuge sites in the city.

During the night, tents installed by the city government had to be covered with plastic so water would not soak the few belongings families managed to save from the earthquake. The park’s ground also became completely waterlogged, making it even harder for people who must spend the night there while waiting for a housing solution.

The rain also affected San Jorge University Hospital in Pereira, where flooding occurred. Videos shared on social media showed leaks and damaged pipes in some areas of the medical facility, although the extent of the rainfall damage had not been reported as of now.

For now, the people affected are waiting on inspections, repairs and housing decisions before school life can resume. In Pereira, that means classrooms remain shut even as emergency crews try to keep children and families in place; in La Encharca, it means children are still waiting to learn whether their school can safely reopen at all.

What happened

Families in Kigonze, a displacement site in Ituri, are confronting Ebola while trying to rebuild lives after conflict, and a red-roofed transit centre has become both a medical shield and a test of trust for the site’s 18,500 residents.

Esther’s illness began as stomach pain and vomiting

When 19-year-old Esther developed severe stomach pain and vomiting, she tried to treat herself but was not getting better. During a door-to-door visit in Kigonze, community health volunteers recognized her symptoms, called an ambulance and took her to the hospital. Her mother and younger sister were sick too.

Esther says her mother died before she learned that they had tested positive for Ebola. Esther then spent more than three weeks in an Ebola treatment centre. She survived, and says she held on to hope because the treatment had already made her feel much better.

Her experience came after years of displacement. Esther was 11 when she fled Drodro in Ituri with her family. She remembers homes on fire, hiding until the shooting stopped, and walking nearly 20 kilometres to reach safety. Years later, Kigonze became home, but the move brought a different kind of hardship: rebuilding a life while remaining vulnerable to new shocks. Esther’s father later died of illness at the site as the family struggled to rebuild.

A small centre became the first line of defence

Rising above hundreds of shelters in Kigonze, a red-roofed building has become a symbol of protection for the site’s residents. Since mid-May, eastern Democratic Republic of the Congo has been battling an Ebola outbreak that spread beyond its epicentre in Ituri, adding another layer of fear for communities already affected by conflict and displacement.

The eight-bed transit centre at Kigonze, operated by International Medical Corps with funding from the U.S. Department of State, serves as the community’s first line of defence against Ebola. Suspected cases can be isolated and tested there before being referred to specialized treatment centres if they test positive.

Dr. Sambo Sidikou, IMC’s Ebola treatment centre coordinator, said early response can help stop the virus before it spreads in a densely populated displacement site. He added that acceptance and trust are key, and said hygienists, nurses and even some doctors come from the displaced community. He also said isolation should not mean cutting people off from their families. Relatives can still visit if they follow infection prevention measures.

For people in Kigonze, the centre is more than a medical facility. It is part of a response increasingly rooted in the community itself, where most people working there are displaced too. That includes Mbusi, who arrived in Kigonze in 2018 after armed groups looted Drodro. She said they killed her father and abducted her brother, and that the family felt it had no choice but to leave. Her two younger children were born at the site. Now a hygienist at the Ebola Transit Centre, she said: “There is no difference between war and Ebola.”

Odette also found her way back to health care after displacement forced her to leave her village and lose her nursing job. She took market work, odd jobs and temporary hospital and pharmacy positions before joining the Kigonze Ebola Transit Centre. She said she wanted to help her community. She also recalled a time when people were afraid to seek care, saying they used to think Ebola was deliberately injected at the hospital. She believes health workers from the displaced community have helped build trust and encouraged earlier care-seeking.

Survival has become part of the response

The response in Kigonze is also shaped by people who know displacement from the inside. Dr. Moïse Lobho Ngaba, who trained in Goma and was recruited by IMC to help run the Ebola Transit Centre, also remembers fleeing conflict in Djugu. His great-uncle lives at the site, and he encourages neighbours to come to the centre and see his nephew when they are ill or afraid to seek care. He said these connections can make a difference when rumours, misinformation and fear discourage people from seeking treatment.

That fear has had real consequences. In June alone, more than half of the attacks affecting humanitarian workers happened while they were supporting the Ebola response, according to the evidence. Nearly one million people in Ituri have been displaced by conflict, and many families have spent much of their lives in displacement sites where overcrowding and limited services increase vulnerability during outbreaks.

Esther is now part of the response herself. After surviving Ebola, she works in the nursery unit of the Ebola treatment centre, caring for children who have tested positive for Ebola or are suspected of having it. The centre considered her an ideal candidate because she had survived the virus, and she accepted. She says she works “in the service of God.”

Her role has brought her back through the same doors that once led her to treatment. Now she comforts frightened children and tells others not to wait when they become sick: “Don’t be afraid. Don’t stay in your shelter and die. If you feel unwell, go to the hospital.” In Kigonze, survival has become something shared — and, for families still living with displacement, a source of strength as well as hope.

What happened

Mocha’s shoreline economy has given way to a heavy military presence, with fighters, repeated Houthi strikes and displaced families reshaping a city once known for fishing and coffee, and residents now weighing security against the loss of their livelihoods.

In Yemen’s coastal city of Mocha, the dominant dialect is no longer that of the native fishermen who grew up relying on the sea. Instead, it is the dialect of fighters who fled Houthi-controlled areas. Once a small fishing town, Mocha has become a busier place where almost all essential services are available, largely because fighters have settled there with their families.

The city’s transformation sits on top of a much older history. Mocha is a centuries-old trading city on Yemen’s southwestern coast along the Red Sea, in Taiz governorate, and its port was once world-famous for exporting Yemeni coffee. That changed after the civil war erupted in 2014 between the Houthis and the internationally recognised government: the port was closed in 2015, then reopened in 2021 after being rehabilitated by the Republican Guard, a faction of Yemen’s former elite military units led by Tareq Saleh and backed by the UAE. Saleh had initially fought alongside the Houthis, but after his uncle, former President Ali Abdullah Saleh, was killed by the group in December 2017, he fled Sanaa and began building his own forces in Mocha. Since then, the city has served as the Republican Guard’s headquarters.

Fighters, families and a changing city

Mocha’s new role has drawn in more people than soldiers alone. Many Yemenis fleeing Houthi-controlled areas have joined the Republican Guard there, attracted by higher salaries paid in Saudi Riyals. The result is a city where the fighter population has become so visible that one resident said walking through Mocha can feel like being back in Dhamar because of all the familiar faces.

The city’s growth has also pushed families outward. Ibrahim Salem, a fighter in his 40s who originally comes from Dhamar governorate, said he joined the Republican Guards in Mocha seven months ago after escaping Houthi-controlled areas, where he had previously served as a Houthi fighter. He said he decided not to keep his family in Mocha because of the danger, renting a home for them in a village in Taiz’s al-Maafer district instead. Another fighter, Ramez, said he had moved his family to a village away from Mocha from the start, arguing that it was safer and cheaper than a city close to military targets.

Both men described a wider pattern of fighters moving relatives out of the city and into rural areas in Taiz and Lahj governorates. Salem said this was now his family’s second displacement, first from Dhamar to Mocha and then from Mocha to a rural locality.

A strategic port under repeated attack

Mocha’s location has made it militarily important and increasingly exposed. About 60km from the Bab al-Mandab Strait, the city holds a strategic position on the Red Sea. The Houthis have targeted it repeatedly in the last fortnight, and more than 25 Houthi attacks have struck the Port of Mocha over the past week.

The strikes have brought direct civilian consequences. While the Houthis said they were targeting military reinforcements and positions, government officials said 16 civilians were killed and 22 others were injured in Friday’s strikes alone. As the attacks continued, operations at the seaport ground to a halt, leaving more than 1,500 port workers without a livelihood.

For residents who once depended on the sea, that collapse has been devastating. Akram, a fisherman in his 40s, said local fishermen are no longer allowed to sail because of the escalation, and many believe choosing Mocha as the Republican Guards’ headquarters stripped them of their jobs. He said the town had gone from peaceful fishing life to a place where “the sea used to be our primary source of income, but today it feels more like a battlefield.”

Akram also said some fishermen had abandoned their trade to join the Republican Guard because the fighters’ salaries, paid in Saudi Riyals, were as much as four times what a fisherman typically earned. But he said the city’s residents were still paying the price. Another resident, Zaher, who works as a driver for an international humanitarian organisation, said he was glad to see Mocha grow and attract aid groups and new investment, but argued that safety had to come first. “All we need is security,” he said, adding that people in the city wanted the Houthis to avoid targeting civilian infrastructure.

For Mocha, the conflict has turned a port town once known for coffee and fishing into a place defined by fighters, displacement and repeated attack, with little agreement on whether the city’s new bustle is survival or ruin.
