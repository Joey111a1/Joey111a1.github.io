Rise in migrant deaths as 19 bodies found along Arizona border last month
global_front_pageglobal_south_and_exploitedpolitics_and_powermaterial_harmanchor
What happened

The remains of 19 migrants, including children, were recovered along the Arizona-Mexico border last month, the highest monthly death toll in the area since 2024, as advocates warn that the desert’s real death count is still far higher than the records show.

Newly released data from the medical examiner’s office in Pima County, Arizona, shows that the 19 remains pushed the total found this year to 73, which is 14 more than at the same point last year. The figures add another grim month to a long-running pattern in the Arizona desert, where deaths are tracked by local authorities and by Humane Borders, a nonprofit that rescues migrants from dehydration and exposure.

Experts and immigration advocates say the latest toll reflects US policy abroad and at home, including the recent blocking of asylum seekers. They say people are still making the journey through Mexico despite extreme temperatures and the threat of arrest by ICE.

A route that keeps taking lives

Octavio Fuentes, executive director of Humane Borders, said the current administration has been effective at “closing legal ports of entry” and making it “darn near impossible” for people to enter the country “the right way.” The group works alongside Pima County to track migrant deaths, and its data shows that at least 4,505 migrants have died in the Arizona desert over the last 25 years, or roughly one death every other day.

The causes are often the same. Humane Borders says the most frequent are dehydration and exposure, but people also die from hypothermia or from blunt force trauma after passing out and hitting their heads on rocks. The latest numbers, Fuentes and other advocates argue, show that danger has not diminished even as the border has become harder to cross legally.

Fuentes also pointed to a longer enforcement history. He said that since Operation Gatekeeper in 1994 under the Clinton administration, the approach has been “prevention through deterrence,” pushing crossings away from safer, more obvious places and into remote terrain. “Out of desperation, people think, ‘How else can I get in?’” he said. “And that’s what has driven people into the Arizona desert for the last 32 years.”

Why the count may still be incomplete

Brad Jones, a Humane Borders board member and a professor with the Global Migration Center at the University of California, Davis, said the latest figures show a high number of “contemporaneous” deaths compared with recent years. In other words, he said, the deaths appear to have happened recently rather than reflecting old remains that were only just found.

Jones said the desert often hides evidence quickly, and that it is often “random happenstance” when a hiker or law enforcement officer comes across human remains. Scavengers and animals can make remains “disappear without a trace pretty quickly,” he said, which means the known toll is almost certainly too low.

He said estimates suggest the true number of migrant deaths could be as high as four times what has been found. “For every migrant’s remains that you find, how many do you not find?” he asked.

The broader pattern, according to the advocates and researchers quoted in the data, is that people are still taking the risk because the alternatives feel worse. Amelia Frank-Vitale, an anthropology professor at Princeton University, said many migrants feel they have no choice at all, even as US policies and ICE enforcement make the journey more dangerous.

What happened

Ceuta buried the first 18 migrants who died in the July 30-31 crossing at the border pier, but most of the dead still have no names and Morocco has not accepted their return, leaving families and officials with numbered graves and an unresolved repatriation effort.

The final burial took place Friday afternoon at the Sidi Embarek Muslim cemetery after 82 people died in the mass entry, mostly men and one woman. A dozen imams from the city’s mosques joined the public burial, and Health Counselor Nabila Benzina said only nine of the deceased have been identified.

An emergency burial

The bodies were laid on their right side, facing Mecca, with care that the earth would not touch them. The ceremony began shortly before the five o’clock prayer and ended at eight, in an expanded area of the cemetery that had already been prepared for the burials.

The setup showed how unusual the day was for Ceuta’s only Muslim cemetery, which handles about 20 funerals a month. Six gravediggers, wearing suits, gloves and masks, worked in humid afternoon heat beside a trench with concrete walls, wooden planks and piles of sand. “We have summoned all staff,” Benzina said. “We have never done this before.”

The first deceased were taken from the hearse at 18:23 in four white funeral bags and placed on four orange stretchers. City imams then began praying as the bodies were lowered into the graves, with Qur’anic verses recited by the imam with the most authority in the group. Later batches were buried in the same way, with the soil covered by clay bricks and sand and water sprayed to reduce dust.

Graves with numbers only

The headstones for the dead were identified only by numbers because their names and stories are unknown. Their cases remain tied to a judicial file with limited information: where each was found, physical characteristics, clothing and any details that could link them to their families.

Benzina said the dead are still difficult to return because Morocco is not accepting handover to families. She said the funeral home has tried twice, but “the reasons are not known.” Some relatives have come to mourn, she said, but not to reunite with their loved one in their country.

Morocco and the families

Benzina said the authorities will keep trying, although time is short because of health recommendations. The funeral home will continue attempting to send the identified dead to Morocco, while the burial work is due to continue on Saturday and over the coming days until all the bodies are interred.

There is little expectation that exhumations will follow if victims are later identified. Based on Ceuta’s experience, families of Moroccan or Sub-Saharan victims who died trying to reach the city by swimming usually do not claim the remains, although there have been Algerian victims repatriated. For now, the graves in Sidi Embarek remain marked by numbers, and by the effort to give the dead a dignified burial even while their identities are still incomplete.

What happened

Haiti received its first deportation flight from the United States since Temporary Protected Status was revoked, bringing more than 160 Haitian nationals to Cap-Haïtien and deepening concern in a city locals say is already stretched by displacement, overcrowded services and the prospect of more arrivals.

A first flight lands in Cap-Haïtien

The charter flight landed in Cap-Haïtien on Thursday, when Haiti’s National Migration Office said 161 people stepped down a little after noon. It was the first deportation flight to Haiti from the US since the revocation of Temporary Protected Status, or TPS, which had protected about 350,000 Haitians in the US from deportation and given them legal work permits.

The arrival took place in Cap-Haïtien rather than Port-au-Prince because the capital’s main international airport is considered too dangerous. The US government has also kept a ban on US commercial flights to Haiti’s capital through early September because of ongoing gang violence. Many of the deportees were dressed in white and covered their faces as journalists filmed them. Nearly all declined to speak, and some appeared to fear being targeted by gangs.

DHS Secretary Markwayne Mullin posted on X Thursday morning, hours before the flight took off: “It’s a great day to deport illegal aliens. Good morning America!”

A city already under strain

Cap-Haïtien has been a major hub for internally displaced people since Haiti’s devastating earthquake in 2010, and locals say it is already crowded with people fleeing disaster and violence from elsewhere in the country. The city is also the first-choice destination for the Trump administration to deport Haitian migrants.

Some residents warned that the city cannot absorb many more arrivals. Demonstène Reynold, a mechanic in Cap-Haïtien, said: “Cap-Haitian alone can’t receive more people.” He said the city is already receiving people from the country’s interior, including Hinche and Port-au-Prince, because of the political crisis.

CNN described street traffic as snarled and trash collection sites as overflowing. At a hospital visited by CNN, patients were queuing outdoors in the heat. Louis Ronald Espoir, an administrator at Hospital Fort Saint Michel, said the city’s medical facilities are at capacity. “As you can see here with your own eyes, all the hospitals in the region are full despite the best efforts of the government,” he said. “If they’re full with the current population, I’d be lying to say we can take in anyone else.”

Angie Bell, the city’s former mayor, said Cap-Haïtien needed more development and more security before it could safely take in large numbers of returnees. She estimated the population had risen from around 350,000 in the 2003 census to at least 800,000, adding that a lack of housing has pushed many people to live on the outskirts while working in the city.

The TPS fight and what comes next

The flight came after the Trump administration’s legal victory this month to remove temporary deportation protections from hundreds of thousands of Haitians in the US. The end of TPS followed a Supreme Court ruling backing the administration’s effort to end the program and a later decision by Washington DC district judge Ana Reyes removing a stay on deportations.

The administration has repeatedly argued that Haiti is safe enough for Haitians to be sent back, despite a State Department “Do not travel” advisory and the flight ban to Port-au-Prince. Haitian communities in the US are now confronting the loss of protection, while immigrant-rights advocates warn about the dangers of sending people back to Haiti.

In Cap-Haïtien, the immediate question is how many more flights will follow. Haitian officials have already been told to expect an increase in removals, and locals say the city is struggling to make room for the people already arriving.

What happened

Peru’s southern Andes were hit by a 7.2 earthquake on August 20, 2026, affecting communities in Ayacucho, Arequipa and Ica and reviving concern that scaled-back civil protection budgets and weak infrastructure leave towns exposed to the next disaster.

The quake’s epicenter was in Parinacochas, near Coracora in the Ayacucho region, and monitoring agencies registered it at a depth of between 80 and 100 kilometers. Because of that intermediate depth, the shaking was felt across a broad area, including parts of Arequipa, Ica, Apurímac and Cusco, and even in high-rise buildings in Lima. Initial assessments coordinated by the National Institute of Civil Defense, or INDECI, and regional authorities said the damage was concentrated in rural districts and small urban centers.

Local reports described damage to unreinforced masonry structures and traditional adobe houses. Preliminary reports said 50 homes, 6 schools and 4 health centers were affected in rural districts. Mountain passes connected to the Interoceanic Highway were also temporarily blocked by rockfalls and landslides triggered by the shaking.

The event landed in a country already described as highly exposed to natural hazards. Peru sits on the Pacific Ring of Fire, where the Nazca Plate subducts beneath the South American Plate, making it susceptible to frequent earthquakes and tsunami risks along the coast. It also faces regular hydrometeorological hazards linked to the El Niño-Southern Oscillation cycle, including heavy rainfall, flash floods and huaicos, or mudslides, that affect both coastal cities and rural Andean valleys.

The broader debate is about more than geology. The evidence points to decades of unmanaged urban growth, uneven compliance with building codes and administrative centralism shaping how communities experience and recover from disasters. It also says public financial reporting has chronic under-execution of Budget Program 0068, the fund for disaster prevention and emergency reduction, while regional and municipal authorities often lack the technical teams needed for projects such as seismic retrofitting, hillside containment and river stabilization.
