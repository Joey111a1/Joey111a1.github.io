Ebola frontline workers missing wages as outbreak spreads ‘exponentially’
global_front_pageglobal_south_and_exploitedpublic_healthpolitics_and_powereconomy_and_businessmaterial_harmanchor
What happened

Frontline Ebola workers in the north-east of the Democratic Republic of the Congo are going to work without pay while the outbreak accelerates, leaving nurses and hygiene staff worried about infection, family exposure and whether the response can keep up.

Sophie Mwadawa Kabundo, a nurse at an Ebola treatment centre, said she went to work every day afraid she would contract the virus and pass it to family and friends. She said more and more confirmed cases were being admitted, some colleagues had died, and staff were worried that workplace accidents could put them in even greater danger.

Martin Bolombi, her colleague at the Elikya treatment centre in Bunia, in Ituri province, said the outbreak was causing him “a lot of stress”, but that he had no choice but to keep working until the situation was brought under control. Kabundo said she and other workers were under “immense pressure” because they were exposed to infection and feared their families and other loved ones might contract the disease because of them.

Wages withheld on the Ebola wards

The workers said the strain was made worse by missing wages. Bolombi, a hygiene worker, said he had been working since May and had only been paid for June. He was among about 100 people who protested outside the Elikya treatment centre during a strike last month over unpaid bonuses.

Kabundo said she was also missing wages and called on the government to provide solutions as soon as possible. She said they were not being paid even as they continued to face the risks of working in a treatment centre while patients kept arriving.

Bolombi said he was working seven days a week with barely any time to see his family. Even so, he said logistics had improved compared with the early days of the response, though not enough to meet growing needs as the disease progressed. Kabundo, despite the lack of remuneration, said there had been “a large number of recoveries” at Elikya and argued that the progress should be recognised, but not used as a reason to relax.

An outbreak growing faster than the response

The unpaid wages sit inside a wider response that the senior UN Ebola coordinator, Julien Harneis, said was being overtaken. Speaking on a video call from Bunia, he said more than 2,500 people had died and that the outbreak was spreading exponentially in an area larger than France. He warned that existing funds would run out within weeks and said 160 healthcare workers had fallen ill with Ebola, 43 of them fatally. “The outbreak is growing faster and wider than the Ebola response,” he said.

Harneis also said the DRC was dealing with an epidemic in an area that had suffered three decades of conflict and generated huge humanitarian needs. The World Health Organization estimates that the outbreak is on course to exceed west Africa’s 2014-16 Ebola outbreak as the biggest ever globally. That earlier outbreak recorded more than 28,600 cases and 11,325 deaths across Guinea, Liberia and Sierra Leone, according to WHO data.

The infections in the DRC are of the rare Bundibugyo species of the virus. There are no approved treatments or vaccines for that strain, though trials for both are under way. The outbreak has spread to six of the country’s 26 provinces and is concentrated in the north-east, with more than 4,200 confirmed cases in Ituri.

Unpaid wages are only one of the problems slowing efforts to contain the disease. Misinformation, community mistrust and infrastructure deficits are also hampering the response. Dr Eddy Kambale Kahandukya, a deputy medical coordinator for Médecins Sans Frontières in the DRC, said lack of community engagement remained a major challenge in breaking the chain of transmission. He said patients who suspected they had symptoms and were waiting for laboratory results sometimes sought refuge in the community and created new clusters that fuelled spread.

Fear was also feeding mistrust, said Manon Dumortier, an emergency coordinator in Bunia for Mercy Corps. She said people were seeing many deaths without understanding what was happening, while others disappeared into health centres and were buried before relatives could see them, which had encouraged speculation and rumours.

The outbreak also reached Uganda, where authorities reported 20 cases and two deaths, all in Kampala, before declaring the country Ebola-free on 28 July after the last patient, a Congolese national, was discharged on 16 July.

This is our home,” Wina said.

Their work sits alongside a separate legal fight. In December last year, a Swiss court agreed to hear a climate case filed by Asmania and three other Pari Island residents against Holcim, seeking financial compensation for climate-related loss and an order for the cement giant to reduce its greenhouse gas emissions.

But on Pari Island itself, the longer-running dispute is over land ownership. In 2014, PT Bumi Pari Asri claimed rights over a large part of the island. Islanders and rights groups have challenged how land ownership certificates and right-to-build permits were issued, while the company’s field supervisor, Slamet Huzaini, said the certificates were obtained legally from the North Jakarta National Land Agency. PT Bumi Pari Asri says it owns about 90 per cent of the island, with 62 freehold certificates and 14 right-to-build permits.

Residents say the stakes are personal: they have lived on Pari for generations and fear eviction if the company’s claims stand. In 2018, the Indonesian Ombudsman found maladministration in relation to PT Bumi Pari Asri’s certificates, but the dispute has still not been definitively resolved.

What happened

Women in the Associação de Caranguejeiros e Amigos dos Mangue de Magé (ACAMM) in Baixada Fluminense, Rio de Janeiro, are turning ubarana, a fish long undervalued by local fishers, into income through the Cozinha das Caranguejeiras, where traditional recipes, community meals and environmental work now shape the group’s livelihood.

The women make dishes such as strogonoff, escondidinho, meatballs, fritters, coxinhas, croquettes, kibbeh and risoles from ubarana at the ACAMM headquarters. Their method, passed down through generations, helped add value to a fish that had not been commercially prized. The meals are sold at special lunches backed by invitation sales to the community, visitors, tourist groups, researchers and companies, as well as through orders.

Márcia Regina, a fisher for 40 years and now retired from the activity, said the lunches bring in tourists, university people and others studying environmental issues, along with companies and people interested in the group’s work. She said that helps support the women crab harvesters. “We have been helping 15 families,” she said, adding that the kitchen also has a social role because women sometimes arrive depressed and leave feeling better after talking, sharing stories and supporting one another.

The project grew out of older local knowledge in Rio Suruí, in the mangrove area on the recôncavo of Guanabara Bay. According to Márcia, the women once lived from crab harvesting and also prepared fish for their own consumption when husbands brought it home from fishing. Later, they began working through the association and started selling what they made. Rafael Santos, president of ACAMM, said the fish had first been offered only as a snack in association meetings, until someone from outside suggested selling it to generate income and strengthen the chain of women already preparing it at home.

Biologist Verônica Souza, from Projeto Andadas Ecológicas of the NGO Guardiões do Mar, said ubarana lives in coastal and estuarine environments, including mangroves across Guanabara Bay, but its many fine bones make filleting difficult and keep its commercial value low. She said the women found a way to process the meat so the bones are not noticed, showing how community culinary knowledge can change how a species is valued.

The kitchen won a temporary physical address in 2021 with support from an edital of Funbio, then moved three years later to a larger space inside ACAMM after another edital. The group says its work also connects to mangrove conservation, including cleaning and replanting, and to community-based tourism and environmental education in Magé.

What happened

Honeybee queens may not be passive victims of pesticide exposure: when chemicals build up over time, they can pass some of that burden into their eggs, a defense that may help the queen but could leave the colony facing an unresolved cost.

Researchers led by the University of California, Davis say worker bees normally act as a hive’s first line of defense, filtering harmful substances before they spread. But in honeybees, they found queens can also defend themselves through what the scientists call maternal offloading — moving pesticides from their own bodies into their eggs. The findings, published in *Current Biology*, are described as the first evidence in honeybees of a queen reducing her chemical burden this way.

Tracking where the pesticides went

To study the process, the team built small “nanocolonies” with one queen and 60 worker bees. The bees were given pollen, water and food contaminated with methyl parathion, and the pesticide carried a low-level radioactive marker so researchers could follow it through the hive. On day one, worker bees filtered out 95% of the pesticide and moved it into the honeycomb, but by day 10 that figure had fallen to 86%.

Sascha Nicklisch, the paper’s senior author, said the queen “offloads these chemicals into her eggs to get rid of them,” adding that no one had shown this in honeybees before. Angela Encerrado-Manriquez, the lead author, said the results suggest worker filtration can be overwhelmed over time, after which queens have their own defense.

The study, which also involved Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory and the U.S. Department of Agriculture’s Agricultural Research Service, raises questions about how long queens can keep transferring contaminants, what that means for colony health, and whether the effect differs by pesticide. The researchers said it could matter for beekeepers, growers and integrated-pest-management planning, especially when colonies are foraging or expanding.
