What happened

Tulshi Patel traced her mother and aunt’s last known location to the Nepal-China border from a TikTok video, turning a social-media clip into a key clue as the flood’s death toll climbed and search teams faced a widening list of missing people across Nepal and Tibet.

The TikTok clue at Friendship Bridge

Patel said her mother, Hemangini Jitendra Patel, and her aunt, Hema Sunil Amin, had set off on a pilgrimage that was meant to take them into Tibet, but they had not been contactable since the flash floods hit. Speaking to LBC’s Matt Frei, Patel said she had last spoken to them on Monday and received a photo on Tuesday. Their last known location, she said, came from a GPS signal from the tour group at a place called Friendship Bridge at the Nepal-China border.

She said a TikTok video then helped confirm where they had been. “We did our own deep-dive research and we found a TikTok video that verified and showed my mum and auntie and three other people from the tour at the border waiting to cross, I guess, that the local Nepali tour guide had made,” Patel said. She said the video was posted on Wednesday morning, just before 6am Nepal time. The women had been due to cross on Tuesday, but were delayed and crossed on Wednesday, she said.

Patel described the trip as something her mother and aunt had talked about since the start of the year and said it had been “on their bucket list.” She said they had been in good health and had prepared well for the journey.

A flood that moved fast and left little warning

The flood that hit Nepal and Tibet on Wednesday tore down valleys and swept away entire villages, with at least 675 people killed and more than 3,000 still missing, according to the figures cited in the supplied reporting. Among those missing are 33 British nationals, including a 13-year-old, NHS workers and a priest.

Axios said the disaster exposed gaps in warning systems built to watch for extreme rain and swollen rivers, not sudden collapses high in the mountains during clear weather. The U.S. Geological Survey said a section of glacier and mountain debris collapsed on the north side of Lāngtāng Lirung, creating energy comparable to a magnitude 5.2 earthquake, though no earthquake triggered it. USGS investigators said the collapsed glacier flow likely gathered more ice, water, rock and sediment, rapidly amplifying into a stronger mass that caused the flooding.

Axios also reported that the flood is considered a “blue-sky flood,” a type of glacier-driven flood that can happen under clear skies. It said there were no typical warning signs and that Nepal’s flood-warning system focuses on rainfall, river levels and larger river systems, which meant it did not catch the event as it surged downward from the mountains.

Search efforts and the widening toll

The supplied reporting says the disaster mainly hit Gyirong County in Tibet and Nepal’s Rasuwa district, and that authorities warned more water could soon rush through the valleys along the Chinese border, threatening the search for survivors. The Guardian reported that poor weather has hampered aerial rescue operations and that rescuers have been trying to reach people trapped in tunnels and other damaged sites.

The situation has also drawn in families outside Nepal. Patel said she had messaged her mother and aunt “in the hopes that maybe we’ll see a tick to say they’ve seen it or read it,” and had tried to contact the tour guide. She said the family had considered going to Nepal to join the search, but decided against it because they did not know how close they could get to help.

The broader search remains uncertain as the number of missing continues to shift in the reporting. The Guardian’s updates said Nepal’s disaster agency reported 2,426 people missing on its side of the border, while China’s Xinhua news agency reported 554 missing in Tibet. Another Guardian update said the total missing had risen to nearly 3,000 and that the death toll in Nepal had reached at least 675.

What happened

Pakistan suspended eight officials and ordered criminal proceedings after a fire killed 14 babies in the neonatal ward of Islamabad’s Pakistan Institute of Medical Sciences, leaving investigators still trying to explain how a nursery blaze could spread so fast and expose so many safety failures.

Prime Minister Shehbaz Sharif issued the orders on Saturday after an investigative committee presented its interim report, his office said. The fire broke out shortly before 7am on Wednesday in the Mother and Child Healthcare Centre at PIMS, starting inside the nursery before spreading rapidly through oxygen outlets fitted at each incubator. Officials said the fire was confined to a third-floor nursery, but staff were able to rescue only one baby from among the 15 being cared for there.

Sharif said the deaths of the children were a “heartbreaking tragedy” and promised “exemplary punishment” for those responsible. The government also approved action against a company responsible for hospital security and its officials, and appointed the National Institutes of Health chief executive as PIMS’s acting director in the interim.

What the inquiry said went wrong

The interim inquiry said the hospital had no meaningful fire safety protocols in place. It found that only two of PIMS’s 13 standard operating procedures dealt with fire or emergencies, and the nursery had no fire alarm or sprinkler system. The report also said supervisory staff were not on duty when the fire started.

According to the statement, only two nurses, a female security guard and a house officer were in the ward. Emergency services were not called until six minutes after the fire began, and the first responders arrived 15 minutes after receiving the call. Investigators also noted that a separate fire had broken out at the hospital’s nursing hostel a month earlier without leading to safety improvements.

One nurse, Razia Noreen, pulled a child to safety before the flames engulfed the ward. Sharif awarded her the Sitara-i-Khidmat state honour for public service and a cash reward of one million rupees, according to the statement.

Who faces action now

The eight suspended officials named by the prime minister’s office include the hospital’s executive director, two joint executive directors, the head of its neonatal department, two senior doctors, a security official and the head of the Capital Development Authority’s emergency services. CNN and Al Jazeera both said the suspended group also includes other doctors and the hospital’s security leadership.

Sharif also ordered departmental and criminal proceedings against those identified by the committee. The government has pledged five million rupees to each affected family, and Health Minister Mustafa Kamal called Pakistan’s health system “rotten” while backing a parliamentary investigation.

What happened

Survivors of secret detention in Bangladesh say release has not ended their ordeal, because many now face fresh warrants, convictions and court cases, while compensation remains uncertain and justice still feels out of reach.

Three cases involving enforced disappearance are currently under trial at the International Crimes Tribunal, and its investigation agency is probing several others. The report also says allegations of state-sponsored enforced disappearances were widespread during the 15-year tenure of deposed prime minister Sheikh Hasina, who, along with former security officials, is accused in most of the cases.

Victims and their families described years that disappeared from their lives, with many spending what little they have on lawyers and long-distance hearings. Some have stopped speaking to the media, convinced that justice will never come. The Commission of Inquiry on Enforced Disappearances documented and detailed the incidents, while one survivor, Mikel Chakma, said he now lives in what he calls his “second Aynaghor” even after two years of release.

Release did not end the punishment

Mikel Chakma, a youth leader of the United People’s Democratic Front, said he was abducted on April 9, 2019, while travelling from Narayanganj to Dhaka. His family believed he was dead. He said he spent more than five years inside the Joint Interrogation Cell, and on August 7, 2024 — two days after Hasina’s ouster — he was found blindfolded in a teak forest beside the Chattogram-Khagrachhari highway.

But, he said, freedom only brought another kind of captivity. Mikel faces 10 arrest warrants and two convictions. Eight cases were filed against him in Rangamati and Chattogram, dating back to 2007, 2011 and 2018, and police also submitted charge sheets in two other cases in 2020 while he was still inside the JIC. One conviction came in absentia in 2023, while he was still in secret detention; another followed in October 2025, during the interim government’s tenure after his release.

He alleged that the cases leading to his convictions were fabricated. He also said he had previously been forcibly disappeared for a week before those cases were filed. “They beat me senseless and electrocuted my earlobes,” he said, describing one episode from 2007. “I am living in isolation, in hiding, away from my family because I can be arrested any time,” he said.

Families carrying the costs

Other survivors described the continuing social and financial damage of disappearance and prison. On November 4, 2022, he was produced before the media in Dhaka alongside four others and sent to jail. He said he later learned in prison that he had also been implicated in a Rangamati case, a district he said he had never visited. Although he eventually secured bail, he said two years behind bars and the legal battles cost his family up to Tk 15 lakh. His father died shortly after his release on bail, and his mother remains in frail health.

Maulana Hossain Ahmad said he was abducted in Naogaon by plainclothesmen in the same month and held incommunicado for 21 days. His captivity ended with formal arrest in two criminal cases filed in Narayanganj and Rangamati. After another 21 months in prison, he secured bail in June 2024, but he still has to make expensive journeys for hearings in the Rangamati case. “Everyone around me thinks I am a militant,” he said.

Compensation is still being fought for

The bill proposes compensation of up to Tk 1 crore for victims, with the money to come from fines imposed on convicts and the state covering any shortfall.

But Tulee said recognition has still not translated into justice.
