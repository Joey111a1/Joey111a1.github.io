What happened

Fernando Grande-Marlaska is insisting that Spain’s intelligence services gave no prior warning of the mass migrant surge into Ceuta, while another account traces the crossings to online rumours and the question of what drove the rush remains unsettled.

Denial of a prior alert

Marlaska said on Tuesday, after an urgent EU interior ministers’ meeting, that his government had received no report or alert from the National Intelligence Center, or from any other Spanish or EU intelligence service, that tens of thousands of migrants would swim across to Ceuta at El Tarajal last Thursday. He said the warnings before the crossings were only the kind expected in summer, when irregular sea entries into Ceuta can rise, and that this episode could also have been encouraged by the Supreme Court ruling at the start of July banning hot returns for people who reach autonomous cities by swimming.

He pointed to rising attempts before the surge, saying there were 500 between June 29 and July 12 and 1,300 between July 13 and 26. After those warnings, he said, “measures were taken,” referring to a reinforcement of the Guardia Civil in Ceuta weeks earlier, but that it was not enough in the face of the number of people who eventually entered en masse. He also said his department had begun studying a maritime barrier to allow hot returns again, a measure he said was being accelerated in Ceuta after last Thursday’s events and would also be installed soon in Melilla, according to an internal department document.

Marlaska said intelligence-service analyses for this summer had not predicted anything like what happened, not even a repeat of May 2021, when 10,000 migrants entered Ceuta illegally. He added that if such a scenario had been foreseen, emergency measures would have been taken to secure the border. At the end of the crisis, he said, 72,000 people had crossed by swimming at El Tarajal last week, 70,000 of them had already returned to Morocco, and at least 75 had died on the Spanish side. He also said entries had been “completely” interrupted since last Saturday after police and military deployment in the city.

The scale of the response

The minister used the press conference to defend the intelligence services, saying he had “trust and recognition” for their work and rejecting the idea that failing to detect the event should automatically be treated as an error requiring responsibility. He said it was “something extraordinary, exceptional” that “no intelligence service” of any country could detect. He also defended the government’s response to the crisis as “extraordinary” and “effective,” saying other European interior ministers had recognized it in the Tuesday teleconference.

He also reiterated his defence of Morocco and said the situation had been rapidly reversed thanks to Rabat’s cooperation, which allowed the return within days of most of those who crossed. He blamed the crisis on the coordinated action of people-trafficking mafias acting under what he called an “interested interpretation” of the Supreme Court ruling. In the same appearance, he said the Schengen free movement area had not been compromised and criticized Italy for keeping controls on people arriving from Spain.

A different account of how the surge began

A separate account traced the surge to social media posts that suggested Spain’s border with Morocco was opening up. It said the chain began with Instagram posts, then spread through Facebook users tracking coastguard patrols and TikTok videos showing where to swim. As those claims were shared, thousands of Moroccans became convinced that Europe was suddenly within reach and began heading toward Ceuta in North Africa.

That account said packed buses rolled into the neighbouring Moroccan city of Fnideq, regional trains filled up and taxis did too. By the time authorities reacted, it said, some 72,000 people had reached Ceuta in just a few days. It also said it was unclear who was behind the claims that the border was open, or who operated many of the social media accounts, several of which were deleted soon after the crossings. The same account said the EU is investigating what happened and that its migration commissioner expressed concern on Tuesday about online disinformation, without naming a specific culprit.

The New York Times said the female lions died within five days of each other despite zookeepers’ efforts to shelter them from Japan’s blistering summer temperatures.

While autopsies will determine the official cause of death, the zoo said the three lions all showed signs of dehydration and multiple organ failure, suggesting heat stress was a factor. Four remaining lions are recovering, but three others are still unwell and undergoing treatment.

Japan’s heat wave has been severe

The deaths came as Japan has endured a punishing stretch of heat. Temperatures reached at least 40 degrees Celsius (104 Fahrenheit) for five consecutive days in some areas in late July, the country’s longest such streak on record, and heatstroke alerts have repeatedly covered large parts of the country.

The country’s weather agency introduced a new designation this summer for days that reach 40 degrees Celsius, roughly translating to “cruelly hot day.” One hard-hit area is Kumamoto Prefecture, which was also rocked by a deadly earthquake last week and experienced its highest temperature on record on Monday.

The same heat pattern is affecting other parts of East Asia. South Korea is in the grip of a historic and deadly heat wave, and the evidence says Yangsan in the southeast reached 42.5 degrees Celsius on Sunday, the highest temperature recorded nationally in 122 years of observations.

South Korea’s weather agency issued its highest-level heat warning for parts of Seoul and neighboring Gyeonggi Province for the first time Monday since the new alert system was introduced in June, while the same level of warning remained in effect across parts of the south. Across the region, the concern is not only the immediate toll of the weather, but how long the extreme temperatures will last and how much more damage they may bring.

Since 2014, the “Taiwan tree seekers” have been working to find, measure, and document the island’s extraordinary giants. The team brings together professional tree climbers, ecologists, geologists, and remote sensing specialists. The Indigenous Rukai people call these immense firs “The tree that hits the moon.”

The search began with known landmarks. In 2014, researchers from the Taiwan Forestry Research Institute entered the Cilan conservation area to study the “Chilan Three Sisters,” a famous group of giant Taiwania firs that local communities had known about for years but had never been carefully measured. The survey found that the tallest was 69.3 meters high.

The team then pushed into more isolated terrain near Mt. Benya, close to Great Ghost Lake, which is considered sacred by Indigenous peoples. Strange quantum experiment shows “negative time” is more than an illusion
science_generalscience anchorscience_journalism
What happened

Photons sent through a cloud of rubidium atoms appeared to spend a negative amount of time inside it, and a second measurement matched that result, sharpening the question of whether the effect is only a timing trick or something directly measurable.

The experiment, published in *Physical Review Letters*, used photons that must pass straight through a rubidium cloud whose atoms have a resonance with the photons. Because the photon energy has to be well defined, the pulse must be long, and the exact entry time is uncertain. When a photon makes it through, its average arrival time on the far side can come out earlier than expected, so early that it seems to have spent a negative amount of time in the cloud.

That effect has been known for decades and was observed in 1993, but physicists often treated it as an artifact: only the front of the long pulse was thought to get through while the rest was scattered. Aephraim Steinberg, one of the authors of the 1993 paper, wanted to see what would happen if the rubidium atoms themselves were queried during the photon’s passage.

To do that without destroying the effect through measurement, the team used a weak laser beam and looked for tiny phase changes as a probe of whether the atoms were excited. Single runs gave only a rough signal, but averaging millions of runs produced an accurate dwell time. For photons that went straight through, that weakly measured dwell time exactly matched the negative time inferred from arrival times.

The result does not point to a time machine, and the experiment is still explained by standard physics. But it does show that negative dwell time is not just an illusion.
