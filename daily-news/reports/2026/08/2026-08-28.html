What happened

Australian officials are urgently trying to confirm the welfare of tourists missing after flash flooding on the Nepal-Tibet border, with at least 35 Australians initially unaccounted for and the count later rising to 39 as damaged roads and communications slowed contact.

Officials race to verify the missing

Assistant minister for foreign affairs Matt Thistlethwaite said on Thursday evening that the Department of Foreign Affairs and Trade was “working around the clock” to verify the status of the missing Australians. He said the figure was based on reports from the Nepal Tourism Board and officials on the ground, and confirmed that Dfat staff were operating from its post in Kathmandu.

The government planned to send a crisis response team to the affected region, while staff tried to make contact with tour operators and work with Nepali authorities to determine whether independent travellers not attached to a tour group could also be missing. Foreign minister Penny Wong later said additional Dfat personnel, including crisis response experts, were being deployed, but she would not confirm names or details until next of kin had been contacted. On Friday morning, Wong confirmed the number of missing Australians had risen to 39.

A flood zone cut off from contact

The search is unfolding after a catastrophic flash flood hit the Nepal-Tibet border region on Wednesday, sending a “tsunami-like wall of water and mud” through villages and towns along the Bhote Koshi and Trishuli rivers. Rescuers were still searching on Thursday, and Nepal police said 389 people had been confirmed dead, with 466 injured and rescued.

The area is a busy trekking and pilgrimage route, and officials said the damage made communication hard. Thistlethwaite said widespread destruction to roads and telecommunications infrastructure had made it difficult to establish contact with much of the region. The minister said Australians should avoid flood-affected areas including Rasuwa, Nuwakot, Dhading, Gorkha and Chitwan, and stay away from rivers, riverbanks, bridges and low-lying areas.

The missing Australians include one person travelling with Kathmandu Holiday Tours and Travel. The foreign affairs minister said the government was liaising with local authorities and tour operators to confirm who was in the area, while the Australian embassy in Nepal urged people in affected areas to follow local safety advice.

What remains unclear

The main uncertainty is still who has been reached and who remains missing. Thistlethwaite said the government was seeking to verify the welfare of Australians through Nepali officials and tour companies, and Wong said the number being reported was based on what tour operators were passing on from the region.

Nepal’s embassy in Canberra also said it was trying to verify how many Australians were missing and that the tourism industry in Nepal was collecting information about anyone who had been in the affected area. It said it was seeking financial assistance and technical support as search efforts continued, and said it would pass verified information to the Australian government and the embassy in Kathmandu once the data had been gathered.

What happened

Japanese troops conducted live human experiments involving mosquito-borne diseases on their own soldiers during World War II, according to new research released Thursday by the Exhibition Hall of Evidences of Crime Committed by Unit 731 of the Japanese Imperial Army in Harbin, China’s Heilongjiang Province. The study says Japanese forces in China carried out insect-based bacteriological weapons research using mosquitoes, fleas and flies through Unit 731 in Harbin and Unit 1855 in Beijing, along with military medical institutions in Japan. It says fleas were used to transmit plague, flies to spread cholera, and mosquitoes as the primary vector for malaria.

Jin Shicheng, a researcher at the Exhibition Hall, said a paper documenting live human experiments with malaria-infected mosquitoes was published on Jan. 10, 1943 by the Japanese Army Medical Corps, covering work conducted from May to August 1940. At a military hospital in Japan’s Chiba Prefecture, Japanese military personnel first had mosquitoes feed on soldiers who had contracted malaria in China, then allowed the infected insects to bite 10 Japanese soldiers hospitalized with mental illnesses. Jin said multiple test subjects developed fevers above 40 degrees Celsius, headaches and loss of appetite, and said the records show cross-institutional collaboration in bacteriological weapons research.

Why guidance depth mattered

In the setup described by the preprint, hint-based reinforcement learning keeps a prefix of an expert trajectory before each rollout so the policy starts closer to success. The problem is that the useful prefix length is sensitive: existing methods either pick one deterministic depth for a whole batch or probe each sample separately, and both approaches miss the fact that different tasks can need different amounts of guidance.

The authors say their analysis on ALFWorld shows that informative depths do not cluster at one exact point. Instead, they form a band whose informativeness profile is approximately Gaussian around the center. That is the core tension behind the paper: if the useful region is spread across a neighborhood of depths, then a single fixed scalar can be too rigid, while per-sample probing buys precision at the cost of extra rollouts.

What Agent-G² does differently

Agent-G² turns that observation into a sampler. For each task, it draws a guidance depth from a Gaussian whose center and spread are estimated online from rollouts already collected for policy optimization, so it does not need probe rollouts or a learned depth predictor. The center combines a global baseline with per-cluster difficulty, and the spread reflects within-cluster variance.

The method uses the same expert trajectories directly as rollout-starting prefixes inside an on-policy GRPO loop. The preprint says the rollout statistics update both the policy and the schedule, and that the model trains without an auxiliary network or any extra offline annotation beyond one expert trajectory per training task. It also reports that the Gaussian choice is not just a fit to the observed band but a practical two-moment parameterization that matches the information the batch rollouts already provide.

The authors evaluate Agent-G² on ALFWorld and WebShop with Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct and Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct. On ALFWorld, they report success rates of 95.3% at 1.5B and 98.4% at 7B. Against the strongest hint-based baseline, they say Agent-G² is ahead by 2.3 points at 7B, and it also beats the strongest hint-free and Aux-RL baselines by 3.9 and 7.4 points, respectively. Those gains come at under one-third the rollout cost of per-sample probing.

On WebShop, the preprint says Agent-G² reaches a 92.3 reward score at both scales, with 78.9% final-purchase success at 1.5B and 84.4% at 7B. It is presented as the strongest non-probing method on that benchmark at both model sizes.

Training behavior and limits.

The training trace shows Agent-G² starting with deeper prefixes when the policy cannot yet reach learnable states unaided, then moving toward shallower guidance as success improves. The preprint says it reaches the final accuracy of scheduled baselines in roughly half the gradient steps. It does have a modest per-step overhead compared with simple schedules, but it remains much cheaper than probing-based methods.

The main limitation the authors state is that Agent-G² relies on one expert trajectory per training task. They also note that the Gaussian parameterization is motivated by the empirical informativeness profile they observed on ALFWorld and WebShop.
