What happened

Bodies were still being pulled from the water at Ceuta’s Tarajal border on Saturday, after a surge of crossings from Morocco left at least 67 dead and forced the city to expand its mortuary capacity while teams searched for more victims beneath the sea.

Recoveries continue off Tarajal

In the water very close to the Tarajal pier, silhouettes of bodies could be seen beneath the clear surface, some gathered in small bundles. Two of them were holding hands. That image captured the submerged tragedy that followed this week’s mass crossing from Morocco and the repeated dawn swims seen in the second half of July.

Two days after the large entry into the autonomous city, most of those who crossed irregularly through the breakwaters had already returned to Morocco, but the dead kept turning up. A police source lamented on Saturday: “There are more deaths in the sea.” A source familiar with the work said it was “risky to estimate the total number of victims,” and noted that Moroccan authorities were also recovering deaths at sea on the other side of the border.

Officers from the Civil Guard Maritime Service and divers from the Special Group of Underwater Activities were working to locate and remove bodies from the seabed. On Saturday morning, they pulled out a dozen deceased and kept marking points where more bodies were believed to be. In front of the border, the placement of aquatic containment barriers, a 500-meter line of buoys and an underwater mesh up to one meter deep continued alongside the search for new victims.

From the beach, numerous journalists, many from international media, filmed the operation and broadcast the latest developments from the city.

A mortuary expanded for an unprecedented number of bodies

Ceuta was preparing a facility with capacity for 200 bodies. The city had nearly 80 bodies in refrigerated storage by mid-afternoon on Saturday, matching the capacity of the modular chambers installed at the former Military Hospital, according to a source from the Cuatro Culturas Funeral Home, which is helping with the work. The modules were expected to be ready by Sunday morning.

The former Military Hospital, a municipal building that still has an autopsy room, has been put at the service of the Forensic Medicine and Forensic Sciences services under the Ministry of Justice, while the autonomous city provides logistical support. Large black fences protected access to the building, and military vehicles, Civil Guard units and funeral home vans were allowed in. Around 17:00, municipal workers blocked entry to anyone not involved in the operation, while forensic experts worked inside with instructions to keep the facility sealed.

The identification process is beginning with fingerprints and files that include photographs, clothing and DNA samples. Many NGOs and the funeral home were already receiving calls from people who had lost contact with relatives, and families of the missing were also calling the local newspaper El Faro de Ceuta to publish notices.

The victims and what comes next

Among the bodies recovered from the sea, the funeral home source said there were many teenagers. “There is a range from 13 to 25 years and another from 25 to 35, more or less,”, pending more precise identifications. Until Friday, all had been male except for one woman, and there were also “quite a few children aged 12 to 14,” the source added. Most appeared to be Moroccan, though there were also people of sub-Saharan origin, including some children from that background.

Ceuta has grown used in recent years to finding young people dead at sea after they tried to swim from Morocco, but the scale of this week’s event exceeded previous cases. The officers believe most of the dead were caught in the human stampede from Morocco, either crushed or drowned, and some bodies show a greater degree of decomposition because the sea crossings have been going on for about two weeks.

In the coming days, Civil Guard officers will mark squares and inspect all areas. If a body can be identified, permission for repatriation is sought from a judge; if no name can be established, the remains are buried with a number.

The Supreme Court ruling at the center of Spain’s Ceuta migrant crisis
global_front_pageglobal_south_and_exploitedpolitics_and_powereconomy_and_businessmaterial_harmmajor supplement
What happened

A Supreme Court ruling on sea arrivals became a flashpoint after about 50,000 migrants crossed into Ceuta, leaving at least 57 dead and prompting Spain to move fast on returns while officials and experts disagreed over whether the court decision had fueled the surge.

Around 50,000 migrants crossed into Spain’s North African exclave of Ceuta this week, overwhelming local authorities. Spanish leaders said they were working to expel everyone who had entered as quickly as possible, and by Friday evening said most migrants had been returned to neighboring Morocco. Many had also gone back voluntarily after failing to find food or shelter in Ceuta.

The ruling that was seized on

The dispute over why so many people crossed centered on a Supreme Court decision issued on June 29. Spain’s Supreme Court said authorities cannot summarily deport migrants who arrive by sea without due process, because they did not cross the physical border infrastructure referenced in Spanish law.

That ruling covered migrants intercepted while swimming into Ceuta and Melilla, the other Spanish exclave on the North African coast, who have often been removed to Morocco immediately and without formal proceedings. Spanish officials including Prime Minister Pedro Sánchez said human trafficking networks encouraged the crossings by misrepresenting the decision. In that account, traffickers and migrants wrongly assumed the court had barred the deportation of anyone who tried to enter by sea.

Gemma Pinyol-Jiménez, head of migration policies and diversity at Instrategies and an associate researcher at GRITIM–Pompeu Fabra University, said the ruling only meant people could not be pushed back at sea because there was no crossing of a formal border. She said the process could still be very brief and that people who entered irregularly could be sent back to Morocco, including under a bilateral readmission agreement signed in 1992.

Daniel Thym, a professor of public, European and international law at the University of Konstanz, called it “a very technical ruling.” He said it distinguishes between migrants who climb over border barriers and those who reach Spanish territory by swimming around them, and that it does not amount to a sweeping human rights judgment. He also said judges suggested a way the government might work around the ruling by installing physical barriers in the water.

What happened in Ceuta

The crossings themselves were the immediate crisis. At least 57 people died during the journeys, and the influx prompted tighter border security measures across Europe. The surge also exposed the limits of Ceuta’s small territory and the pressure on local authorities trying to house and process arrivals.

Pinyol-Jiménez said migration movements almost never have one single cause, and that the ruling may have been only one trigger among several. The evidence cited Spain’s relatively strong economy, a recent regularization program for undocumented migrants already living in the country, favorable summer conditions at sea, and longstanding economic pressures on young people in Morocco and elsewhere in North Africa as other possible drivers.

Thym pointed to the large number of adolescents among those who crossed, saying many were 16 or 17 and therefore counted as unaccompanied minors. He said those who apply for asylum receive a proper procedure that often takes months or years. Official statistics also show unemployment for Moroccans aged 15 to 24 at 37.2%, even as Spain’s nationwide unemployment rate fell to 13% in 2025.

Political fallout and what comes next

The migrant surge quickly became a political flashpoint. Spain’s opposition accused Sánchez’s government of losing control of the country’s borders, while Italian authorities announced temporary border controls for arrivals from Spain and France said it would reinforce checks along its frontier. Ceuta and Melilla are Spanish territory on Morocco’s Mediterranean coast and make up the European Union’s only land borders with Africa, which has long made them sensitive migration routes.

Spain and Morocco’s relationship also hangs over the crisis. Morocco claims sovereignty over both enclaves, and a similar surge in 2021 was widely seen as retaliation during a diplomatic dispute over Western Sahara. In the current crisis, Pinyol-Jiménez said organized movements of this kind do not happen without at least passive collaboration from Moroccan authorities, while Morocco has denied involvement.

Sánchez has already announced plans to install floating barriers in the water around Ceuta. The government says the move would comply with the Supreme Court ruling while restoring the ability to carry out immediate returns.

What happened

At least 13 people died when a small sightseeing plane crashed in Peru during a flight over the Nazca Lines, leaving police with no immediate explanation for the disaster and investigators still working at the scene in the country’s south.

Police Major Jorge Andrade said the dead included 11 passengers and two crew members. The crash happened on Saturday as the plane was carrying passengers, described by authorities as mostly tourists, to view the Nazca Lines, the archeological site known for its monumental geoglyphs carved into the desert soil. Andrade told reporters from the crash site that police had information that all 13 on board had died.

The plane reportedly departed from Pisco in the department of Ica. Local media said the crash site was quickly engulfed in flames, preventing any rescues. There were no immediate further details, and Andrade did not give an indication of what may have caused the plane to go down.

Why the Nazca Lines matter

The Nazca Lines are in the desert near Peru’s southern coastal regions, along the Andean mountain range. They date back to between 500BC and 500AD and have been designated a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

UNESCO describes the site as “among archaeology’s greatest enigmas because of their quantity, nature, size and continuity.” It says the geoglyphs depict living creatures, stylised plants and imaginary beings, as well as geometric figures several kilometres long, and are believed to have had ritual astronomical functions. Because of their huge scale, the Nazca Lines are often viewed from the air, where drawings of animals such as monkeys, hummingbirds and cats can be seen more clearly.

Peru is a major tourist destination, with visitors regularly travelling to historical sites such as Machu Picchu and Cusco. More than 4.1 million tourists visited the country of just under 35 million in 2025, according to Peru’s Ministry of Foreign Trade and Tourism.

What is known about the crash

The small plane was flying over the Nazca Lines when it crashed, according to local reports. Police said the aircraft was carrying tourists, and the South China Morning Post reported that the crash happened just after 1pm local time after takeoff from the airport in Pisco, about 240km south of Lima.

Andrade said at the scene that “given the severity of the accident, there are no survivors” and that four bodies had been recovered so far. He said there was no immediate indication of the cause.

Peru’s civil aviation authority will investigate the incident. The crash involved a plane from the fleet of the sightseeing company Aerodiana, according to reports, but further details were not immediately available.

What happened

The Tawjihi results that usually bring Palestinian students celebration instead arrived in Gaza as a measure of survival, grief and endurance, with top scores marked by deaths, displacement and the struggle to study at all.

For most students, the General Secondary Education Certificate is the end of school and the start of a new chapter. In Gaza, the meaning of that milestone has changed. Simply staying alive long enough to sit the exams and learn the results became an achievement for thousands of students, while some of the highest scorers were mourning relatives, injuries and destroyed homes.

Saba Rabah was one of them. She topped Palestine’s scientific stream with an average of 99.9 percent, fulfilling a childhood dream she and her parents had shared. But the moment was shadowed by the absence of her father, who was killed in an Israeli strike on a neighbouring building in al-Maghazi refugee camp in central Gaza.

A dream reached without her father

Rabah said her father had always pushed her to excel. “In my last conversation with my father, he told me he dreamed I would rank among the top students in the scientific stream and study medicine, just as I had always hoped,” she told Middle East Eye. “He said he wanted to spend his retirement in my future clinic.”

That dream did not disappear, but it was carried into a place of loss. “We cried instead of celebrating,” she said of the result. Rabah also described a daily struggle to keep studying while Gaza’s war disrupted every part of life. “No words can describe the suffering of studying while starving or while fleeing,” she said.

She recalled being forced, during repeated displacement, to choose between carrying her books, her mother’s heart and blood pressure medication, or helping her 25-year-old brother, Saeed, who has quadriplegia. Her family’s rented shelter was damaged by an Israeli strike days before her final exams, leaving her to study in the only room still intact, the one place the family also cooked, slept and spent most of the day.

Studying without books, electricity or space

Rabah said the basics of school had become difficult to find. Textbooks, stationery and even paper were scarce, while unreliable electricity and internet access made studying harder still. “It’s hard for high school students to find even second-hand books because no new textbooks have entered Gaza for the past three years,” she said. “Even A4 paper is expensive. Two hundred sheets cost around $150. Everything is expensive.”

Even so, she said her mother and siblings kept reminding her of her father’s hopes whenever she felt overwhelmed. “Every family in Gaza has experienced grief. We have lost fathers, mothers, siblings and sometimes entire families,” she said. “We didn’t choose to live through this genocide, but we keep trying to live and hold on to the small moments because that is our right.”

Other students faced the exams while recovering from injuries. Maram Muqit, from northern Gaza, sat hers four months after an Israeli bullet lodged in her spinal cord and left her with hemiplegia. Her mother, Naheel, said Maram had been on her way back to the tent from a maths class when she was injured, and that doctors told the family she might never walk again if she remained in Gaza because of the lack of medical supplies.

Before she was wounded, Maram attended two makeshift schools despite the danger of travelling across northern Gaza. When internet access was available, she downloaded lessons from YouTube and studied materials her teachers shared on WhatsApp. Her home in Beit Lahia was destroyed in the first month of the Israeli attacks, and the family now lives in a tent in the al-Atatra neighbourhood in the north.

“It was exhausting for her to balance treatment with studying, but she still achieved an outstanding 96.6 percent,” her mother said. “When the results came out, we cried instead of celebrating. Her father and I tried to help her stand, but she still couldn’t keep her balance.” The hospital later allowed Maram to spend a day with her family at their displacement camp, where relatives and friends held a small celebration. Doctors and patients at al-Wafa Hospital celebrated with her too, and some brought gifts.

Results marked by injury and death

Maram said her future remains uncertain. She hopes to study dentistry, but fears her disability could stop her from pursuing it. “I am happy I achieved the grades I needed,” she said. “But I am sad that I may never walk again.” She said she hopes for an electric wheelchair, travel abroad for treatment and a chance to go to university.

Not every student lived to see the results at all. Yahya Nasman scored 96.6 percent but was killed alongside five members of his family in an Israeli air strike on their home on al-Nasser Street in western Gaza City, a week before the results were announced in July.

His uncle, Waleed, said Yahya had been exhausted after a year of studying while helping the family fetch water, collect firewood and survive the war. Two days before the attack, Waleed invited Yahya and another student from the family to celebrate the end of their exams. Over pizza, Yahya said he wanted to study architectural engineering so he could help rebuild the homes destroyed in Gaza.

The day before the strike, Yahya brought his six-year-old sister, Arwa, to play with Waleed’s daughters. Waleed said he never imagined it would be the last time he would see them alive. The next day, another brother, 12-year-old Ayham, found the family house reduced to rubble and ran to alert relatives.

“We rushed there hoping someone had survived,” Waleed said. “My brother, his wife and their three children, including Yahya, were killed instantly.” Five days later, the family learned Yahya had achieved one of the highest Tawjihi scores in Gaza. “What should have been a celebration became another funeral,” Waleed said.
