What happened

Russia and Ukraine traded overnight strikes that killed at least seven people and wounded many more, while a WHO warehouse in Ukraine storing emergency health supplies was destroyed, underscoring how the fighting is hitting both civilians and medical aid.

The warehouse was in Dnipro and held humanitarian supplies for frontline health facilities. World Health Organization director-general Tedros Adhanom Ghebreyesus said on Sunday that the building was destroyed by a strike on Friday. He said no casualties were reported because a WHO staff member and drivers evacuated 130 of about 300 pallets before the attack, then left the area. “I cannot say loudly enough: attacks on health care MUST STOP. Otherwise, people are deprived of lifesaving healthcare,” he said.

Odesa, Kharkiv and Zhytomyr were hit overnight

In southern Ukraine, Ukrainian emergency services said Russia launched a “massive” strike on Odesa overnight and at least eight people were injured. Volodymyr Zelenskyy said in an X post on Sunday that the “brutal strike” targeted energy infrastructure as well as residential buildings and the port. He condemned what he called “ballistic terror” attacks on Odesa, Kharkiv, Pavlohrad and other regions, and said: “Every such attack on human life serves as a constant reminder – we need more pressure, more sanctions against Russia, and more air defense for Ukraine.”

Kharkiv was also hit hard. According to the head of the region’s state administration, a separate Russian strike on the Saltivka district killed two people and injured at least 21 more. Ukraine’s emergency services said firefighters rescued seven people from the upper floors of a high-rise building. Another report put the number injured in Kharkiv at 13.

Ukrainian emergency services also said another overnight strike on the Zhytomyr region injured three people.

Russia’s defence ministry gave its own account of the overnight strikes, saying its forces hit drone warehouses in the Kharkiv region and fuel depots and stores of other materiel belonging to Ukraine’s armed forces at the port of Odesa. It also said it shot down 153 Ukrainian drones during the night, including over Russian-occupied Crimea and the Black and Azov seas.

Belgorod’s death toll climbed after a drone attack

On the Russian side of the border, the regional crisis centre in Belgorod said five people died in a Ukrainian overnight drone attack on Sunday. Acting governor Alexander Shuvayev said earlier that three people had been killed and 25 others injured in what he called a “massive terrorist drone attack” by Kyiv, before the death toll later rose to five.

The exchange of strikes left damaged homes and buildings in Belgorod region as well. Local authorities said Ukrainian drone strikes damaged 29 blocks of flats and five private houses, along with administrative buildings and unspecified commercial facilities.

The back-and-forth strikes came as Zelenskyy again pressed for more pressure on Moscow and more air defence for Ukraine, while local authorities on both sides reported civilian deaths and injuries from attacks that hit cities, ports and residential areas overnight.

What happened

Benjamin Netanyahu's refusal to accept Donald Trump's 15-point Gaza plan has left the US-backed deal stuck on a central dispute: whether Israel must pull back before Hamas is fully disarmed, or only after disarmament is complete.

At a cabinet meeting on Sunday, Netanyahu said Israel rejects the “15-point document” and that the Israeli military “will not carry out any withdrawal until Hamas is genuinely disarmed” and will continue to thwart threats against Israeli forces and citizens. Hamas, meanwhile, said it remained committed to the roadmap and ready to engage seriously in carrying it out.

Israel’s rejection comes against the backdrop of continued strikes in Gaza since the initial ceasefire agreement last October. The plan itself, as described in the evidence, includes a Board of Peace to oversee implementation, the disarmament of Hamas, the withdrawal of Israeli troops and the reconstruction of Gaza.

Netanyahu draws a line on withdrawal

Netanyahu made the rejection explicit on Sunday, saying: “Israel rejects the 15-point document.” He said the military would not withdraw “at all” until Hamas was “genuinely disarmed,” and he told ministers that Israel was now discussing the issue with US officials.

The comments put the sequencing of the plan at the center of the dispute. Trump’s Board of Peace said last month it had reached an agreement for the “complete disarmament” of Hamas and other armed groups in Gaza. But Hamas has said handing over its heavy weapons was contingent on Israel ending “all forms of aggression” and withdrawing its forces from Gaza.

Hamas says it still backs the roadmap

A Hamas official told the BBC on Sunday that the group “reaffirms it commitment” to the agreement and was ready “to engage seriously and responsibly in implementing” the 15-point plan. The official also called on mediators to ensure “that all parties comply with what has been agreed.”

That position sits alongside Hamas’s earlier insistence that it would not move forward unless Israel met its obligations. In the evidence provided, Hamas said it would only hand over heavy weapons if Israel stopped strikes and pulled its forces back. The gap between those positions and Netanyahu’s refusal to accept any withdrawal before disarmament has left the plan in dispute.

Trump said when the plan was revealed that it was a “monumental step toward lasting peace and security,” and he said Israeli forces would withdraw “as disarmament is completed.” Netanyahu’s public rejection on Sunday showed that Israel was not prepared to accept that order.

What happens next

Netanyahu said he is in discussion with US officials about how to go forward, but the evidence also shows pressure from inside his own coalition. Far-right National Security Minister Itamar Ben Gvir dismissed the draft as “not acceptable,” and other hardline voices have urged him to hold a new cabinet vote and end the Gaza plan.

There is also a wider diplomatic effort still under way. Nickolay Mladenov, described in the evidence as a senior diplomat tasked with implementing Trump’s peace plan, defended the plan on Sunday and said discussions were ongoing in the hope of a “positive outcome.” He said the board had asked Israel “to give the process a chance” and called the plan “the only way forward” to ensure there was no repeat of the 7 October 2023 attack by Hamas.

For now, though, the most immediate outcome is stalemate. Israel has rejected the 15-point document, Hamas says it remains committed to the roadmap, and the question of when — or whether — Israeli forces would withdraw remains unresolved.

Trump says US is ‘low-keying it’ with Iran, stresses economic pain
global_front_pageglobal_south_and_exploitedwar_and_conflictpolitics_and_powereconomy_and_businesspublic_healthmajor supplementanchor
What happened

Iran has hardened its terms for reopening the Strait of Hormuz just as Donald Trump says the US is “low-keying it” with Tehran, leaving the shipping route tied to sanctions, frozen assets and war-damage claims rather than any quick settlement.

The latest round of demands comes after a June memorandum of understanding on Hormuz was agreed and later suspended amid US strikes on Iran, Iranian retaliation across the Middle East and renewed attacks on shipping. Tehran’s new position appears to go beyond that earlier formula.

Iran raises the price of reopening Hormuz

On Saturday, Mohammad Bagher Zolghadr, a highly influential figure in the Revolutionary Guards, laid out a broad set of conditions. He said the US must lift its naval blockade, withdraw its military forces from around Iran, lift sanctions and release frozen Iranian assets abroad.

Zolghadr also demanded compensation for damage from wars this year and in June last year, which he said was principally conducted by Israel. CNN described the list as “maximalist,” and said the implication was that these conditions would have to be met before, or alongside, any concessions on the strait.

That sits uneasily with the limited arrangement that had been in the works with Oman. Hardliners have been insisting that the Oman deal is not the same as reopening the waterway in the way Washington wants, and that “America must correct its behavior” first. Iranian officials have framed the strait as a point of leverage, not a simple transit issue.

Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi said on Sunday that talks with Oman on transit and management of the strait were approaching their final stages, but he said reopening Hormuz was still subject to other conditions and compensation for what he called violations of the June agreement. He also said Iran currently had no negotiations with America, only messages through intermediaries.

Trump signals restraint, not escalation

Trump, by contrast, signaled that he is prepared to let economic pressure build rather than launch another military offensive. In a brief phone call, he said, “We are low-keying it,” and added that oil prices are falling and American consumers are feeling less pain from the conflict.

He also said the US was “just watching Iran” and its “huge inflation” and lack of money. CNN noted that Trump has threatened major strikes before without following through, while also reporting on dwindling US munitions, something Trump has denied.

The result is a standoff in which both sides are talking past each other: Tehran is tying any change at Hormuz to broader concessions from Washington, while Trump is signaling that economic pressure may do the work. Axios also reported that he did not sound frustrated on Sunday about Iran’s delay over a deal to reopen the strait.

What remains unresolved

The Trump administration has not responded to Iran’s latest conditions. US Vice President JD Vance said on Saturday that officials were working through “a traffic scheme” so ships could pass safely, but that still leaves the bigger question unanswered: whether Washington will accept any of Tehran’s terms.

Traffic through the strait remains limited, and the dispute is still being managed through indirect exchanges rather than direct talks. Iran says the Oman negotiations are in their final stages, but it is also clear that reopening Hormuz, if it happens, is still contingent on conditions that the US has not met.

OPS Issues Alert Over Rise in Measles Cases
global_front_pageglobal_south_and_exploitedpublic_healthmaterial_harmpolitics_and_powerregional expansion
What happened

The Pan American Health Organization issued a new epidemiological alert over a rise in measles cases in the Americas. As of July 18, the region had 47,459 confirmed cases in 16 countries and one territory, including 44 deaths recorded in three countries, the OPS said. It added that this is more than three times the total recorded in 2025 so far, when 15,011 cases were reported in 15 countries and 32 deaths in three nations.

The OPS said the region is seeing the highest number of confirmed cases in 22 years and remains at “very high” public health risk because of active outbreaks, gaps in immunization and high mobility. It also said the total confirmed cases are more than double those reported in the epidemiological alert of May 29. Guatemala, Mexico, the United States and Peru account for 95 percent of this year’s cases, and the OPS urged stronger vaccination and epidemiological surveillance.

What happened

China's producer price index rose 3.5 percent year on year in July, while the monthly reading fell 0.7 percent, leaving factory-gate prices moving in different directions as weather, imported factors and shifting demand pushed sectors up or down.

The National Bureau of Statistics said Sunday that the PPI, which measures costs for goods at the factory gate, climbed from a year earlier even as it edged down from June. The same data showed China’s consumer price index, a key gauge of inflation, rose 0.5 percent year on year in July.

NBS statistician Dong Lijuan said imported factors weighed on prices in some industries. She also cited seasonal pressures, including high temperatures, heavy rainfall and typhoons, which slowed construction activity and pulled down prices in certain sectors. At the same time, industrial transformation and consumption upgrading boosted demand and drove up prices in others.

The breakdown pointed to stronger price gains in oil and gas extraction, non-ferrous metal mining and dressing, coal mining and washing, electrical machinery and equipment manufacturing, and computer, communication and other electronic equipment manufacturing. From January to July, the PPI increased by an average of 1.8 percent year on year.

Xinhua also said new drivers of growth continued to gain momentum, with artificial intelligence, high-end equipment and new materials flourishing. It said prices rose in smart unmanned aerial vehicle manufacturing, new carbon materials, and shipbuilding and related equipment manufacturing, and that prices in smart household consumer devices and skincare cosmetics manufacturing also increased.

What happened

A new vision-language-action model called World-to-Wrist VLA tries to improve fine-grained robot manipulation by predicting how the wrist view will evolve under task context, addressing cases where main-view and wrist-view images do not play the same role.

The preprint argues that existing multi-view VLA models often treat main-view and wrist-view observations as parallel inputs, even though wrist-local interactions can matter most in contact-rich tasks. World-to-Wrist VLA, or W^2-VLA, is designed around that gap: given current multi-view observations and an instruction, it builds a compact latent interface between the VLM and a wrist predictor, then uses wrist history plus that interface to forecast future wrist latents and turn them into future-aware context for action prediction. The same work also introduces W^2-CoT, a synthesis pipeline that produces structured annotations for manipulation progress, physical transition cues, and wrist-local evidence, which are used as auxiliary supervision.

What changes in the model

W^2-VLA treats future wrist modeling as task-conditioned latent prediction rather than as a separate visual side task. The model contextualizes a set of latent modeling tokens from the current views and instruction, then passes their hidden states to a wrist branch. That branch uses a frozen V-JEPA 2.1 encoder for wrist history and future targets, a four-layer latent predictor, and a lightweight adapter that converts predicted future wrist latents into a compact wrist context for the action head.

The paper says this design is meant to resolve task ambiguity in wrist-centered prediction: similar wrist histories can lead to multiple plausible futures, so global task context is used to pick the relevant one. To shape that context, the W^2-CoT annotations are rendered in three fields — Subtask, Reasoning, and Wrist — and are used during training alongside future wrist prediction and action generation. At inference time, the system does not need future wrist observations or autoregressive W^2-CoT decoding.

Where it was tested

The authors evaluate W^2-VLA on LIBERO, RoboTwin 2.0, and three real-world manipulation tasks on the CoBoT Magic platform: Table Cleaning, Occluded Placement, and Bimanual Plug Insertion. The implementation uses StarVLA Community with Qwen3-VL-4B-Instruct as the vision-language backbone and a DiT-based flow-matching action head. The full model has about 4.97 billion parameters, uses 16 latent modeling tokens, and produces action chunks of 8 steps on LIBERO and 16 steps on RoboTwin 2.0 and the real-world tasks.

On LIBERO, W^2-VLA reaches a 98.5% average success rate across the four suites, with 99.6% on Spatial, 99.8% on Object, 99.2% on Goal, and 95.2% on Long. The paper says that on RoboTwin 2.0 it gets 60.71% in the Easy setting and 18.21% in the Hard setting. For the real-world tasks, it is compared with π0 and VLA-JEPA under the same data and protocol, with 30 standard trials and 30 OOD trials per task. Under standard conditions, W^2-VLA averages 70.00% success and under OOD conditions 52.22%, with the strongest OOD result on Bimanual Plug Insertion at 33.33%, compared with 10.00% for VLA-JEPA.

The real-world progress scores also favor W^2-VLA. On Bimanual Plug Insertion, for example, the paper reports progress-score gains over VLA-JEPA from 1.86 to 2.60 in standard conditions and from 1.53 to 2.27 in OOD conditions. During deployment, the model generates a 16-step action chunk in 183 ms, which the paper says corresponds to 87.43 Hz.

What the ablations show

The ablation table suggests both major additions matter. Removing the wrist predictor lowers LIBERO average success from 98.5% to 97.5%, with the biggest drop on the Long suite. Removing W^2-CoT supervision lowers the average to 98.0%. The paper also says that a 16-token latent interface gives the best tradeoff between accuracy and speed, while explicit decoding is slower and larger token counts do not consistently help.

The authors frame the result as a World-to-Wrist pathway: global task understanding is used to anticipate wrist-local dynamics, and that future-aware wrist context is then fed into action generation.

Colorado State University researchers used computational studies, and University of Colorado Boulder scientists used spectroscopy, to examine why the approach works differently. Robert Paton said their calculations showed that “the decisive selectivity emerges after electron transfer has already occurred,” with the desired reactant escaping reversal while the molecule easier to reduce is recycled back to its starting material.

What happened

Researchers studying one of only two complete dodo skulls say the bird’s braincase holds new clues about how it sensed the world, pointing to a more complex animal than the clumsy extinct bird of popular image and leaving open how it actually behaved day to day.

The skull in question has sat in the Natural History Museum Denmark’s collections for centuries. It became central to an international investigation led by researchers at the University of Lethbridge in Canada, who used high-resolution CT scanning to build a detailed three-dimensional model without damaging the specimen. The work, published in the *Zoological Journal of the Linnean Society*, also drew on the other complete skull, held by the Oxford University Museum of Natural History.

By digitally reconstructing the space where the dodo’s brain once sat, the team compared its anatomy with that of living pigeons and doves, its closest relatives. The results suggest the dodo may not have been strictly daytime active and may instead have been able to move or forage at dawn and dusk. The bird may also have relied more heavily on smell than modern pigeons and doves, and its large upper beak appears suited to gathering touch information from its surroundings.

Peter Andrew Hosner, Associate Professor and Curator of Birds at the Natural History Museum Denmark, said having access to one of only two complete dodo skulls gave researchers a rare chance to compare the species with its closest living relatives and build a stronger picture of how it experienced Mauritius before it became extinct in the late 17th century. The researchers also say skull anatomy alone cannot give a complete account of the dodo’s behavior, even if it helps move beyond centuries of speculation.

Dinosaurs became giants — so why did they never become tiny?

What happened

Researchers from the American Museum of Natural History and Princeton University say dinosaurs may have been pushed toward giant sizes while never crossing into truly tiny ones, leaving an open question about what kept even the smallest non-bird dinosaurs from shrinking into mouse- or sparrow-sized niches.

In a study published in *Evolution*, the team used mathematical models to examine how body size evolved across vertebrates. The models could explain the size ranges seen in mammals, birds and turtles through energy use and physiology, but they did not explain why the smallest dinosaurs outside the bird lineage still stayed relatively large. The researchers suggest ecology may have set a lower size limit, with early small mammals possibly competing for the smallest body-size niches. Roger Benson said, “Everyone loves a giant dinosaur,” but the team looked “at the other end of the scale,” adding that the absence of tiny dinosaurs may be as interesting as giant ones.

Why tiny dinosaurs may have been missing

The gap was striking. The largest dinosaurs exceeded 80 tons, while the smallest known non-bird dinosaurs weighed about one pound, or roughly 450 grams. That is far heavier than many tiny living animals, including the bee hummingbird and the Etruscan shrew, and far above the dwarf gecko. The researchers also noted that tiny animals are common today: about 75 percent of living mammal species and 90 percent of living bird species are smaller than the smallest known dinosaurs outside the bird lineage.

The team considered whether tiny dinosaur fossils were simply missing, but argued that fossil sites preserving dinosaurs often also preserve much smaller animals such as mammals, lizards and amphibians. They said the evidence instead points to dinosaurs being either extremely uncommon at that size or absent altogether.

60,000-year-old ostrich eggshell engravings reveal a surprisingly sophisticated human mind

What happened

Archaeologists working at several sites in southern Africa have uncovered hundreds of engraved ostrich eggshell fragments more than 60,000 years old, made by groups of Homo sapiens in the region. A study led by researchers at the University of Bologna and published in PLOS One examined 112 fragments from Diepkloof and Klipdrift in South Africa and Apollo 11 in Namibia, and concluded that the markings were far more organized than simple scratches or improvised decorations.

The team used geometric and statistical techniques to reconstruct the directions, angles, and paths of the engraved lines. Their results showed that more than 80% of the configurations contained consistent spatial patterns, with angles close to 90 degrees and repeated parallel lines appearing often. More elaborate designs, including hatched bands, grids, and diamond-shaped motifs, also showed rotation, repetition, translation, and “embedding.” Silvia Ferrara, who coordinated the study, said the signs reveal “a surprisingly structured, geometric way of thinking,” and described the work as evidence of “real visuo-spatial planning.”
