A Tragedy in Iran Might Persuade Hegseth to Change Course

What happened

Pete Hegseth’s Pentagon is weighing whether to restore up to half of the civilian-harm prevention jobs it cut, a possible reversal driven by the Iran war and the Minab school strike, even as the final decision still depends on Hegseth and the death toll in that strike remains disputed.

When President Trump chose Hegseth from Fox News to lead the Defense Department, Pentagon officials quickly adapted to his preference for “lethality” and battlefield swagger over restraint. Hegseth then pushed through a broad purge, including deep cuts to staff assigned to avoiding civilian casualties in war, unwinding protections that had been built up after the wars in Iraq and Afghanistan.

How Hegseth dismantled the civilian-harm program

The staff targeted in Hegseth’s cuts had been part of a military-wide system developed after tens of thousands of ordinary people were killed in Iraq and Afghanistan. Congress had mandated some of the later enhancements to that system, but Hegseth fired or reassigned almost all of the personnel working on the program. Within months of his taking office, the Pentagon had slashed the total number of staff working to prevent and investigate civilian-casualty incidents by about 90 percent.

Hegseth also brought the same approach into the war in Iran. Early in the conflict, he told reporters, “No stupid rules of engagement, no nation-building quagmire, no democracy-building exercise, no politically correct wars. We fight to win, and we don’t waste time or lives.” He had arrived at the Pentagon with a record of attacking what he saw as unnecessary shackles on battlefield operators, including military lawyers and international law, and his on-air defense of troops accused or convicted of war crimes helped build his bond with Trump.

The Minab strike changed the pressure

The possible reversal is tied to the military’s reckoning with the human cost of the five-month, on-again, off-again war with Iran, especially an air strike in the first hours of that conflict on a school in Minab, in southern Iran. One account says the strike killed almost 170 people, most of them children; another says at least 168 people died, also mostly children. In both versions, the school attack has become the defining civilian tragedy of the war so far.

The details of that strike worsened the pressure around it. After the attack, U.S. defense officials reportedly could not believe the U.S. military had hit a school and wondered whether the Israeli military had done it. Trump suggested Iran was responsible. But the strike involved a Tomahawk missile, a weapon Iran does not have, and an initial examination found that although Iran’s military had once occupied the building, it was no longer there. U.S. government intelligence had noted the building’s conversion into a school, but that information did not reach the planners, defense officials said.

Inside Iran, the Minab death toll has become a symbol of U.S. aggression. State media and government officials have invoked the incident repeatedly and commemorated it publicly, including during the FIFA World Cup, when members of Iran’s team wore #168 pins and posed in front of rows of children’s backpacks.

Legal and political pushback inside Washington

The civilian-harm cuts have also drawn formal scrutiny. A Pentagon inspector-general report this spring found that the deep reductions in civilian-harm protections may have violated laws designed to safeguard noncombatants. Democrats have urged the Defense Department to reverse course. The Guardian reported that the Pentagon is considering reversing about half the staff cuts it made to the civilian-harm prevention program.

Some of the pressure is coming from within the military itself. A U.S. official said the leadership of the Pentagon’s combatant commands has uniformly supported restoring the personnel because commanders value the extra safeguards. The case for those safeguards, according to civilian-protection advocates, is that civilian casualties can fuel insurgent recruitment, erode local support, and undermine political objectives. Annie Shiel of the Center for Civilians in Conflict said restoring personnel dedicated to civilian protection, and allowing them to do their jobs, would be “one critical step towards preventing future harm.”

The Pentagon has not announced a final decision. An official said the setup for preventing civilian harm is “currently undergoing a strategic reassessment to inform its future reorganization” and will remain in compliance with congressional requirements, but that “no final decisions have been made.”

What happens next

For now, the proposal awaits Hegseth’s signature. If he approves it, the Pentagon would restore at most part of what it cut and would be acknowledging that, in this one area, his reforms may have gone too far. If he rejects it, the civilian-harm system he hollowed out will remain in a much diminished form even as the Minab strike continues to shape the war’s political and moral fallout.

What happened

Russia’s attacks on Ukraine’s fuel stations have turned ordinary refueling stops into danger zones, injuring civilians and police officers while leaving Ukrainians wondering whether a routine fill-up could be the next drone strike.

A gas station turned into a blast site

Policeman Mykhailo Tsypliakov said he and his colleagues ran into a gas station after it was already on fire. He heard the buzz of a second drone descending and hit the ground. “I saw a flash, then got up right away,” he said. “I saw that my arm, legs and torso were injured.”

Tsypliakov is one of dozens of victims of what Ukraine says is Russia’s latest campaign against civilian infrastructure: fuel stations. Ukrainian officials say more than 200 have been hit in June and July, or about three a day. Tsypliakov said the strikes are meant “to intimidate, to sow fear and panic,” adding: “Panic is the scariest thing right now. Because when a person panics, they lose control of themselves.”

Why gas stations matter

The attacks have concentrated on gas stations close to the front lines, where Russian attack drones can more easily reach them. But airstrikes, longer-distance drones and smaller attack UAVs have also added to the toll, including strikes near Kyiv.

Fuel stations matter more in Ukraine than they might elsewhere. With commercial air travel impossible and the country tied together by a vast road and rail network, they have become places where evacuees, soldiers and haulage drivers can stop, regroup and recover. Yaroslav Starovoitenko, president of the Ukrainian Oil and Gas Association, said the attacks target ordinary civilians. He said gas stations had also served as places of refuge during earlier efforts to cut off electricity and heating.

A CNN analysis located and confirmed at least 30 videos of attacks on gas stations across Ukraine in June and July. Regina Kharchenko, acting mayor of Zaporizhzhia, said the city was hit with 54 strikes targeting gas stations over a 24-day period until July 27. She said those attacks killed four people and injured 29. “This is deliberate terrorism targeting civilian infrastructure and civilians,” Kharchenko said. “Russia is deliberately targeting facilities that have no military significance, but… are essential to the daily functioning of a big city.”

Fear, disruption and the broader campaign

The pressure is not limited to one city or one region. Zaporizhzhia is increasingly within range of Russian drones, and Kramatorsk in Donbas and Kharkiv near the Russian border are also heavily affected. Ukrainians see the campaign as an attempt to create the same kind of disruption they have caused in Russia, after repeated Ukrainian strikes on Russian refineries led to long fuel queues around Moscow and other major cities.

The campaign has also been folded into a broader Russian assault on civilian targets. Russia has launched record numbers of ballistic missiles in recent weeks, and the United Nations said June was the deadliest month for Ukrainian civilians since 2022. Moscow has also posted some of the attacks online: Russia’s elite drone unit, Rubicon, released a video this month showing nine strikes on gas stations in the Kharkiv and Sumy regions.

For people trying to keep working, the danger has not always stopped them. At a gas station outside Kyiv, one delivery driver said no one knows where the next attack will land, but he still has to work. Another said there are no problems in Kyiv yet, “but of course there will be if they keep destroying them.”

What happened

Tens of thousands of people crossed from Morocco into Ceuta in a sudden surge that left at least 57 dead and pushed Spain into a border crisis, with Pedro Sánchez blaming trafficking gangs while estimates of how many entered and returned kept shifting.

A border that suddenly gave way

Spain said about 50,000 people crossed into Ceuta on Thursday and in the early hours of Friday, while the president of the enclave put the figure at about 60,000. Ceuta’s population is around 83,600, so the influx overwhelmed the small Spanish territory on Morocco’s North African coast.

The crossings came after Spain’s Supreme Court ruled that migrants stopped at sea while trying to reach Ceuta or Melilla could not be summarily returned to Morocco. Sánchez said on Friday that the surge was caused by criminal networks “purposefully misinterpreting” that ruling. In another description of the episode, he called it “a violation of Spain’s territorial integrity.”

The route into Ceuta has long been sensitive. Ceuta and Melilla are Spain’s enclaves in North Africa and, as the evidence noted, have been a flashpoint in relations with Morocco. The crossings took place by land and sea, with people swimming around the breakwater, crossing on foot, and then moving further into the city.

Deaths, returns, and the scale of the surge

At least 57 people died during the surge, according to local media, and authorities in Ceuta said many of those who died drowned as crowds streamed over the border by land and sea. One report said rescue services were still searching waters near the border.

By Friday evening, Spanish officials said more than 48,000 people had voluntarily returned to Morocco, while the interior ministry also put the number of crossings lower than the Ceuta authorities did, at around 50,000. Other accounts said around 48,300 had gone back by late Friday, and one official told POLITICO that “most of the people that came yesterday are now back in Morocco.”

Some migrants described the return in bleak terms. One man told Reuters he was going back because “people are dying here,” and added: “I beg you, please, if you haven’t come yet, don’t come.”

The size of the movement was large enough to change the pace of the city almost immediately. One report said border posts reopened land routes and that there was still a steady flow of people heading back, while another described “a huge influx” that took place over roughly 24 hours and overwhelmed the city.

Political fallout in Spain and beyond

Sánchez travelled to Ceuta on Friday morning and said he would use all the resources at his disposal to guarantee the safety of the city’s population. Spain later deployed armed forces to bolster security in Ceuta and in Melilla, where between 300 and 400 crossings were also reported overnight.

The crisis quickly spilled into European politics. Italy’s right-wing prime minister, Giorgia Meloni, described the images from Ceuta as “shocking” and said “uncontrolled immigration” was a security threat to Europe. A later account said Italy moved to suspend its free-travel Schengen arrangements with Spain by sea and air. Finland’s interior minister backed Meloni, while Germany’s chancellor demanded that Morocco “take back illegal migrants immediately.”

The European Commission later said Ceuta and Melilla were subject to special Schengen rules that prevented onward movement within the zone. EU sources said the crisis would give member states a chance to discuss the movements into Ceuta, the returns to Morocco, the support being provided to Spain, and diplomatic engagement with Morocco.

That is roughly the amount Arizona and Nevada receive and enough water to serve more than 25 million people a year. The federal plan spares Colorado, Utah, New Mexico and Wyoming from mandatory cuts for now. It is the government’s response to years of deadlocked negotiations among the seven states that rely on the river, after they failed to agree on a single plan. Some of the laws and guidelines governing the river are set to expire at the end of 2026, adding urgency to the dispute.

The cuts are also tied to the river’s physical decline. The evidence says the Colorado River’s two key reservoirs, Lake Mead and Lake Powell, are at their lowest combined level since 1957, and a record-low snowpack has added to the shortage. The river now supports more than 40 million people in seven US states, several tribal nations and Mexico, and it also supplies hydropower.

Arizona and Nevada have faced mandatory reductions before, but the new proposal goes deeper and could affect agriculture, cities and tribes.
