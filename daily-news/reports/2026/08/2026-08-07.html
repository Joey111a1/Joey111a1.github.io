What happened

Juan Jesús Vivas told the European Parliament that the July 30-31 mass entry into Ceuta was not, in his view, a migration crisis but a threat to the city’s integrity, as he pressed for reinforcements and blamed Ceuta’s security on relations with Morocco, a country he said does not recognize Spanish sovereignty.

Ceuta’s leader put sovereignty at the center of the hearing

Vivas, the president of Ceuta’s autonomous city government, made his remarks this Thursday in an extraordinary session of the European Parliament’s Internal Affairs Committee, held by videoconference. He said the events of July 30 and 31 put “our integrity” at risk: “Ceuta’s, Spain’s, Europe’s.”

He told MEPs that the city has still not returned to normal and needs reinforcements to deal with what he described as the “collapse of migrant reception centers.” He also opened by saying it was “one of the most difficult moments in the recent history of the city,” and he used the word “invasion” to describe what happened.

Vivas tied that description to the scale of the arrivals he said occurred over those two days. He said 80,000 people entered Ceuta between Thursday and Friday, a figure he compared to the city’s entire population. At another point, he likened it to “50 million people” entering Spain.

Parliament heard competing messages about responsibility

The hearing was called by the committee’s chair, Spanish conservative Javier Zarzalejos. Conservative MEPs complained that Interior Minister Fernando Grande-Marlaska and Minister of Security, Social Affairs and Migration Elma Saiz did not appear, although their attendance was not mandatory.

Magnus Brunner, the EU Interior Commissioner, did attend. He said that “the situation in Ceuta is, first and foremost, a matter for Spanish authorities, who are in charge of managing the reception system.” Brunner also said the Commission was in close contact with Spanish authorities to assess their needs quickly and provide support when needed.

He added that Spanish authorities, working with Moroccan authorities, were expected to fulfill commitments so that measures taken in response to the situation would not have repercussions in the Schengen area. That frame left the immediate management of the crisis with Spain, while also pointing to coordination with Morocco and concern about spillover beyond Ceuta.

Calls for more security and a figure still in flux

Inside the committee room, Vivas said the city still needed help to cope with the aftermath. He called for the “immediate expulsion” of irregular migrants who crossed the border and remained in the autonomous city, saying the number still there was between 3,000 and 6,000 according to the figures he presented in Parliament.

He also said the fact that the exact number of people still in the city was not known showed “the precariousness of the means with which we are still acting.” That uncertainty over how many migrants remained in Ceuta ran alongside his broader demand for stronger resources and a faster response.

Asked about rumors of another possible mass entry on August 15, Vivas urged stronger security. “Given the experience on July 30 and 31, we have full confidence in our security forces and in the armed forces to prevent another massive entry of that character,” he said. His warning left open the question of whether Ceuta could face another surge.

A royal audience followed the Brussels appearance

Also on Thursday, Vivas met King Felipe VI at Marivent Palace in Palma. After the audience, Vivas said the King had committed to visiting Ceuta, although he gave no date for that trip.

Vivas said he left the meeting “comforted” by the King’s words, and reported that Felipe VI sent “a message of affection, support, and encouragement” to the people of Ceuta. He also said the King would visit the city, while stressing that the timing was still unknown.

The audience was one of the last items on the King’s schedule before his trip to Colombia for the inauguration of the new head of state in Cali on Friday. For Vivas, the encounter came amid a week in which he said Ceuta remained far from normal and still lacked the resources to deal with the aftermath of the surge.

'Morocco has given me nothing': The young migrants risking everything to reach Ceuta

What happened

Ceuta is facing a mass migrant crisis after tens of thousands crossed from Morocco, at least 141 people were reported dead, and the central question remains why the surge happened and who is responsible for it.

The crossing began on 31 July, King Mohammed VI’s Throne Day, and it has left the Spanish enclave trying to process a wave that, according to the latest figures, brought 72,000 people into the territory irregularly from Morocco last week. The Spanish government says 70,000 have since been returned.

A crossing with no clear explanation

Speculation about the trigger has spread quickly, but no clear answer has emerged. Some commentators have said it was a kneejerk reaction from Morocco after Spanish Prime Minister Pedro Sanchez’s recent visit to Algeria. Others have claimed Israel or the US was involved because of Spain’s stance against the genocide in Gaza and the war on Iran.

Morocco’s Association for Human Rights said the “harrowing scenes” on the border reflected despair among young people, driven by lack of prospects, endemic corruption, the rise of authoritarianism, the absence of social justice, and poor employment and living conditions. The group said full responsibility lay with the Moroccan state and compared the situation to what would be expected in war and conflict zones.

Morocco’s Ministry of the Interior said on 1 August that the surge was caused by a misinterpretation of data spread on social media and by human trafficking networks that exploited the Spanish Supreme Court’s 8 July decision banning the immediate return of migrants arriving by sea.

Young people, children and families at the border

What is clear is that many of those who crossed were young Moroccans who said they felt they had no choice. Around 8,000 children were among them, and at least 141 people died in the crossing, with the figure expected to rise.

Hamza, 19, from Kenitra, said he walked about 41 kilometres from Tetouan to Ceuta. He said Moroccan border security “stood back and watched us cross” and told people to hurry into the water. He said police used sticks if migrants hesitated. Hamza said he would not go back: “Morocco has not given me anything.” Even with diplomas, he said, the country does not value them and wages are too low.

Asmae, 23, from Casablanca, said she hitchhiked to Ceuta after seeing Instagram posts about the crossing. She said the Moroccan police were passive and did not beat migrants unless there were fights or problems. “People leave of their own free will,” she said.

Another young Moroccan, Younes, said he crossed because there was nothing for him in Morocco despite having a bachelor’s degree and speaking multiple languages. He said he thought Ceuta would give him an opportunity in life. While swimming the final stretch, he saw three teenagers die in the water. When he reached Ceuta, he said he felt “unmatched happiness,” but that changed after he was beaten by the authorities and saw closed stores, no water, and people collapsing.

Competing claims and the search for missing people

Carla Hobbs, head of the Madrid office and policy fellow at the European Council on Foreign Relations, disputed the Moroccan ministry’s explanation. She said it would have been very hard for the crossing to happen without Moroccan complicity. She pointed to reduced border controls, migrants’ own accounts that authorities encouraged them to cross, and what she described as a broader pattern of Moroccan migration coercion.

Hobbs said the immediate trigger appeared to be a viral online campaign after the Spanish court ruling, but she also pointed to the timing of Sanchez’s trip to Algeria 10 days earlier. She said the latest episode would not be forgotten by the Spanish government, especially with elections approaching, while noting that Morocco may see migration pressure as a lever that produces little unified European response.

For families, the crisis has become a desperate search for missing children. Najaat, a mother of six, said three of her children tried to reach Ceuta. Two daughters, aged 12 and 13, took their birth certificates and wrote down her phone number before leaving. She said she has not heard from them and wants to know whether her son, aged 14, is dead or alive.

No Name Kitchen, an NGO on the ground, said it has seen irregular return processes and has filmed police beatings. Its coordinator, Ric Fernandez, said the team has had to scale up from four people in Ceuta to 18, while also trying to reach vulnerable people hiding in the mountains and warehouses and documenting cases of people who may be entitled to international protection.

What happened

Unpaid government wages are pushing health workers in eastern Democratic Republic of Congo toward a boycott even as they work inside a rapidly expanding Ebola outbreak, raising the risk that the response could lose the staff holding it together.

Every morning, Micheline Kayimpa puts on layers of protective gear before entering an Ebola isolation ward, knowing that one small mistake could cost her life. At the Elikya Ebola Treatment Center in Bunia, she spends her day caring for patients with Ebola, administering medicine and helping those too weak to feed themselves. Because Ebola spreads through bodily fluids, even routine care carries constant danger, from treating patients to cleaning contaminated spaces. “I am very afraid for my safety,” she said. “Sometimes, a small detail we overlook can prove fatal.”

But Kayimpa says the virus is not the only thing wearing workers down. After months of treating Ebola patients, she says she still has not received a single government paycheck. She is among dozens of health workers who have protested delayed wages and threatened to stop work. MSF provides bonuses, she said, but those payments do not replace the government wages she relies on. Kayimpa said she stopped work for three days in protest and has resumed only in the hope that her case will be resolved. If nothing changes, she says she is ready to walk out again.

A response already under strain

The wage dispute is unfolding while the outbreak is growing fast. The World Health Organization says this is now “the largest Ebola outbreak ever reported” in the DRC. Since the virus emerged in mid-May, the Congolese health ministry said Wednesday that it has infected at least 3,874 people and killed 1,751. The outbreak was jointly detected in the DRC and neighboring Uganda, but Uganda has since declared itself Ebola-free while transmission has continued to accelerate in eastern Congo.

Bunia, where Kayimpa works, is the capital of northeastern Ituri province and the epicenter of the outbreak, where more than 90% of confirmed cases have been recorded. The flare-up has reached this scale in just over two months, surpassing the country’s last major Ebola epidemic, which lasted nearly two years between August 2018 and June 2020. Only the 2014–16 Ebola epidemic in West Africa was larger and deadlier.

Conflict, mistrust and attacks on treatment centers

The response is also operating in a difficult setting. This outbreak is caused by the less common Bundibugyo strain, for which there are no proven vaccines and treatment options remain limited. Ongoing fighting in eastern DRC has made it harder for health workers to reach affected communities, and aid agencies say mistrust, misinformation about Ebola and resistance to restrictions on traditional burial practices have fueled hostility toward health workers.

That hostility has already turned violent. In late May, demonstrators seeking the bodies of two relatives forced their way into an Ebola treatment facility, the third such attack since the outbreak began. Health workers had to move patients to safety as gunfire erupted nearby. Similar attacks were reported in June and July, and the United Nations said last month that “at least a dozen facilities have been attacked, largely due to fear, misinformation and deep community mistrust of outside authorities.”

Inside Elikya, the pressure is immediate. The 90-bed isolation ward, run by Médecins Sans Frontières, is already full, and many patients arrive only when they are critically ill. Kayimpa said there are too few nurses for the number and severity of cases. “We’re sacrificing our lives for the people,” she said. She also said some of the earliest health workers infected in the outbreak did not know it was Ebola and treated patients without protection, and that some died.

Officials say payments are being processed

DRC communications minister Patrick Muyaya Katembwe acknowledged that delayed payments had triggered staff protests at several Ebola treatment centers, but said the outstanding wages were now being processed. He said the delays were largely due to administrative verification of which workers had been deployed to the Ebola response and making sure payments reached the correct recipients. “Anyone officially listed will be paid,” he said.

MSF said the protests were about government compensation, not payments from the humanitarian group. It said it was continuing to meet its obligations to staff in the response and that health workers should be compensated in time for work done under “extremely challenging conditions.”

An aid worker familiar with the response said the outbreak cannot be fought without the people risking their lives in treatment centers and in communities. “Every interruption gives the virus more time to spread,” he said. For Kayimpa, the decision is simpler: “As long as I don’t receive my wages, I will boycott work until I’m paid,” she said.

The Lovell Telescope at Jodrell Bank faces closure if alternative funding is not found, after UK Research and Innovation said it will end all annual support for e-Merlin in 2028, triggering calls from scientists and public figures to restore the money.

UKRI, the main UK funding agency, announced on 27 July that it would cut £2.8 million a year from e-Merlin, the seven-telescope radio network funded through the Science and Technology Facilities Council. Lovell depends on e-Merlin for its only scientific funding, so the telescope will stop operating unless another source is found. An open letter calling to save it says closing Lovell would be like “pok[ing] out some of the world’s clearest eyes on the sky” and calls the move “foolish and short-sighted.”

The letter has been signed by figures including astronaut Tim Peake, physicist and musician Brian Cox, and poet laureate Simon Armitage. Lovell is used not only to study the cosmos but also to track human-made objects, including modern space debris and the rocket that launched Sputnik-1 in 1957.

Part of wider UK science cuts

The funding threat comes amid broader cuts in the STFC’s portfolio. The wider Jodrell Bank site, owned by the University of Manchester, will remain open for scientific outreach and as headquarters of the Square Kilometre Array, but the Lovell telescope itself is at risk. The STFC is investing £327 million in the SKA between 2021 and 2030.

The cuts are part of a prioritization exercise under the previous prime minister, Keir Starmer, and the Department of Science, Innovation and Technology. UKRI says its overall budget is rising, with new money mainly going to artificial intelligence and quantum technologies, while STFC funding has stayed flat and costs have risen.

What happened

Scientists at Peking University have turned a levitating magnet into a magnetometer that can measure magnetic fields closely enough to detect the faint signals of the brain’s electrical activity. The technique is reported in Science and is described as simpler than existing approaches. A laser beam tracks the motions of the tiny magnet in a vacuum chamber as it wobbles in response to magnetic fields, and the device is said to be sensitive enough for ultrafaint signals while bringing the sample to within a few hundred micrometres of the sensor.

Some of the most sensitive magnetometers use superconducting quantum interference devices, or SQUIDs, which work only near absolute zero and require complex cryogenics. Another state-of-the-art device, the spin-exchange relaxation-free, or SERF, magnetometer needs near-perfect shielding from external magnetic interference. Wei Ji and collaborators suspended the sensor magnet between a lifting magnet above it and a diamagnet underneath it, which pushes the sensor magnet up and stabilizes it. The whole setup sits in a vacuum chamber about the size of a Tupperware box, and Ji says its accuracy relied on noise-reduction techniques.

What happened

Researchers from the Universities of Edinburgh and Bristol reported that altered gene activity may contribute to neck and back pain by damaging spinal discs, and that a drug already used for osteoporosis blocked spinal mineral buildup in zebrafish. In fish bred without a functioning copy of a collagen IX-related gene, the spine developed abnormalities that resembled disc disease in people: vertebrae fused, mineral deposits formed, and the tissue between the bones became abnormally hard.

The team said the mineralization began only after a supportive scaffold layer in the developing spine started to deteriorate. Their gene-activity analysis pointed to problems with fat processing, the mTOR pathway, phosphate control and vitamin A signaling. They also said a bisphosphonate, which is used to treat osteoporosis, prevented minerals from accumulating, while reduced food intake or drugs that suppressed fat metabolism also reduced spinal fusion. The researchers said the findings highlight biological processes that could become targets for future back pain treatments and suggest zebrafish may be a useful model for testing potential therapies.

Forget the sperm race: Fertilization may depend on teamwork

What happened

A study of arthropod reproduction suggests fertilization may depend as much on sperm teamwork as on sperm competition, challenging the familiar race-to-the-egg picture and raising a new question about how cooperative behavior shapes reproductive success.

Cooperation inside the reproductive tract

Evolutionary biologists at Syracuse University, the University of Siena in Italy and the University of Szeged in Hungary examined arthropods — the group that includes insects, spiders, crabs and centipedes — and traced cases in which sperm joined into organized groups or structures that may help them reach and fertilize an egg. The behavior, known as sperm conjugation, was first documented more than 100 years ago and was generally considered uncommon. The new study, published in *Nature Communications*, says it occurs widely among arthropods and has evolved many separate times.

Steve Dorus, a professor of biology at Syracuse University and a co-author, said fertilization is often viewed as competition among individual sperm, but in many species cells work together in ways that can influence reproductive success. In many species, that cooperation involves sperm-associated material, or SAM: a membrane-enclosed substance that can attach sperm to one another or form structures around them.

The researchers suggest SAM may have helped sperm conjugation emerge, possibly starting as a way to package or protect sperm. They also found a repeated pattern of gain and loss across evolutionary history, including the conclusion that the common ancestor of all insects possessed conjugated sperm. The team compared sperm structures across hundreds of species and placed those traits on an evolutionary family tree covering major animal lineages over the past 600 million years.

The study leaves one central question open: why cooperation develops at all. The researchers say future work will need to observe sperm groups inside actual reproductive systems and sort out the benefits and costs of working together.

Cell-inspired nanoreactor turns sunlight into hydrogen peroxide

What happened

Researchers led by Prof. LI Can at the Dalian Institute of Chemical Physics of the Chinese Academy of Sciences, with Prof. Jian Liu’s team at Inner Mongolia University, created a hollow CdS@polydopamine nanoreactor that imitates two important features of living cells and turns sunlight into hydrogen peroxide. The work, published in the *Journal of the American Chemical Society*, is presented as a new way to reproduce some of the highly organized chemical functions of cells inside synthetic nanomaterials.

The nanoreactor has two biomimetic elements: a dynamic catechol/o-benzoquinone redox pair in the polydopamine shell that acts as a proton relay, and a compartmentalized structure with a hollow cavity and porous shell that helps reactants accumulate, supports molecular diffusion, and traps incoming photons. Under visible-light illumination in water, it produced H2O2 at 3.24 mmol gcat.-1 h-1 and reached a solar-to-chemical conversion efficiency of 1.2%. The researchers used in situ spectroscopy, photochemical analysis, finite element simulations, and theoretical calculations to study the mechanism, and also embedded the nanoreactors in sodium alginate hydrogel to make recyclable photocatalysts that could synthesize H2O2 under natural sunlight.
