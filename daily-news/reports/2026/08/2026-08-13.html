What happened

A Ukrainian drone and missile barrage on Russia’s Black Sea port of Novorossiysk damaged two major grain export terminals, hit a key naval hub and killed at least three people, raising fresh questions about how Russia will keep grain moving through a port now under repeated attack.

The overnight strike also injured another 24 people, local officials said, and sent explosions across the city as air defences shot down aerial objects. An emergency situation was declared in Novorossiysk on Wednesday after the barrage damaged pipelines and suspended the city’s water supply.

Damage at Novorossiysk

Novorossiysk matters because it is one of Russia’s largest ports and now hosts most of Russia’s Black Sea fleet after the ships were moved from Sevastopol, in Russia-occupied Crimea, which Moscow had judged too exposed to Ukrainian attacks. Reports said the strike damaged two major grain export terminals and hit the last big Russian naval base on the Black Sea.

A major grain trading company confirmed to Russia’s TASS news agency that its terminal had been hit, while another told Reuters and Russian outlet RBK the same. Repeated explosions reportedly rang out over Novorossiysk throughout the night as air defences shot down aerial objects, and regional governor Veniamin Kondratyev said a man was also killed by drone debris elsewhere in Krasnodar region.

Kondratyev added that several locations in the surrounding region were struck by “several hundred drones” overnight, with dozens of buildings damaged, including homes. He said the attack damaged areas around the port and pipelines, and forced the suspension of water supply in the city.

Competing claims over the strike

Volodymyr Zelensky said Kyiv had launched rockets, jet and naval drones in a “unique operation” to strike the Russian base, though he did not mention grain terminals being hit. In a post on social media, he said: “The occupying fleet and all the infrastructure that supports it will not be safe as long as Russian aggression continues.”

Ukraine’s military later said four Russian warships, including two frigates, were hit. Russia, meanwhile, has not commented on the issue.

The claims underline how the strike touched both military and commercial infrastructure. The port is not only a naval foothold but also a major grain-export hub, and the reported damage to terminals adds pressure on a trade route that Moscow relies on for shipments from one of the world’s biggest wheat exporters.

Grain exports and the wider Black Sea fight

On Wednesday, the Russian agriculture ministry said it was working to redirect cargo flows to ports in the Baltic and Caspian Seas as well as land routes. It did not mention Ukraine’s attacks, but said it was “taking into account current logistical constraints.”

The timing is significant because the Black Sea has again become a contested route for grain and shipping. After a period of relative calm, Kyiv and Moscow have resumed striking one another’s ports and shipping over the course of this summer. The last remaining sea route out of Ukraine has also been badly affected by Russian strikes on cargo ships and the port of Odesa.

The wider economic stakes are substantial. In July, the Ukrainian Agribusiness Club said exports had fallen 23%. A Moscow-based analytics company said Russian exports for August could fall by more than half compared with the same month over the last five years because of disruption in the Black and Azov seas. Russian foreign ministry spokeswoman Maria Zakharova accused Ukraine of striking Russian grain and agricultural infrastructure and said this was driving up global food prices and grain shortages.

Russia and Ukraine together account for a significant share of global grain trade, especially for buyers in North Africa, the Middle East and parts of Asia. That is why damage to terminals, ports and shipping routes around Novorossiysk is more than a local security issue: it also feeds directly into the struggle over whether grain can keep moving across the Black Sea.

Osorio said he went toward the rubble and shouted for survivors despite warnings about a possible gas leak. Then he asked everyone around him to be silent. A faint sound came from the debris — “I’m with a baby,” a man’s voice replied. Osorio said he and several others began moving debris by hand, but a large concrete slab blocked the way. “On the first attempt, we couldn’t lift it,” he said. They lifted it together on a second try, making a path to the baby.

Osorio said the infant’s head looked purplish because he could not breathe, but his color began to return once the rubble was cleared. The baby was then handed to a police officer and taken to an ambulance. A man, still half-trapped under a concrete plank, remained in the wreckage as rescuers shifted to freeing him. Search teams race through the hardest-hit cities

The wider rescue effort entered its third day on Wednesday as emergency crews kept digging through the remains of buildings in Cali, Pereira, Manizales and Quibdo. In Pereira, rescuers pulled Daniela Largo alive from the rubble late on Tuesday after what was described as a complex 12-hour operation, with crews carving a small opening to free her pinned arm and a doctor giving her IV fluids before she was carried to an ambulance. In the same city, another man was pulled out lifeless, covered with a white blanket.

Authorities said the earthquake has caused enormous human loss and continuing uncertainty. President Abelardo de la Espriella said at least 265 people had died. The search is taking place under pressure from time and damage. Aid agencies consider the first 48 to 72 hours crucial for finding survivors, and Cali mayor Alejandro Eder said rescue teams were still finding signs of life under the rubble. “The next 20 hours are the most critical to find and rescue people alive,” he said. “Every minute is vital.”

Aid arrives, but some places are still out of reach

Tonnes of aid from regional partners began rolling into Colombia on Wednesday as the destruction widened beyond collapsed buildings to buckled roads and crushed cars. Authorities converted sports arenas, schools and other public buildings into shelters for thousands of displaced people. In Pereira and Cali, families were sleeping in parks, some because they feared aftershocks or the collapse of damaged homes, others because they had nowhere to return.

The earthquake also exposed how uneven the response is in the country’s remote areas. In Chocó, which was described as the epicenter, an indigenous leader told CNN that help had not yet reached the area. Victor Chamapuro, leader of the Wounaan Indigenous Reserve “Buenavista” in Chocó’s Litoral del San Juan, said, “All our houses are on the ground.” He said people were sleeping outside and asked authorities for food and medicine. The reserve can only be reached by boat in a five-hour journey from the port of Buenaventura.

Colombia’s geological service has documented 130 aftershocks since the quake, adding to the anxiety in damaged areas. While aid from abroad continued to arrive and rescue workers kept searching, the main question remained whether more people could still be found alive in the rubble — and how long the most isolated communities would have to wait for help.

Millions of adults in the US should be taking blood pressure-lowering medication

What happened

Millions of U.S. adults with untreated high blood pressure could qualify for medication under updated heart-disease risk guidelines, and a new analysis says the change could prevent thousands of deaths, though how many people are affected depends on which risk tool doctors use.

Published July 29 in the *Journal of the American Heart Association*, the study analyzed National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey data from 2009 to 2018 and asked how death rates might have changed if the 2025 hypertension guidelines had been applied during those years, lead author Dr. Mustafa Al-Jarshawi said.

At the center of the finding is a broader definition of who should be treated. Nearly 1 in 3 adults in the United States with untreated high blood pressure would benefit from treatment and are not getting it, the study found. Nearly 10 million Americans now meet newly updated guidelines by the American Heart Association and American College of Cardiology that redefine an adult’s cardiovascular risk.

How eligibility is shifting

Doctors have traditionally wanted blood pressure below 120 mmHg systolic and 80 mmHg diastolic. When readings are around 130/80, they have typically advised lifestyle changes such as increasing exercise. But the new American Heart Association PREVENT tool goes further, considering blood sugar, kidney function and a person’s decade-long risk for heart disease as well as blood pressure.

Using that tool, some 81 million Americans would be eligible for blood pressure medications. Dr. Andrew Freeman, director of cardiovascular prevention and wellness at National Jewish Health in Denver, said that means “people who have traditionally not been at very high risk are now eligible to be treated with medications more aggressively.” He added that when people have additional risk, “blood pressure is really the silent killer.”

The study’s numbers narrow the issue further. Among 81 million adults with high blood pressure and no cardiovascular disease, nearly 23 million may be newly eligible under the new guidelines. Of those 23 million, 9.7 million were untreated.

What the study estimated

Al-Jarshawi said the study estimated that about 200,900 all-cause deaths and 162,600 cardiovascular deaths could be prevented over the next 10 years. It also found that becoming eligible to receive blood pressure medication was associated with a 23% lower risk of dying compared with no treatment, and a 50% lower risk of dying from a heart-related issue.

Adults with diabetes appeared to benefit the most from treatment. The study said that was because diabetes and high blood pressure often occur together, which significantly increases cardiovascular risk.

The researchers also framed the work as a look at what might happen if the 2025 hypertension guidelines had been used during the years covered by the survey data. Newer data is available, but the most up-to-date version of the survey has not been available long enough for researchers to know participants’ cause and time of death.

Al-Jarshawi said eligible people should not hesitate to start medication along with lifestyle changes to reduce long-term risks. Freeman said the findings highlight the need for physicians to watch for early signs of disease and consider blood pressure treatment even in younger people, because “we’re seeing a lot more young people now who have chronic disease and diabetes and other things that may be relatively mild, but still at risk.”

Four ways the water crisis plan could change how you receive water
global_south_and_exploitedpolitics_and_powereconomy_and_businessregional expansion
What happened

South Africans facing dry taps, long walks for water and week-long outages may see delivery change under the government’s new National Water Action Plan, which aims to cut losses and tighten control of a failing system, though activists say it still lacks the detail to deliver real change.

President Cyril Ramaphosa released the plan on 23 July 2026, and it will be managed by the National Water Crisis Committee, or Watercom. Its members include the Department of Water and Sanitation, the National Treasury, the departments responsible for cooperative governance, human settlements, public works and the environment, the South African Police Service and the South African Local Government Association. The plan rests on five actions: responding to interruptions in priority areas, reforming municipal institutions and financing, unlocking infrastructure investment, strengthening legal and regulatory mechanisms, and tackling corruption and criminal activity in the water sector.

Four ways the plan could change water delivery

One major change is a push to separate oversight from operations. South Africa is described as a global outlier because 144 municipalities act both as water services authorities and water services providers. The plan’s related Water Services Amendment Bill would require providers to be licensed on technical and financial competence, and if a municipality is “not willing or able” to meet those standards, national government could appoint expert public or private entities to take over operations. But the bill is still before Parliament’s Portfolio Committee on Water and Sanitation and in public consultation, so implementation is still months away.

A second shift is how the plan treats communities without reliable supply. It identifies 2,688 communities that lack access to reliable water, and splits the response between rural and densely populated areas. In rural or sparsely populated places, the plan prioritises boreholes, protected springs and rainwater harvesting. In denser areas, it aims to use national grants to extend existing networks to people on the periphery.

Third, the plan targets the tanker economy. It says so-called water tanker mafias have taken advantage of collapsing piped supply and municipal procurement, and it frames the problem as a national security issue. The stated goal is to regain control over tanker provision and move back toward a “fixed pipe” model.

The fourth and biggest promise is to reduce non-revenue water — water lost through leaks, theft or faulty meters — from 47% to 39.5% by 2031. The plan treats leak repair as a “catalytic action” that could recover nearly 10% of national supply without building a new dam.

Water activist Dr Ferrial Adam called the plan “uninspiring”, saying it lacks timelines, budgets and enforcement mechanisms, and warning that it leans too heavily on private contractors.

What happened

China’s grid is leaning harder on long-distance transmission, storage and flexible demand to keep up with record summer electricity use, with Chongqing showing both the pressure of peak heat and the payoff from new supply lines.

In the southwest megacity of more than 30 million, sweltering summers put air conditioners, automotive factories and electronics assembly lines under strain. A key change came in June last year, when the 2,260-kilometer Hami-Chongqing transmission line using ultra-high-voltage technology went into operation. It carries electricity generated in Hami, in northwest China’s Xinjiang Uygur Autonomous Region, to Chongqing, and more than 70 percent of that power comes from clean energy sources. Shuai Hong, deputy chief engineer of the power dispatch and control center of State Grid Chongqing Electric Power Company, said it is the city’s “strongest source of confidence” for coping with summer peak demand. He said electricity purchased from outside the municipality now accounts for about one-third of Chongqing’s total grid load this year, and the line can transmit up to 6 million kilowatts at peak, or roughly one-fifth of the city’s maximum power load.

The Chongqing line is one of more than 40 UHV lines in China, built to move power from energy-rich western regions to places where demand is heaviest. Other parts of the grid are using different tools to do the same job. In Shandong, 172 energy storage power stations discharged simultaneously on Aug. 3 as midday and evening demand hit record highs, while Beijing was supplied with green electricity through a “solar-wind relay.” Wuhan has also been piloting a vehicle-to-grid pricing mechanism since July 1, letting eligible electric vehicles feed power back into the grid during peak periods.

Turkey's cross party Kurdish peace coalition makes history with PKK clemency law

What happened

Turkey’s parliament has approved a first-ever clemency framework for former members of the Kurdistan Workers’ Party, drawing ruling-party lawmakers, pro-Kurdish MPs and opposition figures into the same vote while leaving open whether the long conflict can actually be brought to an end.

The PKK has fought the Turkish state since the early 1980s, and the violence has killed thousands of civilians and members of the security forces. The group first sought independence and later greater autonomy for Turkey’s Kurds. This vote marked the first time in Turkey’s modern history that the government brought forward legislation setting out a framework for resolving that conflict.

The vote that brought rivals together

The path to the law followed two years of negotiations with imprisoned PKK leader Abdullah Ocalan, after President Recep Tayyip Erdogan’s government and its coalition partner, the Nationalist Movement Party, led by Devlet Bahceli, decided to pursue an end to the fighting. Bahceli set the process in motion in an October 2024 speech, when he emerged as an unexpected advocate of peace. In that address, he said he would tolerate Ocalan appearing before parliament, even though he had previously called for Ocalan’s execution and his party, the MHP, had long been among the fiercest opponents of what the PKK stood for.

After months of work by a parliamentary committee on the Kurdish question, and hours of negotiations with Ocalan, PKK commanders in northern Iraq’s Qandil Mountains and leaders in exile in Europe, the legislation was introduced in parliament last week. It was quickly ratified on Monday. The scale of support was striking: alongside the ruling Justice and Development Party and the MHP, the law was backed by the secular Republican People’s Party, smaller right-wing and Islamist parties including New Welfare and the Felicity Party, the liberal Democracy and Progress Party, the Islamist Kurdish Huda Par, and socialists in the Workers’ Party of Turkey and several smaller left-wing parties.

Imprisoned Istanbul Mayor Ekrem Imamoglu backed the legislation in a statement, while Ozgur Ozel, who leads the New Party after splitting from the CHP, also said he supported it. Ozel allowed his party’s lawmakers to vote as they wished, and many opposed the measure, but the overall result was still overwhelming: 468 of parliament’s 592 members voted in favour.

What the law allows

The legislation creates a partial clemency route for former PKK members. Those who did not commit violent crimes, including intentional homicide, can receive pardons. Prisoners convicted of nonviolent offences can also be released.

The law is tied to a sequence of conditions. The PKK would first have to lay down its weapons and show Turkish authorities that it was meeting its side of the agreement. The group and its affiliated organisations would also be expected to end illicit activities, including smuggling counterfeit goods and drugs. Only then could the repatriation of eligible members begin.

According to the plan described in the legislation, that repatriation could start in September and would open a six-month implementation period. Former PKK members who fail to apply within that window would no longer be able to benefit from the law. Turkish intelligence would monitor the process and prepare a report confirming that the PKK had disarmed before it is sent to the National Security Council, which would formally trigger the next stage.

What remains unresolved

The law covers PKK members in all locations, including Europe, and any sentences based only on PKK membership would be dropped on the grounds that membership ended when the organisation dissolved. Some convicted PKK members who have already served 25 years in prison could also become eligible for release.

Even so, it is still unclear which parts of the PKK — whether in Europe, Iraq, Syria or the Qandil Mountains — will try to use the law. Ocalan is not given a formal role, though officials expect that once the law is enacted and implemented he will receive greater freedom in his living arrangements on Imrali Island, including more comfortable accommodation and broader access to journalists and opinion leaders.

Ozel also warned that settling the Kurdish question could open the way for a new alliance between Erdogan and the pro-Kurdish Peoples’ Equality and Democracy Party, potentially helping Erdogan pursue another term. He said the opposition would not stand in the way of peace: “We have not, and will not, put our anger before the future of the nation.”

What happened

Five years after the U.S. withdrawal from Afghanistan, the country has not become the global terrorist hub many American policymakers feared, and the Taliban has ended up helping Washington against Islamic State Khorasan Province even as the costs of that cooperation remain stark.

That reversal looks especially striking against the chaotic exit at Kabul International Airport, where an ISKP suicide attack killed 13 U.S. service members and about 170 Afghan civilians trying to flee Taliban rule. For years, the warning from Washington was that a Taliban-run Afghanistan would again become a haven for terrorists, as it had been before the 2001 invasion.

That fear was rooted in the Taliban’s record in the 1990s, when it sheltered al-Qaeda and like-minded networks that trained thousands of fighters and helped plan the 1998 embassy bombings, the 2000 attack on the USS Cole, and 9/11. Those concerns helped keep the United States fighting a war its officials knew they were losing. In April 2021, William Burns, President Biden’s CIA director, warned that a withdrawal would create a “significant risk” of an al-Qaeda resurgence; four months later, when Biden pulled out, experts said he had given terrorists a leg up.

But the outcome since then has been different. Under Taliban rule, Afghanistan has not become the global terrorist hub feared in Washington. Al-Qaeda, still far weaker than in 2001, has not carried out an attack from Afghanistan on the U.S. or Europe since the withdrawal. The group has also been hit hard by U.S. drone strikes, including one in 2022 that killed Ayman al-Zawahiri, and it has still not named a replacement. Its de facto leader, Saif al-Adel, is hiding in Iran.

The Taliban remains no friend to the United States, and it continues to brutalize Afghans, especially women. It also still shelters militants including al-Qaeda, which has begun training more members in Afghanistan since the U.S. left. Even so, the Taliban has become a counterterrorism partner of a sort: it has arrested and killed ISKP fighters and leaders, and sometimes persuaded them to surrender. Because it now controls the whole country and has networks everywhere, Washington has used its intelligence to warn Iran and Russia about ISKP threats and help European states disrupt plots.

The uneasy result is that staying in Afghanistan looks like a mistake, but so does assuming that old enemies cannot sometimes be useful against worse ones.
