What happened

Six months after the Iran war began on Feb. 28, Trump’s promised quick exit has given way to a moving target: military victory, a Hormuz-linked peace deal and now economic squeeze, while Iran, Oman and regional mediators keep searching for a safer off-ramp.

Trump entered the war projecting a four- to five-week campaign. Instead, his own description of the endgame has kept changing, and he now says the administration is focused on weakening Iran economically and stopping it from getting a nuclear weapon. There are no ongoing U.S.-Iran talks, he said.

The original finish line was military victory

Two days after the war began, Trump set out an expansive military objective. “Our objectives are clear,” he said on March 2, adding that the U.S. was destroying Iran’s missile capabilities, annihilating its navy, preventing it from obtaining a nuclear weapon and stopping the regime from arming and directing terrorist armies outside its borders.

In the opening weeks, Trump also suggested a role in Iran’s political future. By April 7, he was saying, “We have already met and exceeded all Military objectives, and are very far along with a definitive Agreement concerning Longterm PEACE with Iran.” Six weeks later, that promise had become more concrete: on June 17, he said, “We reached an agreement with Iran that achieves everything we set out to accomplish. Everything and much more.”

That June agreement took the form of a memorandum of understanding that called for an immediate end to hostilities, the reopening of the Strait of Hormuz and 60 days of negotiations toward a final nuclear deal. It was meant to tie together the military phase and the diplomatic exit. But it did not survive the summer.

Hormuz became the bargaining chip

The Strait of Hormuz has been Iran’s most powerful card since the war began, and the fight over it soon became central to Trump’s endgame. U.S. officials say the American military has steadily eroded Iran’s leverage over the waterway, and Trump told Axios on Thursday: “That sucker is open. The Iranian response is very mild. They don’t want us to go back at them. That’s the whole ball game. The rest doesn’t matter.”

After the memorandum of understanding collapsed, the military worked to reopen the strait unilaterally, according to the evidence. That effort included a U.S. military task force guiding ships through the southern channel with air cover, intercepting Iranian drone and cruise missile attacks, a two-week bombing campaign that undercut the IRGC’s ability to hit ships, and a mine-sweeping operation involving Navy divers, Navy SEALs, underwater and surface drones and private contractors.

U.S. officials say the number of ships crossing the strait and the amount of oil exported through it remain below pre-war levels, but they say the situation has improved and that Iran has lost much of its control there. At the same time, the evidence notes periodic reports of tankers being struck by projectiles and skepticism from oil experts and tanker trackers about U.S. figures. Iran, for its part, has continued to demand that the U.S. implement the June memorandum and offer terms for reopening the strait.

From a deal to a blockade

After saying on July 8, “I don’t want to deal with them anymore.... As far as I’m concerned, it’s over,” Trump later sketched out a different choice: “There’s a military exit where we just keep going just the way we are... or there’s a smarter strategy and that’s you make a deal.” By then, the earlier off-ramp had collapsed, and the administration was moving toward a broader economic campaign.

On Aug. 19, Trump made that shift explicit: “I am announcing the MOST CRUSHING ECONOMIC OPERATION EVER TAKEN AGAINST ANY COUNTRY! This will be Economic Warfare and Isolation on an unprecedented scale.” He also said, “We are just watching Iran with its huge inflation and the fact they have no money,” and the administration has framed the campaign as a blockade and sanctions push meant to choke off Tehran’s finances.

Trump has kept insisting that Iran cannot obtain a nuclear weapon, but the route to preventing that has changed several times over the six months: combat, then negotiations, then a deal tied to Hormuz, and now economic pressure. He told reporters Thursday that he was “not in a hurry” to get Iran back to the negotiating table and added, “We don’t want to speak to them. We’re not looking to meet or anything.”

The off-ramp now runs through mediators

Even as Trump says he is not interested in direct talks, regional mediation has not stopped. Iran and Oman are discussing a framework to restore safer commercial passage through the Strait of Hormuz, while regional mediators push for a broader off-ramp. Qatar’s prime minister has been in Tehran for talks, and the Pakistani army chief and Oman’s foreign minister have also been part of the recent round of engagement described in the evidence.

White House envoy Steve Witkoff is still talking to Qataris and other mediators, but U.S. negotiators appear to be betting on pressure rather than immediate diplomacy. The question now is whether the economic squeeze, along with the changing reality in the strait, can push Tehran into new terms — or whether the war simply keeps moving from one unfinished exit to the next.

Haitians deserve “more than mere survival”, acting deputy UN relief chief tells Security Council

What happened

Haiti’s humanitarian crisis is deepening as violence, hunger, mass displacement and repeated shocks leave millions in need, and the UN is warning that emergency aid alone is not enough to stop a country where families are being pushed toward “mere survival.”

At a Security Council briefing in New York on 28 August 2026, Indrika Ratwatte, the acting UN assistant secretary-general for humanitarian affairs, said Haiti was facing “one of the most severe” crises in the Western Hemisphere. He told the council that more than half the population — some 6.4 million people — need humanitarian assistance, while health services are crumbling and people are fleeing violence.

Violence and displacement are widening the emergency

Ratwatte pointed to the latest attack on Kenscoff as another marker of how dangerous conditions have become. He said dozens of people were killed and more than 2,600 were forced from their homes, many for the second or third time. He also said over 50 people were reportedly abducted. Families are still searching for missing relatives, he added, while communities are left with trauma, displacement and uncertainty about what happened to loved ones.

The briefing described violence as only one part of the emergency. Haiti, Ratwatte said, remains highly vulnerable to recurring climate shocks and other natural hazards. He said Hurricane Melissa brought death and destruction last year, the country is now facing another hurricane season, and recent seismic activity in the region is a stark reminder of Haiti’s earthquake risk.

The scale of displacement is now central to the crisis. Ratwatte said nearly 1.5 million people — 12 per cent of Haiti’s population — are internally displaced, and half of them are children. He said displacement has doubled in just two years and that conditions in many displacement sites are “squalid,” with overcrowding, insecurity and inadequate water and sanitation.

He also said more people are being displaced in the Port-au-Prince metropolitan area and the Artibonite department even as families return to parts of the West and Centre departments, including places where safe, voluntary and sustainable return has not yet been established. “People must be able to return home safely and voluntarily,” he said.

Ratwatte said deportations to Haiti continue at scale. In the first eight months of 2026, he said, almost 200,000 people were forcibly returned, a 10 per cent increase from the same period last year. He said many arrive without resources, documentation or support networks, and that some have urgent protection needs, including survivors of sexual violence, pregnant and lactating women, people with disabilities, and unaccompanied and separated children.

Children, health services and aid access are under strain

The briefing also focused on the impact on women and children. Ratwatte said humanitarian partners have recorded over 5,500 incidents of gender-based violence in the first six months of the year alone — about 30 cases a day. He said women and girls make up the vast majority of survivors, about 70 per cent of reported incidents involved rape, and nearly three quarters of reported rape cases involved collective rape.

For children, Ratwatte said life has become a daily struggle for survival. He said more than 2.6 million children across the country need humanitarian assistance, and the number of children recruited and used by armed groups rose by an estimated 200 per cent in 2025, according to UNICEF. He said children are more vulnerable when they go hungry, lose access to school or are separated from their families, and that in some areas armed groups are seen as one of the few sources of food, income, shelter or protection.

Education is also being hit hard. Ratwatte said more than 1,600 schools remain closed, affecting 7,500 teachers and depriving more than 240,000 students of learning. He said every day out of school leaves children more exposed to violence, exploitation and recruitment.

Essential services are being squeezed as well. Ratwatte said healthcare, livelihoods, water and sanitation continue to be disrupted by violence and insecurity, and that just 10 per cent of health facilities with inpatient capacity remain operational in Port-au-Prince, including only one public hospital.

He told the council that humanitarian partners reached more than 2.4 million people in the first half of the year, but said access to many of the most acute needs depends on sustained engagement and on preserving humanitarian principles. He warned that aid workers must not be criminalized for engaging with armed groups when necessary to reach people in need, or for providing medical care to people injured during security operations.

Ratwatte ended with three calls: stop the violence, fund the response, and tackle the wider causes of the crisis. “The people of Haiti deserve more than mere survival,” he said. “They deserve safety, opportunity, and hope for a better future.”

What happened

Doctors have agreed the world’s first universal definition of a heart attack, with new guidance intended to improve treatment for women and reduce delays in care. The agreement was announced in Munich on the opening day of the annual European Society of Cardiology congress, and it was signed off by the ESC, the American College of Cardiology, the American Heart Association and the World Heart Federation. It is meant to update older definitions that prioritized the most serious cases caused by a clot blocking blood flow to the heart, while less common forms more often seen in women were treated as less important and sometimes less urgently.

The new guidance upgrades three types of attack that are up to 10 times more common in women: coronary artery spasm, coronary embolism and spontaneous coronary artery dissection (Scad). It also lowers the troponin threshold for diagnosing any type of heart attack in women, after women had previously been expected to reach the same troponin level as men. Prof Bryan Williams called it a “landmark moment”, and Prof Nicholas Mills said it was the first truly global approach to aligning how heart attacks are diagnosed.
