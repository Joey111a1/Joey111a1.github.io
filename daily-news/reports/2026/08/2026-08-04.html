What happened

Rapists and people convicted of serious child sex offences will be kept out of England and Wales’s early release scheme after a government overhaul, but the change still leaves other prisoners eligible to go free early and has not calmed anger from victims’ families.

The government said prisoners convicted of rape, grooming and serious child sex offences will be excluded from the forthcoming scheme. Prime Minister Andy Burnham had paused plans to release some prisoners in September, saying the move was needed to stop unprecedented overcrowding in prisons after backlash from survivors and victims. The scheme will now restart in October.

Burnham said he “wanted to go even further”, but that would not be possible without the prison system collapsing and putting the public “at much greater risk”. In a statement on Monday, he said: “I fully understand the anger, anxiety and distress that’s been caused. I’ve heard that loud and clear.”

What the new rules change

The sentencing system had already been altered in January so that early release depended on the length of a fixed sentence rather than the type of offence. Some of the most serious offenders were already excluded, including murderers serving life sentences or those on extended sentences.

Under the latest changes, rape and child sexual offences will also be excluded. The government said this does not require changes to existing legislation before October, although it may amend the law later to exclude certain offenders from being released.

Other prisoners will still be covered by the broader scheme. Those serving longer sentences of four years or more will be eligible for release at the halfway point of their term, rather than after two-thirds. People serving less serious sentences could be freed after a third of their sentence instead of half. The changes are due to be phased in, beginning with prisoners serving less than 575 days and eventually reaching people jailed for 12 years or more, who would become eligible in June 2027.

The government also said offenders who serve their full sentence for rape or serious child sex offences will face stronger supervision after prison, including 12 months of GPS monitoring and tougher community oversight. It said the “presumption” will be that offenders are tagged and placed under longer supervision and “restriction zones”.

Andrew Harper’s family and wider criticism

The decision has been especially sensitive for the family of PC Andrew Harper. Lissie Harper, his widow, said it was “disgusting” that his killers could be eligible for early release and later said she was “beyond appalled and bristling with anger to hear that Andrew’s killers are soon to be free to roam the streets”.

PC Harper, 28, was killed in 2019 when he was dragged by a car down a country road as three teenagers fled the scene of a quad bike theft in Berkshire. His family had previously said they received a letter from the Ministry of Justice confirming that the offenders who killed him could be included in the scheme.

Lissie Harper’s anger sits alongside wider criticism from victims’ groups and other officials. The Ministry of Justice also announced an extra £10 million for frontline victim support services, including a new national helpline. But some critics said the changes did not go far enough, particularly because domestic abuse perpetrators were not excluded. Burnham said the government could not go further without risking prison capacity and public safety.

What happens next

The immediate change is that the scheme restarts in October, but with rape and serious child sexual offences excluded. The government said it may still make further amendments later. It has also promised to phase the changes in over time, while expanding victim support and adding new monitoring and supervision measures for people released under the scheme.
