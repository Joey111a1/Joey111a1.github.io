Colombia scrambles to find survivors after deadly earthquake

What happened

A magnitude 7.4 earthquake that struck western Colombia on Monday left rescue teams digging through collapsed buildings in Cali and Pereira, with death tolls still shifting and hundreds of people reported missing or trapped as families waited for word from the rubble.

The quake hit at 7:34am local time on Monday and was felt across a wide area of western Colombia. In Cali, Colombia’s third-largest city, rescue crews used hammers, shovels and heavy machinery to reach people beneath thick slabs of concrete, while volunteers formed human chains to clear debris. In Pereira and other hard-hit places, people spent the night outdoors after homes were destroyed or left unsafe, and aftershocks were still being felt on Tuesday.

Searching the wreckage

In Cali, the collapse of the seven-storey Vanessa Building became one of the most urgent rescue sites. Residents and volunteers described the structure as reduced to wreckage, with rescuers trying to reach an estimated 20 people trapped underneath. A local volunteer coordinating the effort, Maximiliano Herrera, said there were “approximately 17 children trapped under the rubble,” and said two children, Samuel and Sara, had shown signs of life but were in “the most out-of-reach corner of the entire site.”

Maria Isabel Cumbal, watching the rescue operation at the site in the south of the city, said, “I never thought I would see my city like this.” Nearby, the collapsed apartment block had fallen onto a neighbouring building that housed a nursery, according to volunteers at the scene. Samir Valencia, who lived in the building until 2022, later said he had watched a mobile-phone video from a friend’s apartment inside the Vanessa Building, and that his own new home had also been severely damaged.

Luis Saenz, a 22-year-old journalism student from Cali, said the quake felt “eternal” and recalled being at home with morning coffee when it struck. He remembered his cat darting under the bed before his family fled into the street. Saenz later joined other survivors wearing a hard hat and face mask to sift through the destruction.

Death tolls, damage and emergency measures

Reported death tolls differed across the accounts. Al Jazeera cited government figures saying at least 254 people had died nationwide, most of them in Pereira and Cali. BBC News said more than 180 people had been confirmed dead, while an aggregated figure gathered from local officials put the toll higher, potentially above 240. In Cali alone, local government statistics cited by Al Jazeera put the death toll at least 95, with 45 structures fully or partially collapsed.

The earthquake also damaged about 5,000 homes, according to the United Nations Office for the Coordination of Humanitarian Affairs, while BBC News reported that more than 1,100 homes had been destroyed and almost 8,000 damaged. In Cali, officials set up a unified command post to coordinate the rescue effort. BBC News said Mayor Alejandro Eder imposed a curfew from 8pm to 6am to guard against looting, and Al Jazeera reported that he announced the “militarisation” of the city with an exemption for rescue teams, medical staff and other essential workers.

President Abelardo de la Espriella declared a state of emergency and visited affected areas, including Pereira and Quibdo, according to the reports. He said almost 2,600 people had been injured and nearly 200 remained missing, while rescue operations continued across the hardest-hit cities. Support also arrived from firefighters, rescue teams, volunteers and private companies, with some of the heavy machinery at the Vanessa Building donated by construction firms.

What still hangs in the balance

The main uncertainty remains how many people are still trapped or missing, and how high the final death toll will be. In Cali, officials said 239 people remained trapped, while rescuers at the Vanessa Building kept working through the night and into Tuesday morning. In Pereira, BBC News reported 79 deaths according to the city’s fire department, and in Manizales the mayor said five people had been killed.

Search efforts were also complicated by fears of looting, the strain on local agencies and fresh aftershocks. Even as rescue workers and volunteers kept clearing rubble, some residents said the response felt disorganised. Others said they were still waiting for names to be answered from beneath the debris.

What happened

Fishermen along Yemen’s Red Sea coast are being forced to choose between hunger and the danger of gunfire, sea mines and disappearances as the shoreline becomes a contested front line, with no clear answer over who is restricting access to the water.

In al-Makha, also known as Mocha, Mazhar says he has struggled to make a living since war arrived in the region in 2015. Fuel is expensive, renting a boat is expensive, and his family depends entirely on fishing income. But the bigger problem, he said, is that every trip offshore carries the risk that he will not come back.

Mazhar said a fishing boat in al-Khawkhah was recently fired on and two colleagues were lost. “No one knows why, and the authorities haven’t announced the cause of the incident,” he said. He also described fishermen disappearing without a trace. Even so, when there is no food left at home, he said he still goes out.

A coast where fishing and war overlap

Yemen’s Red Sea coast is one of the main hubs of the country’s fishing industry, and before the war it was Yemen’s second-largest source of export earnings after oil. But the industry has been hit hard since the early years of the conflict. A 2018 study cited in the report found fish exports from the region had fallen by about 70 percent.

The coastline is also strategically important for reasons beyond fishing. Yemen’s principal port of Hodeidah sits about 160 kilometres up the coast from al-Makha, and the Bab al-Mandeb Strait lies at the southern entrance to the Red Sea, controlling access to one of the world’s most vital waterways. That has made the area heavily contested since the early days of the war. The Houthis control most of the territory from Yemen’s border with Saudi Arabia down to Hodeidah, while pro-government forces, largely the Guardians of the Republic force, operate south of the port city.

The front line was quiet in recent years, but recent Houthi attacks against pro-government forces across the country have also targeted al-Makha. At sea, the risks have become even more visible: the Houthis began regularly striking shipping in the Red Sea after the beginning of Israel’s war on Gaza in October 2023, and have also recently attacked Saudi shipping. Those attacks prompted the United States, the United Kingdom, Israel and Saudi Arabia to strike Houthi targets in Yemen at various times, including on the Red Sea.

Fishermen trapped between armed actors and shrinking options

Mazhar said fishermen now face strict boundaries they cannot cross, not only in al-Makha but also in neighbouring al-Khawkhah district and much of the Red Sea coast. He said fishermen spend money on scrap barrels and car bodies to make artificial reefs, but with each escalation they are denied access to those reefs for military reasons and forced to fish near shore.

He said he cannot name the party restricting them now because speaking out could put him at risk, but added that both sides in the war have blocked fishermen at different times. Before 2018, local fishermen say the National Resistance Force, which later gave rise to the Guardians of the Republic forces, kept them from venturing into deep waters and reaching their reefs. Restrictions were later eased, but fishermen say both the Houthis and pro-government forces have imposed new limits since 2023.

The dangers are not only from armed men. Akram, another fisherman in al-Makha, said a sea mine exploded under a boat near the shore in al-Khawkhah after he left home with two neighbours. “I can say with certainty that the Red Sea is no longer safe,” he said. He is thinking about moving to Aden, where he believes fishing on Yemen’s southern coastline will be safer.

Fouad Doubla, head of the Fishermen’s Association in al-Khawkhah, said more than 30,000 fishermen have been affected along the Red Sea coast and are struggling to provide basic necessities for their families because fishing is their only income. He said 113 fishermen have gone missing in the Red Sea since 2023, with families receiving no information about where they are. He also said sea mines have killed 264 fishermen and maimed 215 others, and fishermen have been detained by pirates in the Red Sea.

Some have left the profession altogether. Ismail, a 48-year-old fisherman from a family of fishermen, sold gear he had collected over 20 years and used the money to buy a minibus. His wife sold some jewellery to help him start the business, he said, relieved that he no longer has to face the fear each time he goes to sea. Mazhar, still fishing close to shore, said he has thought about leaving too. “As soon as I find one, I won’t hesitate for a second,” he said, referring to an alternative.

What happened

By February 2045, Natalie is still arguing with her husband over what to call the violence that began in 2036, even as the United States is nearly through a negotiated split into two countries. The larger question underneath their marriage is whether the country’s break, and the war that preceded it, should be remembered as a civil war, an insurgency, or something else.

When Oklahoma City made the war feel real

Natalie says she took longer than some people to start calling it the war; by “some people,” she mainly means her husband, Asher. He reached that point on a first Friday in June 2036, after three hours of news coverage from Oklahoma City and headlines that read, in part, “siege: more than 300 killed and 400 wounded in terrorist military attacks.” Asher told her, “This is it, Natalie. It’s started. The civil war.”

She says he claims she rolled her eyes. She does not think she did. What she remembers saying was, “these idiots in Oklahoma obviously want us to think that’s what it is.” That moment sits inside a longer pattern: Asher, whom she met in 2021, had long expected a political nightmare just around the corner, while she thought his alarm often came mixed with a doomer’s relish. He thought she was too disengaged from politics, sometimes guilty of “willful obliviousness,” “toxic positivity,” and denial about the “inevitable American apocalypse.”

Even after the insurgency ended, the argument did not. Seven years later, he still says she is too quick to move on. During a call last November, on their first Thanksgiving apart in 23 years, he asked: “You know people are still getting killed, right?” She answered by pointing out that people were still dying from Nipah and COVID, and that not even most of his fellow San Franciscans wore masks.

The argument over names

For Natalie and Asher, the fight was never only about events; it was also about language. During the summer of the first attacks, she tried out “the hillbilly intifada” for the violence, meaning it ironically and disparagingly. Asher objected to comparing the Plains Patriot Militia to Palestinian terrorists. She then called the early bombings and shootings “the Troubles,” because she thought the attacks might resemble the decades of organized violence in Northern Ireland. That term bothered him too.

Asher’s complaint was that “rebellion,” “insurgency,” or “uprising” — unless modified by “Christian-fascist,” “white-nationalist,” or “neo-Nazi” — romanticized the “anti-tyranny” delusions of people he considered mass-murdering fascists. At one point he referred to the attackers as “your psycho friends,” which triggered a big fight because Natalie grew up in Tennessee and called them “my friends” in that sense. She resisted the label Civil War II, or even “Civil War 2,” because she thought it reduced catastrophe to something game-like.

Over time, though, she says she gave up the resistance. When Asher asked, “So you’re happy to call the big one that didn’t succeed a civil war, but not this smaller one that did?” she finally conceded: if history repeated itself and this counted as their second civil war, then the first was tragedy and this one was “farce plus tragedy.” Her preferred older phrase for the first conflict remains Walt Whitman’s “the War of Secession.”

The scale of the violence is part of why the labels matter to her. She describes “mass shootings and bombings and assassinations and kidnappings and disappearances,” and says the war killed 28,000 Americans, half of them innocent civilians, and wounded another 45,000. It lasted 18 months. Still, she argues against treating it as some all-encompassing apocalypse, saying that “could be worse” remains a true way to think about what happened.

What Disunion left behind

By 2045, the country itself is nearly finished with the aftermath. Natalie says an improbable plan for “Disunion” was agreed to, then supported by large majorities on both sides, and negotiated and renegotiated over seven years. Disunion Day is set for June 30, which made last year’s Independence Day weekend — from June 30 to July 4 — the opening of a new era, with the United States of America and the Free American Republic independent of each other.

She says the name Disunion feels too clinical and wonky, while “the breakup” seems more exact to her: mutual, broad, idiomatic, and plain. To her, Americans had grown apart like spouses in a failing marriage. Even so, she also says the country is still using the same air-traffic-control and interstate-highway systems, eating the same food, sharing the same digital life, and watching the same Super Bowl.

What has changed most for her is not only the fading of war trauma but the fading of the compulsive politicization that came before it. She says the active contempt has become less salient, including in rural Middle Tennessee, where she has stayed rather than moving to San Francisco with Asher. That distance, and the call on a screen, help her avoid the fights that once filled their life. Her biggest regret is missing months of their daughter Logan’s life after Logan chose to live with her father. What happened

Quincy, Washington, has turned data-center tax revenue into brand-new schools, parks and utilities, but the same boom has also made the once-cheap farming town more expensive for many local workers.

A few weeks ago, the city opened a $15 million aquatic center with a six-lane pool, waterslides and a lazy river, part of a public parks spending spree that will culminate next year with the Q-Plex, a 143,000-square-foot indoor sports complex. The town also has a $120 million state-of-the-art high school, along with improved hospital, library, police and fire stations, paved sidewalks, sewage systems and a wastewater treatment plant. Roughly 30 data centers now shoulder an estimated 57% of Quincy’s property taxes, helping pay for what city leaders describe as a civic overhaul.

The change has been years in the making. Microsoft bought 75 acres of bean fields in 2006 to build a 500,000-square-foot data center, drawn by Quincy’s cheap power from two public hydroelectrical plants on the Columbia River. Yahoo, Dell and Intuit later clustered there too. The town’s data centers have created around 900 jobs for engineers, pipefitters and electricians, and Washington State analyses say each of those jobs generates four to six more in construction, retail and local services. Former city administrator Pat Haley called the result the “Quincy miracle,” saying the centers “opened up what could have been a low-opportunity town into this place with brand-new everything.”

But Quincy is still a farming town, producing potatoes, apples and alfalfa, and food processors such as Lamb Weston remain among its biggest employers. For many mostly Hispanic agriculture and food-processing workers, the boom has not felt equal. Home prices have roughly doubled over the last decade, rising faster than incomes, and Quincy still lacks enough apartments and rentals. The city recently approved a zoning code change to make it easier to build rentals for low- and middle-income households. Nina Martinez of the Latino Civic Alliance said, “Families have been pushed out,” adding that “no average farmworker or agricultural worker can afford it.”

The Quincy story comes as data centers for artificial intelligence face bans and pushback in other places, including nearby parts of Washington, over worries about electricity bills, pollution and jobs. In Quincy, officials say residential power rates have risen, but large industrial customers such as data centers are paying more, and the centers use about 8% of the city’s water supply.

South Korea added 108,000 jobs in July from a year earlier, but the headline gain masked continued weakness in manufacturing and a sharp deterioration in youth employment, leaving the labor market with a split picture.

Government data released Wednesday showed the number of employed people reached 29.13 million in July, up from 29.02 million a year earlier. Job growth had turned positive again in June, when employment rose by 63,000 after a decline of 40,000 in May, the first drop in 17 months. The country’s jobless rate stood at 2.6 percent in July, up 0.2 percentage point from a year earlier, while the number of unemployed people totaled 776,000, an increase of 50,000.

Sector trends also diverged. Jobs in health and social welfare services increased by 173,000, arts, sports and recreation-related services added 48,000 jobs, and public administration gained 46,000. Manufacturing remained the weak spot: employment in the sector fell by 68,000 in July from a year earlier, marking the 25th consecutive month of decline, though the drop narrowed from 97,000 in June. The number of economically inactive people also rose by 99,000 to 16.1 million, while those who said they were not working and were simply resting fell by 62,000 to 2.51 million.

What happened

Researchers introduced unsupervised on-policy self-distillation, or U-OPSD, for post-training large language models, arguing that on-policy self-distillation can be done using only a model’s own generations rather than ground-truth signals, environmental feedback, or guidance from larger models. The preprint says the method uses internal consistency to build a pseudo solution from multiple rollouts by majority vote under a self-consistency threshold, then distills on the completions that disagree with that pseudo solution.

Motif 3: Technical Report
science_cs_aipolitics_and_powertechnology_and_aiscience anchorpreprint_not_peer_reviewed
What happened

Motif 3 is introduced as a decoder-only Mixture-of-Experts language model with 314 billion total parameters and 13.2 billion activated per token. Each sparse MoE layer contains 384 routed experts, with eight selected per token. The model is built around Grouped Differential Latent Attention (GDLA), which combines grouped differential attention with the compressed key-value representation of Multi-head Latent Attention. It also incorporates modified manifold-constrained hyper-connections, Expert Specific PolyNorm activations, and multi-token prediction.

It is pretrained on approximately 12.5 trillion tokens covering web documents, STEM, code, mathematics, multilingual content, and domain-specialized corpora. Training uses expert-balancing and numerical-stabilization techniques, plus selective MXFP8 computation and communication, memory-efficient fused kernels, and window-aware context parallelism to support context lengths up to 256K tokens. The post-training pipeline combines general supervised fine-tuning, six specialist teachers trained with reinforcement learning, a software-engineering teacher trained with supervised fine-tuning, and Multi-teacher On-Policy Distillation. The resulting model is described as consolidating reasoning, coding, tool use, professional work, long-context understanding, calibrated abstention, and instruction following, with competitive results on leading open weight model benchmarks.

Stealing Reasoning Traces from Proprietary LLM APIs
science_cs_aitechnology_and_aiscience anchorpreprint_not_peer_reviewed
What happened

Researchers described a vulnerability in proprietary large language model APIs that hide step-by-step reasoning, or chain-of-thought, by returning it to clients as encrypted text blocks that are sent back with later requests. They said those blocks were fully compatible and interchangeable across different sessions, users, and models within a provider’s ecosystem, and used that compatibility to build a scalable decryption jailbreak. By injecting an encrypted reasoning trace from one model into a weaker model from the same provider, they said they could make it decode and output the trace verbatim in plaintext without directly jailbreaking the stronger model.

The preprint says the flaw creates four attack vectors. It said the method can bypass anti-distillation protections and extract proprietary reasoning across Anthropic, OpenAI, and Google; enable large-scale private data extraction; reveal hazardous information hidden in the reasoning process even when the visible answer refuses a malicious request; and support invisible prompt injections inside encrypted blocks. The authors said developers often share session logs publicly without knowing what the encrypted blocks contain, and that decoding 315,320 reasoning blocks from public repositories recovered 367 Personally Identifiable Information artifacts and 182 credentials. They said they disclosed the issue responsibly and proposed cryptographic and system-level mitigations.

Agent Memory Distillation: Empowering Small LLM Agents with Hierarchical Teacher Memory
science_cs_aitechnology_and_aiscience anchorpreprint_not_peer_reviewed
What happened

Agent Memory Distillation (AMD) is a training-free framework for small LLM agents that transfers structured knowledge from a large teacher agent through hierarchical memory. The preprint says memory systems have shown promise for agent performance, but small language models still struggle to generate enough successful trajectories on their own. AMD builds three memory types from successful teacher trajectories: Workflow memory for task-level strategies, Subtask memory for intermediate behavioral examples, and Function memory for function-calling conventions and common pitfalls. Workflow and Subtask memories are injected at the start of each task, while Function memory is retrieved when tool-calling errors occur.

The study evaluates AMD on three tool-use benchmarks with four student models sized 4B to 8B parameters and GPT-5-mini as the teacher. It reports average accuracy gains of 27.2%p on AppWorld, 11.2%p on BFCL V3, and 3.4%p on ToolSandbox, and says AMD consistently outperforms existing memory-based baselines. Further analysis says Subtask memory contributes the largest gains, teacher effectiveness depends on both teacher capability and student compatibility, and 4B-sized students benefit most from AMD.

What happened

Five years after the Taliban returned to Kabul, Afghanistan’s girls and women are still being pushed out of education, work and public life, even as many governments deepen ties with the Taliban without demanding rights gains.

Girls in Fayzabad can still go to primary school until they are 11 or 12, but they are barred from secondary school and university. Nearly four years after the bans on secondary and higher education and on most forms of employment, women are also barred from public-sector appointments. One female Afghan academic told researchers, “It’s like we’re at a crossroads; all the paths are dark.”

When the Taliban took power in August 2021, its leadership verbally promised girls and women would not be excluded from education and workplaces. But the reality has moved in the opposite direction. Nature said the trend is now unmistakable: conditions for girls and women have worsened over the past five years, and the international community has let more than 20 million women and girls slip from view.

The scale is large. Since 2021, around 2.4 million girls have been excluded from secondary education and more than 100,000 young women have been unable to access higher education, according to UNESCO. Some women still work in limited roles, including health care and humanitarian aid, but they do so at great risk. Restrictions have continued to mount: women were stopped from entering UN facilities last September, the Baran Health Sciences Institute in Bamiyan closed in May, and Afghan universities were told to stop teaching 680 books by women or by authors from Western countries or Iran and to drop subjects including gender studies, women’s rights and democracy.
