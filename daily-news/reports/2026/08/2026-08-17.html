As India seeks to prevent exam paper leaks, what can it learn from other countries?
global_south_and_exploitedpolitics_and_powermaterial_harmregional expansion
What happened

India is trying to overhaul its exam security after a leak in the National Eligibility cum Entrance Test (NEET) triggered protests, a minister’s resignation and a criminal probe, while experts debate whether technology can fix a system built around one high-stakes paper.

The NEET leak and its fallout

India’s biggest pre-medical exam was supposed to decide who gets to save lives as future doctors. Instead, the leak of the paper used for NEET reportedly added to the pressure on students forced to retake it, and at least 20 medical students reportedly took their own lives. The crisis also led to student protests across the country in June, the resignation of Education Minister Dharmendra Pradhan and scrutiny from the Supreme Court over the National Testing Agency’s handling of the exam.

The paper leak has also become a criminal case. India’s Central Bureau of Investigation reportedly arrested 13 people, including three NTA subject experts, coaching-centre operators and middlemen, and investigators described what they found as a multi-state conspiracy. The government has since appointed a task force led by Nandan Nilekani, co-founder of Infosys and architect of the Aadhaar digital identity project, to recommend technology reforms to the exam system.

What other countries do differently

Experts say the NEET episode exposed a broader weakness: one paper can put the futures of more than 2.2 million students into turmoil. They argue that the key question is not simply whether India can digitise the exam, but whether it can redesign a system built around a single high-stakes paper, a long chain of human custody and fragmented accountability.

Wally Dalrymple, chief security officer at Educational Testing Service, said computer-based testing “doesn’t solve the integrity problem, it just relocates it.” His point was that technology can make leaks harder, but it cannot restore trust by itself. Cybersecurity researcher Sai Krishna said India should not look for one technology to copy, but for a way to reduce the value of any single leak.

China’s gaokao, the world’s largest university entrance examination, offers one model. Krishna said Chinese authorities treat question papers as state secrets, with undistributed papers printed at specially authorised facilities and transported under strict chain-of-custody procedures, including police escorts, GPS-tracked vehicles and multiple handover checks. Officials can impose severe criminal penalties for leaks or theft. China has also continued to adjust its exam security: during this year’s gaokao, major AI firms including ByteDance, Tencent, Alibaba, Baidu, Moonshot AI and DeepSeek temporarily disabled photo-based question solving and other exam-related responses during test hours, according to a Bloomberg report.

The United States takes a different route. Dalrymple said tests such as the GRE draw questions from a large question bank so candidates receive different but equivalent versions of the exam, and many also use adaptive testing, where question difficulty changes according to a candidate’s previous answers. That makes any single stolen set of questions far less valuable. Dalrymple said ETS relies on a “security architecture instead of a single control,” using refreshed question banks, identity checks, test-centre safeguards and monitoring together so that “one leak can’t take down an entire national exam.”

The redesign question

India’s own experts say the answer may lie in redesign rather than digitisation alone. Former education secretary R Subrahmanyam told CNA the problem is “a design problem,” and said online exams would help but the assessment system as a whole needs attention. In a policy paper published in July 2026 by the Centre for Research in Schemes and Policies, he suggested replacing a make-or-break exam like NEET with multiple test dates and dynamically generated but equally difficult papers, so that the leak of one paper would have far less impact.

He has also proposed a nationwide continuous assessment system built by upgrading existing schools, colleges and polytechnics into a network of accredited digital assessment centres. Under that plan, every senior secondary school would gradually gain secure computer laboratories for both teaching and assessment, with the transition spread over about a decade.

The scale of the challenge is already visible in current proposals. Ministry of Education and NTA plans submitted to a parliamentary committee in July suggest that moving NEET online in 2027 could require the exam to be spread over five to six days across about 1,000 centres in 500 cities, with roughly 500,000 candidates sitting each day. That would require secure computer labs, reliable power and connectivity, trained staff and multiple equivalent test versions.

Even then, technology may only solve part of the problem. Ronojoy Sen, senior research fellow at the Institute of South Asian Studies in Singapore, said tech might prevent leaks but would not change India’s dependence on competitive exams and the pressures that come with them.
