Kurdish hunger striker says ICE force-feeding ‘mentally broke me’
global_front_pagepolitics_and_powerglobal_south_and_exploitedpublic_healthmaterial_harmanchor
What happened

Gabar Choli says eight months of force-feeding in US immigration detention left him physically injured and psychologically shattered, and his case now sits inside a broader fight over how far ICE can go against hunger strikers.

How Choli’s hunger strike began

Choli, a Kurdish film-maker and activist now living in Canada after being deported, said he was detained by US Immigration and Customs Enforcement after his asylum application was denied. He had previously lived in Los Angeles with a work permit while his case was pending, and he said he had sought asylum in the US because of death threats tied to his political activism while in Canada.

He said he turned to a hunger strike to protest the conditions at the facility and to press for a resolution to his immigration case. After he was transferred to the Port Isabel federal detention center in Los Fresnos, south Texas, ICE officials force-fed him there from late May 2025 until he was deported in January, according to a Guardian investigation. Choli said detention center guards physically restrained him while ICE medical staff inserted a feeding tube through his nose and down his esophagus, then pumped nutritional liquid into his stomach, often twice a day.

“It just broke me”

Speaking publicly about the treatment for the first time, Choli described it as deeply damaging. “It just broke me, mentally broke me,” he said in an exclusive interview. “It came to a point that I decided to write my body an apology letter.”

He said he tried to make clear that his protest was nonviolent. “I was respectful. I just told them I am protesting for humane treatment, nothing else,” he said. “But they were determined to break me.” The account places the force-feeding inside a wider pattern of coercive treatment that international human rights groups have widely considered torture.

Choli’s description also includes the physical effects he says he suffered during the repeated procedures. In records and interviews cited by the Guardian, he described being held down while the tube was inserted, and said one nurse “shoved the tube so hard” that he said it tore his nose from the inside. He said that after such sessions he would be taken to solitary confinement and choke on his own blood.

A broader ICE pattern

Choli’s case is not the only one the Guardian has linked to involuntary medical intervention in immigration detention. An investigation last week found that the second Trump administration used court orders in plans to subject at least 10 hunger strikers in ICE detention to involuntary medical procedures. DHS sources later told the Guardian that between January 2025 and 4 August 2026 ICE obtained court orders for involuntary medical treatment for 18 hunger strikers.

Court records reviewed in Choli’s case describe “involuntary medical treatment” as an umbrella term that can include force-feeding. In his case, ICE sought permission from the court to conduct routine physical exams and lab tests, administer hydration, and, if necessary, use restraints to carry out involuntary monitoring, treatment and the administration of nutrients, fluids and medication by intravenous means or through a nasogastric tube.

ICE and its parent agency, the Department of Homeland Security, said in court records that force-feeding Choli was necessary for his health. They also argued that his prolonged hunger strike posed “a serious threat to the security and good order” of the detention center and accused him of trying to “manipulate the immigration system.” DHS later said ICE seeks involuntary medical treatment only to prevent imminent life-threatening harm or death, and that any such treatment is carried out under legal authority and by qualified medical professionals.

What the records show — and what remains hidden.

The public record around Choli’s case is still incomplete. Many of his court records are sealed, and the Guardian said it could not corroborate some details of his account. The paper said court records and interviews with medical and legal experts nonetheless verify that he was subjected to force-feeding, and that his case could be the longest documented case of force-feeding in ICE detention to date.

Those records also show the role of the federal court. Judge Rolando Olvera, who presided over Choli’s case, denied his request for legal assistance and extended the force-feeding order several times at ICE’s request, according to records filed in federal court in southern Texas. Choli said he had no legal representation throughout the eight-month ordeal.

Medical complications were also recorded in the court papers, including swollen sinuses, damage to his esophagus and stomach problems. Dr Chanelle Diaz, an assistant professor of medicine at Columbia University’s medical center, told the Guardian after reviewing other cases of detained immigrants subjected to force-feeding: “That is just not medical care, that is torture.”
