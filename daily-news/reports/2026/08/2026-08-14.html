What happened

Fernando Grande-Marlaska has sent more National Police immigration officers to Ceuta to speed up the expulsion of 5,000 irregular migrants still in the city after the mass entry of July 30, while the Interior Ministry also adds street-patrol reinforcements and keeps revising the scale of the crisis.

On Thursday, the Interior minister announced the arrival in Ceuta of about twenty agents from the Central Immigration and Borders Brigade to accelerate the bureaucratic procedures needed to remove those migrants. They join six officers from the Central Expatriation and Repatriation Unit, who arrived hours after the crisis broke to support Ceuta staff with filiación, registration and documentation of the adults still in the city so they can be returned to Morocco. Marlaska said that anyone in an irregular situation would be sent back “as soon as possible.”

The changing count of who entered Ceuta

Marlaska also used the press conference to repeat that none of the thousands of irregular migrants still in the autonomous city would remain there, go to the Peninsula or be regularized, except in “very exceptional cases” and where there is maximum vulnerability. He said the message was also meant for people hoping to reach the autonomous cities and to warn them not to heed the mafias that “gamble with their lives.”

The numbers around the mass entry have shifted over time. Until now, Interior had put the number of people who entered Ceuta that day at 72,000, and the minister said 70,000 returned to Morocco in the following days. Government sources have since begun raising the initial figure to 80,000, matching what Ceuta president Juan José Vivas has long defended. Marlaska himself also moved the estimate of those still in Ceuta: on Tuesday he had spoken of 2,000 remaining, and then raised that figure to 5,000.

He also said that around 500 asylum applications had been completed, most of them rejected, and that 1,898 minors were already under guardianship.

More officers on the streets

The new reinforcement is not limited to immigration specialists. Marlaska announced the arrival of 45 members of the Prevention and Response Unit, the UPR, from Madrid, Alicante and Seville. Their task is to increase police presence on the streets after Ceuta residents complained about a supposed rise in crime.

That security response comes alongside judicial action already under way in the city. On the previous Wednesday, the Andalusian High Court said Ceuta’s courts had sent nine immigrants to prison in recent days as alleged perpetrators of crimes. Two of them were accused of separate sexual assaults on minors who had also arrived in the city on July 30, and judges also ordered the expulsion of two others for different crimes. Marlaska said on Tuesday that he had ordered the security forces to expel immediately, with judicial authorization, anyone who is detained.

Morocco, returns and the warning message

The minister also praised Morocco’s “exceptional collaboration” and the “strength” of the Madrid-Rabat relationship, which he described as “absolutely close and based on loyalty.” He said that cooperation — which has been questioned repeatedly by the government partner, Sumar, and by the opposition — has allowed “more than 90%” of those who entered irregularly in Ceuta on July 30 to return to Morocco within the next 24 hours.

Marlaska also denied that the crisis has increased the jihadist threat. “We are monitoring all the people who have entered,” he said. The additional police deployment, the changing official totals and the emphasis on rapid returns all point to the same aim: to empty Ceuta of the migrants who remain there while deterring others from trying to cross.

Last Tuesday night in Culiacán, a casual sushi dinner turned into a view of Sinaloa’s cartel war when livestreamer César Gastélum was shot dead at a KFC, and the city was left trying to decide whether he was a random victim, a warning, or both.

A dinner interrupted by a shooting

The evening began with small signs of normal life in downtown Culiacán: a birthday party at the next table, families and friends walking past car dealerships and strip malls after months of self-imposed lockdown, and a Mexican police chief ordering a salmon roll. Outside, three influencers had pulled up to a KFC down the street dressed as food-delivery workers, with insulated bags on their backs and phones streaming their every move.

Gastélum was the group’s front man and had built a following on harmless pranks. The 24-year-old, stocky and bearded, posted videos of himself dancing in pajamas, dressing as a priest, and sipping iced coffee, even as cartel violence continued around him. He had more than 700,000 followers on TikTok. The draw of the scene in Culiacán was exactly that contrast: the mundane and the horrific existing side by side.

At about 8 p.m., Gastélum and his friends were filming themselves waiting for fried-chicken orders when two men on a motorcycle passed by. “Those guys scare me,” he said. When the motorcycle came back, the driver, wearing a black helmet with a face shield, stopped briefly in front of them, raised a gun about two feet from Gastélum’s head, and fired. The livestream caught the moment the bullet hit, then went dark.

What the livestream showed

An ambulance-team text alerted the narrator, who ran across the street to the KFC and arrived as police were coming in. Gastélum’s body was at the drive-through exit, with blood running toward the sidewalk. Soldiers gathered nearby, some of them scrolling through his TikTok feed, while crime photographers took pictures and uploaded videos. José Ángel Beltrán of Luz Noticias narrated on Facebook Live: “As you can see, they have killed another influencer here in Culiacán.”

The killing did not end that night’s violence. Beltrán said another TikToker had been killed a few blocks away months earlier, and he led coverage of a man shot in the city’s botanical garden after a screening of a Lady Gaga documentary. Around the same time, online commenters and Beltrán suggested that Gastélum’s cowboy hat may have meant more than it seemed. Cowboy hats, Beltrán said, have become a symbol of the Mayos faction of the cartel, raising the possibility that a prankster’s look had been read as a signal.

That uncertainty sits at the center of the story. From afar, it was unclear whether someone like Gastélum showed that the everyday and the violent can coexist in Culiacán, or whether he reflected something darker: the way drug cartels have quietly co-opted unlikely public figures. The piece returns to that question because the city’s violence now reaches people whose role in the conflict is not always obvious.

Why Culiacán kept reading like a message

Culiacán, the capital of Sinaloa, is described as the center of the fiercest and most consequential battle between armed groups in Mexico. The current civil war pits one Sinaloa-cartel faction run by the children and supporters of Joaquín “El Chapo” Guzmán, the Chapitos, against another led by allies of Ismael “El Mayo” Zambada García, the Mayos. In 2022 there were 476 homicides in Sinaloa; in 2025 there were 1,657. The current turf war, which locals call la narcopandemia, has widened the list of victims beyond foot soldiers and hit men.

Influencers and content creators are now recruited to spread cartel messages online, and university and vocational students have been kidnapped, presumably because their skills are needed by criminal groups. About a dozen influencers in Sinaloa have been killed, kidnapped, or attacked, and each case has raised the same question: who were they working for, if anyone? Last year, an unidentified plane dropped thousands of flyers over Culiacán accusing several influencers and musicians of ties to the Chapitos, though Gastélum was not named.

The broader setting is one of heavy and unresolved pressure. A U.S.-government analysis says about a third of Mexico is controlled by organized criminal groups. The Trump administration has framed Sinaloa as a major target, and President Trump has said he is willing to use military force against cartels. In the story, that pressure has not changed the basic pattern: violence keeps rising, drug flows keep moving, and many people in Culiacán do not expect the strategy to lower the danger.

The human cost appears in the disappearance cases too. Marta Pérez Guzman’s son, David Antonio Ruelas Pérez, and his classmate José Isaías Ibarra Duarte were kidnapped by armed men while students at an aviation school in Culiacán. Duarte’s mother, Graciela Duarte Valdez, said Mexican authorities showed her surveillance footage of four masked men forcing the boys into a second car. She later went to the closest U.S. consulate, and the State Department said it was “aware of reports of the kidnapping of a U.S. citizen in Mexico.” Even with that attention, she felt abandoned. By the end, her attention had shifted back to another set of detained young men, whose blurred faces on government photos she studied for any hint that one might be her son.
