What happened

The United States has removed Syria from its state sponsor of terrorism list, a move that also strips al-Nusrah Front, now tied to President Ahmed al-Sharaa and known as Hayat Tahrir al-Sham, of a separate terror designation and raises hopes for investment and sanctions relief.

From a decades-old blacklist to delisting

On Monday, the US Treasury said Syria was no longer on the State Sponsors of Terrorism list, ending a designation that had been in place for decades under the rule of the ousted Assad regime. The announcement confirmed a decision first revealed last month, after the move had earlier been announced pending a review by Congress.

The same action also revoked the designation of al-Nusrah Front, the group formerly led by al-Sharaa and also known as HTS, as a global terrorist organisation. South China Morning Post said HTS had once been al-Qaeda’s official branch in Syria, while Al Jazeera English said the group, under al-Sharaa’s leadership, removed former President Bashar al-Assad in December 2024 after a lightning offensive.

Why the designation mattered

The state sponsor label carried restrictions on US foreign assistance, defence exports and financial transactions, which is why the removal has been treated as a major change for Syria’s economic outlook. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent said the action would help foster additional investment in Syria to promote political and economic stability, and said it was in line with President Donald Trump’s promise to deliver sanctions relief.

Marco Rubio said the steps were taken in recognition of what he called positive actions by the Syrian government under Ahmed al-Sharaa to fully distance Syria from acts of international terrorism. He also said rescinding Syria’s designation as an SST, and HTS’s designation as an SDGT, eliminates the final major barriers for private sector investment and promotes Syria’s economic recovery and reintegration into the global economy.

Damascus welcomes the move

In Syria, the decision was already being celebrated, especially by financial officials. The governor of the central bank, Safwat Raslan, said on X that ending Syria’s designation was “a historic step that restores the country to its natural place in the global economic system,” and added that the bank is “working to build a modern and reliable financial system.”

Finance Minister Mohammed Barnieh, quoted by the official news agency SANA, called Washington’s move “a major and historic success for Syrian diplomacy.” He said it “opens a major and long-awaited door to strengthening Syria’s integration into the global economic and financial system, attracting investment and modern technologies, and supporting development opportunities.”

US envoy to Syria Tom Barrack also welcomed the change, calling the end of the designation a “decisive step in Syria’s remarkable journey from isolation to partnership, and from a source of terrorism to a committed partner in the global fight against it.”

A Reverse Ellis Island
global_front_pageglobal_south_and_exploitedpolitics_and_powerclimate_disastereconomy_and_businessmaterial_harmmajor supplement
What happened

Julio Borrell and other non-Mexican deportees were bused to Tapachula, a southern Mexican city where they had no legal status and often no clear place to stay, turning it into a holding zone far from the U.S. border and exposing how far deportation is being pushed outward.

A bus to Tapachula

After 47 hours on a bus rattling through Mexico, Borrell still did not know where the United States was sending him. Through the window, he watched cities give way to banana plantations and coffee farms, while the passengers around him still wore the clothes they had on when ICE detained them: paint-splattered shirts, pajama pants, landscaping uniforms, bathing suits.

The bus passed a sign for the Guatemala border, then pulled over. When Borrell asked where they were, a Mexican immigration agent answered, “Tapachula.” Borrell had never heard of it, and neither had anyone else on the bus. They were not from there, and not even from Mexico. They were Cuban, Venezuelan, Nicaraguan, and Haitian—many of them elderly, some stripped of their green cards after spending most of their lives in the United States.

Borrell had lived in Miami for 46 years. He had come from Havana in 1980 as part of the Mariel boatlift, raised seven American children, and built a life around work as a mechanic. Last December, after a routine immigration check-in, ICE detained him, sent him to Florida’s Alligator Alcatraz for four months, then to Eloy Detention Center in Arizona, and finally onto the buses that ended in Tapachula.

The city that became the border

What happened to Borrell was part of a practice that began last year without any announcement and has quietly grown since then. According to the account, the Trump administration had bused deportees 2,000 miles from the U.S. border to a small city where they do not have legal status, after being unable or unwilling to send them to their home countries.

That shift has made Tapachula a kind of reverse Ellis Island. The city has become the largest concentration point for deportees dropped in Mexico, while many other migrants traveling through Latin America toward the United States are also halted there. The United Nations’ International Organization for Migration has informally begun calling it “TapaYork.” The city’s border role has changed so much that, in the words of the account, the border has effectively been relocated to Tapachula.

According to data from the Mexican Institute for Migration, 12,977 non-Mexicans were deported to Mexico from January 2025 to March 2026, largely to southern Mexico. At the same time, Mexican agents pull migrants out of commercial buses and cars on the freeway leaving Tapachula to keep them from heading north again. Another set of agents waits for the next busload of Cubans, Venezuelans, and Haitians to arrive for processing. U.S. officials continue to say they want “zero border crossings,” and the account says they have asked Mexico for stricter enforcement and accommodation for long-distance deportations.

Waiting, protesting, and trying to leave

For the people stuck there, Tapachula is less a transit point than a place to wait. Borrell had been there about six months when he said he was trying to earn enough fixing tires to rent a studio apartment. He also knew that a handful of other Cuban deportees had been flown even farther away, to African countries such as Eswatini and South Sudan. “Why the hell did they bring us here?” he asked.

Others in the city have built whole lives around waiting. Mervil Wisdael, a Haitian bread seller, said deportees arrive a few times a week looking lost and dragging their feet. Haitians now make up roughly 10 percent of the city’s public-school students, one local sign of how long many have stayed. The city also has Haitian players on its semiprofessional basketball teams, Haitian food stalls in the market, and municipal immigration employees who have taught themselves Creole. Wisdael tells newly arrived Haitians they need to line up at Mexico’s refugee office every 15 days; after 30, 40, or 50 signatures, many eventually get refugee status, though that can take one to two years.

Even so, Tapachula often feels like a dead end. Haitians without Mexican papers are typically caught at a checkpoint just north of the city, and other migrant communities describe the same sense of being stranded far from anywhere they lived before. The account says there are also Cubans, Venezuelans, Central Americans, Congolese, Senegalese, Bangladeshis, Nepalese, a Cameroonian bar, a Ghanaian restaurant, and a Ukrainian shelter guard. People commonly ask each other two questions: how long have you been here, and when are you leaving?

The pressure has also produced friction. In 2022, a city official said Tapachula was in “a state of siege, a state of chaos,” and Haitian street vendors have at times been removed by local police. This month, migrants set mattresses on fire in the city’s migrant-detention center, prompting military intervention; two Venezuelans were arrested, and Mexico’s immigration agency said the protest was tied to “the dissatisfaction of a group of foreign nationals regarding their immigration process.”

Some Cubans in Tapachula say they will begin marching north in the next week or two, even though they think their chances of reaching the border are close to zero. The point, they say, is to remind Americans that they are still there. Meanwhile, the account says the Trump administration plans to push this approach to more remote locations, and not long after the reporter left Tapachula, Liberia announced it would accept 1,200 non-Liberian deportees from the United States.

Technology is outpacing accountability in Ukraine, UN relief chief tells Security Council
global_front_pagepolitics_and_powerwar_and_conflictpublic_healthclimate_disastereconomy_and_businessmaterial_harmhumanitarian expansion
What happened

Tom Fletcher told the Security Council that Ukraine is entering a stage of war where drones and other technology are reshaping civilian life faster than accountability can keep up, with civilians in Kherson and Kyiv bearing the clearest costs and the question of protection still unresolved.

Speaking on 24 August 2026 on the humanitarian situation in Ukraine, the UN under-secretary-general for humanitarian affairs said July was the deadliest month for civilians in more than four years, with at least 437 killed and more than 2,600 injured. He said civilian casualties were up 37 per cent in the first half of the year compared with the same period last year, and argued that the statistics still did not capture the daily suffering.

Kherson and the changing face of the war

Fletcher said he had spent the previous week in Ukraine and had seen in Kherson how drone warfare was “dismantling civilian life.” He described the area as a landscape of kill zones and kamikaze drones, with roads and buildings covered in anti-drone netting, and said people there had become familiar with the sound and lethality of different models. According to him, there had been a 60 per cent increase in first-person view, or FPV, drone attacks this year, often with civilian victims.

He said the humanitarian response was being forced to adapt to that threat. Huge resources, he said, were now going into subterranean schools and hospitals as people prepared for what he called a chilling future underground. In Kherson hospital, a kilometre from the river that marks the front line, Fletcher said 70 per cent of civilian patients were being treated for blast injuries, mainly from FPV drones, and doctors had grown used to frequent mass-casualty incidents involving civilians.

Fletcher also said he met a man recovering from an FPV attack while selling fruit, and another who had lost both legs and needed three operations and 17 blood transfusions. He added that civilians across the river in Russian-controlled areas also faced drone threats, and that the use of drones created constant anxiety because they could track, observe and strike with little warning.

Protection, law and aid under fire

Fletcher said the core problem was not only the weapons themselves but the gap between rapidly advancing technology and the rules meant to restrain it. Drone operators, he said, appeared detached from their victims and from the law, yet the obligations under international humanitarian law remained unchanged: civilians and civilian objects must be protected, regardless of the weapon or the party using it.

He extended that warning beyond the battlefield. Fletcher said humanitarians were facing the same dangers and needed the same protection. Between January and July, he said, 10 humanitarian workers were killed in Ukraine and almost 40 were injured. At least 20 warehouses had been destroyed, erasing millions of dollars in aid, and UN convoys and evacuation vehicles had been struck while trying to save civilians. He said these attacks were not only harming individuals but also depriving the most vulnerable people of help.

The toll, he said, was not limited to the front line. Fletcher described a massive attack during his final night in Kyiv that hit homes, a school and a children’s hospital and killed at least 15 civilians. He also said there were reports of civilian casualties and infrastructure damage inside the Russian Federation.

What he asked the Council to do

Fletcher urged the Security Council to defend international humanitarian law, credibly investigate violations and hold perpetrators accountable. He called for concrete steps to protect humanitarian action, including systems for responding to strikes near convoys, better humanitarian notification, and temporary ceasefires for evacuations.

He also pressed for support to prepare for winter. The UN, he said, had launched a $358 million Winter Response Plan to provide heat, shelter and medical care to two million people, but this would require immediate procurement and prepositioning of supplies. He said Ukraine remained underfunded despite being better supported than other crises, and called for a stronger bridge to long-term recovery.

Fletcher ended by warning that as artificial intelligence and autonomy enter warfare, preserving human judgment in military decision-making is even more urgent. “Technology is now outpacing accountability,” he said, arguing that unless the rules of war are enforced, civilians will continue to pay the price.

The broader fight is also still being shaped by Ukraine’s shortage of interceptors. Burnham said allies need to keep searching their stocks for air-defence systems, while Kyiv continues to ask for more weapons to counter Russian missiles and drones.

What happened

The sanctions reach beyond Tehran

The Treasury Department said the package will hit Iran’s aviation, digital assets, gold, technology and shipping sectors, and will also sanction 60 specific individuals and vessels. Bessent said the targets are meant to cut off major sources of revenue, including oil and gas, while also pressuring Iran’s trade partners.

The department also said Iran has used cryptocurrency to bypass long-running sanctions and to facilitate transactions involving the Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps and members of the Iranian regime. It said the new shipping sanctions target Iran’s state-linked fleet, which it alleges is used to move oil and “sensitive weapons components,” while the aviation measures target Iranian airlines the department says are used to transport weapons, military personnel and financial resources to Iran’s proxies.

Washington also suspended several broad exceptions to its sanctions regime, including those covering academic exchanges, personal money transfers and some sporting activities.

Trade partners are now in the frame

Bessent said on Monday that the new sanctions expose Tehran’s trade partners to secondary penalties, and that every country would be given a defined timeline to shut down Iran-related business activity. According to a Treasury Department release, the targets include ships based in or linked to Singapore, China and Hong Kong.

That wider reach is what makes the announcement more than a simple Iran sanctions update. “Today’s sanctions are mostly incremental, but are part of trying to intimidate remaining trading partners into cutting ties [with Iran],” Rachel Ziemba, an adjunct senior fellow at the Center for a New American Security think tank, told Al Jazeera. Peiman Salehi, a Tehran-based geopolitical analyst, said Iran appears to have “much less room than it did in previous years to simply work around sanctions.”

The Trump administration is also signaling that it is prepared to keep turning the screw. Markets and consumers are already part of the story

The pressure campaign matters well beyond government and shipping circles because it is tied to oil flows and transport costs. John Deal, managing director of capital markets at Post Oak Group investment bank, told Al Jazeera that if sanctions provoke Iranian retaliation against Gulf shipping, reduce oil exports, or push insurers and shipping companies away from the region, Americans could feel it quickly through gasoline, diesel, airfares, freight costs and inflation.

The Treasury Department’s move came as Brent crude fell by more than 2% on Monday to $85.22 a barrel, while the average US price for a gallon of petrol stood at $4.09, up from $2.98 on February 28, according to the American Automobile Association.

Temporal confidence steers each branch

At the center of ParaTempo is a signal the authors call temporal confidence. Instead of looking only at a single probe or at token-level uncertainty, the system periodically probes each active branch for a tentative answer distribution, then aggregates recent probes in a sliding window. Temporal confidence is defined as the exponentiated negative entropy of that aggregated answer distribution, so it rises when recent probes consistently concentrate on one dominant answer and falls when probability mass stays spread across alternatives.

The control logic follows from that one signal. After a warmup phase, ParaTempo uses the observed temporal-confidence values to set a pruning threshold for the current problem. Branches below that threshold are removed. Branches that keep committing to the same dominant answer over several probes can be retired early, which stops them from consuming more generation tokens while preserving their answer and confidence weight for the final vote. When pruning frees up a slot, ParaTempo can fork a new branch from a promising donor branch, carrying forward its prefix but sampling independently. Global generation stops when the confidence-weighted vote across branches becomes sufficiently concentrated.

The authors emphasize that this is asynchronous: branches do not have to wait for one another to reach the same depth. They argue that avoiding synchronization barriers is part of why the method can reduce both total computation and critical-path latency.

Scientists injected mitochondria taken from a woman’s own leg muscles into the vitreous fluid of her eyes in an attempt to treat severe blindness, finding no inflammation or side effects but only a temporary improvement in light responses and no restoration of vision.

The woman had suffered a brain bleed in February that injured her optic nerve and retina, leaving her nearly blind and with no pupil responses to light. In May, the researchers obtained emergency approval from the US Food and Drug Administration to try the transplant, which they say was the first such procedure in the eyes. The idea was to see whether healthy mitochondria — the energy-producing organelles inside cells — could be taken up by damaged retinal cells and help them recover function.

That approach has been explored before in other parts of the body: previous human trials have put mitochondria into the heart and brain. David Putrino, a neuroscientist at the Icahn School of Medicine at Mount Sinai and a co-author of the preprint, said the goal was safety. “We can’t prove efficacy at all, nor are we trying to,” he said, though he added that they saw “a significant effect on physiological changes in the eye.”

After the injections, the woman’s pupils responded to light again, but the effect faded after about four weeks. Putrino said a post-transplant low-vision assessment suggested she was perceiving shapes and shadows in her left eye. Temurkhan Ayupov, a mitochondria biologist at the Institute of Molecular and Clinical Ophthalmology Basel, said the study shows the method was “relatively safe” but does not confirm a therapeutic effect.

The findings were posted as a preprint on 10 August and have not been peer reviewed.

What happened

An interdisciplinary team at the University of Göttingen has imaged the three-dimensional wavefunction of an organic molecule only nanometers in size, with findings published in *Nature Communications*. The work targets molecular orbitals, the electron wavefunctions inside molecules, which carry information about how a molecule behaves and interacts with its environment, including how it absorbs light and how chemical reactions may unfold.

Professor Stefan Mathias said the wavefunction is a fundamental quantity in quantum mechanics that cannot be directly observed or measured. To reconstruct it, the researchers used photoelectron spectroscopy, which measures the momentum of electrons emitted from the molecule, and then used advanced computer algorithms to recover the missing half of the wavefunction. The team said this produced an image of the complete molecular orbital and revealed features smaller than the distance separating the carbon atoms in the molecule. Dr. Matthijs Jansen said the group combined a redesigned algorithm that needs much less experimental data with a lab-based soft-X-ray light source that provides ultrashort light pulses, which they said could make three-dimensional wavefunction imaging more practical.

A ghostly ribbon of stars reveals hidden dark matter

What happened

Astronomers have identified the first stellar stream ever seen outside the Milky Way, a faint ribbon of stars in UGC 9050-Dw1 that may let them probe dark matter in a galaxy beyond our own, where such streams had long been predicted but remained too dim to find.

For billions of years, an ancient cluster of stars has been gradually coming apart, shedding stars that now trace a narrow arc across space. The international team included an astrophysicist from Northwestern University, and the study was co-led by Julie Kiel Holm of the University of Copenhagen and Sarah Pearson of the Technical University of Denmark. It was published Aug. 12 in Nature. Archival Hubble Space Telescope data played a key role: David Sand and Catherine Fielder, both astronomers at the University of Arizona, presented the observations, and David Hendel noticed the faint, narrow arc while examining images of the ultra-diffuse galaxy UGC 9050-Dw1.

The galaxy lies roughly 115 million light-years from Earth. Because it contains relatively few stars, it provided a dark enough background for the stream to stand out. Researchers then ran thousands of computer simulations, testing combinations of globular cluster characteristics and possible dark matter distributions to see which could recreate the stream’s observed shape. From that, they reconstructed the gravitational field of the host galaxy and estimated how unseen dark matter influenced the stars’ paths.

Tjitske Starkenburg of Northwestern, a coauthor of the study, said stars in a stellar stream travel along nearly the same orbit, and that orbit is shaped by the galaxy’s gravity. By modeling that gravity, the team can estimate the galaxy’s total mass. Since the amount of visible matter is already roughly known, the remainder must be dark matter. The results indicated that UGC 9050-Dw1 contains a large amount of dark matter, consistent with expectations for an ultra-diffuse galaxy.

The finding matters because it gives astronomers a new tool for studying one of astrophysics’ biggest unanswered questions. Dark matter makes up roughly 85% of all matter in the universe, but it cannot be seen directly. Starkenburg said thin stellar streams may be especially useful because small concentrations of dark matter can leave gaps or clumps in them. The team said that with more searches, similar streams around other galaxies could help reveal how dark matter is distributed across the universe.

Common medications may change your gut for years

What happened

Medications can influence the community of microbes in the human gut long after a person stops taking them, according to a large study led by researchers at the University of Tartu Institute of Genomics. The researchers analyzed stool samples and prescription records from more than 2,500 participants in the Estonian Biobank’s Estonian Microbiome cohort. They found that most of the medications examined were associated with differences in the gut microbiome, and for a substantial number of drugs those differences could still be detected years after use. The lasting effects were not limited to antibiotics; antidepressants, beta-blockers, proton pump inhibitors and benzodiazepines were also linked to distinctive microbial fingerprints.

Lead author Dr. Oliver Aasmets said past drug use can be as important as current medication in explaining individual microbiome differences. The study also found that benzodiazepines had associations comparable to broad-spectrum antibiotics, and that drugs in the same class did not always affect the microbiome the same way: diazepam and alprazolam differed in how strongly they appeared to disrupt gut microbes. In a smaller follow-up group, changes after starting or stopping certain medications were accompanied by predictable shifts in gut microbes, with persistent effects confirmed for proton pump inhibitors, selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors and antibiotics such as penicillins in combination and macrolides.
