Morocco Exhibits Its Maximum Strength as Guardian of Ceuta Border

What happened

Moroccan police arrested more than a hundred people on Saturday as crowds moved toward Ceuta, renewing pressure on the Spanish enclave just weeks after a huge July crossing left the border strained and raised fears of another mass attempt.

Footage posted online showed large crowds, most reportedly from sub-Saharan Africa, heading toward Ceuta. Moroccan authorities said earlier in the week that they were working to foil such attempts, and police reportedly used tear gas as the crowds advanced through northern Morocco.

The crowd moved through Fnideq and the mountains

The route described in the reports ran through the mountains and past Fnideq, a coastal town in northern Morocco. The people trying to reach Ceuta were believed to have chosen that path to avoid security checkpoints.

The arrest totals varied as the day went on. Reuters, citing a source in Morocco’s Ministry of Interior, said at least 111 people were arrested after hundreds of police were deployed. Later on Saturday afternoon, the local outlet Le360 said as many as 294 people had been arrested, including 46 Moroccans.

A repeat of July’s larger crossing

The new push came only weeks after a far larger movement toward Ceuta. El País said the crisis began on July 30, when an estimated 80,000 people crossed into the city from Morocco, pushing the enclave of a little over 83,000 inhabitants to the brink of collapse.

Al Jazeera put the late-July figure at approximately 72,000 people entering Ceuta and said at least 96 died trying to reach the enclave. It also said about 5,000 migrants remained in Ceuta on Thursday, according to Spain’s interior minister, Fernando Grande-Marlaska.

The latest attempt also followed what Al Jazeera described as a new disinformation campaign on social media encouraging people to flock to the city. Moroccan authorities had said they were trying to stop such attempts, and the Saturday deployment showed how aggressively they moved once crowds began forming.

Ceuta stayed under a heavy border deployment

El País said Ceuta had already been reinforced with an unprecedented police deployment after the July surge, including police, Civil Guard, military personnel, riot units, divers, drones, helicopters and boats. It said Morocco had also tightened control around the border, with more police and army personnel on the ground in areas facing Ceuta.

That broader security buildup framed the response on Saturday. El País said the Moroccan authorities detained 294 irregular migrants in Fnideq and surrounding areas by early afternoon and that most of them were later transported by bus to distant southern cities such as Ouarzazate, more than 800 kilometers from Ceuta.

Even with the heavier presence, the underlying question remained the same as after July: whether Morocco’s tightened controls would be enough to stop another large crossing, or whether the pressure on Ceuta would return again.

What happened

Israeli air strikes on southern Lebanon killed at least 11 people on Saturday, with one strike in Ansar and another in Deir al-Zahrani, as Lebanon and Israel traded blame over an attack that again strained a fragile truce.

State media and health officials said the dead included children, and Lebanese media reported that the intensity of the overnight bombardment forced many residents to leave their homes. Emergency crews were also seen transporting a body in Deir el Zahrani after the strike there.

Casualties spread across Nabatieh and Ansar

Lebanon’s health ministry and state news agency said an air strike hit a house on the edge of Ansar village, killing seven people and wounding two. They said a second strike in Deir al-Zahrani killed four and wounded 17. Earlier reports from state media said at least nine people had been killed and more than a dozen wounded in strikes on several towns and villages in southern Lebanon.

Lebanese media also reported artillery fire and other strikes in areas including Ali al-Taher Heights near Nabatieh al-Fawqa, Kfar Tebnit, Zawtar al-Sharqiya and other parts of southern Lebanon. Al Jazeera reported that children and members of one family were among those killed in the Ansar strike, and that the bombardment “deepened fears among residents” as Israeli strikes and demolitions continued across the south.

Israel says it hit Hezbollah sites; Lebanon rejects that account

The Israeli military said it struck “Hezbollah infrastructure” in the Nabatieh and Ansar areas, adding that the attacks came in response to “action against Israeli soldiers.” Israel’s prime minister’s office said Hezbollah had violated the ceasefire by attacking soldiers inside Lebanon and seriously wounding three, though the military later said the soldiers were hurt by an explosive drone.

Lebanese Prime Minister Nawaf Salam rejected Israel’s account and described the escalation in Nabatieh, Ansar, Deir al-Zahrani and surrounding villages as “extremely dangerous,” saying it was undermining efforts to stabilise the region. He said the seven people killed in the strike on Ansar were not military infrastructure and that the children and women killed in it were not military targets. Salam also said responsibility for dealing with any military infrastructure on Lebanese territory, “if it exists,” rests with the Lebanese state through the army and other state institutions.

Hezbollah said the attacks would be met with an “appropriate” response and urged Lebanese authorities not to continue what it called “humiliating” negotiations with Israel. Lebanese President Joseph Aoun also condemned the strikes, saying the escalation in Nabatieh and surrounding areas was a repeated violation of the framework meant to reduce hostilities and protect civilians. He said the attack on Ansar killed members of an entire family and called the escalation “a clear message” to the negotiation process and US-led efforts to implement the agreement.

A truce under strain

The strikes came despite a truce that went into effect on June 20 and a June 26 “framework agreement” between Israel and Lebanon that laid out a plan for Israeli forces to withdraw from southern Lebanon in exchange for the disarmament of Hezbollah. The deal envisions steps toward an eventual peace agreement, but Hezbollah has refused direct talks and was not party to it.

Disagreements over implementation have continued. The latest strikes were described as among the deadliest Israeli attacks in Lebanon since the June agreements led to a significant reduction in hostilities, but Saturday’s toll and the competing accounts of who was targeted showed how quickly that restraint remains under pressure.

What happened

The 7.4-magnitude earthquake that shook western Colombia on August 10 left Ana María Saavedra Caycedo as the only survivor of her immediate family, while the wider death toll remains reported in different ways, with hundreds killed and many more still missing.

A family pulled from the rubble

Ana María Saavedra Caycedo lay under the rubble for hours with arm injuries and a fractured pelvis after the quake struck while the family was in their apartment in Cali, one of the hardest-hit cities. Search and rescue efforts at the site began immediately and continued for days, as family and friends went into the streets to ask for help and volunteer in the rescue operations.

The triplets — Ana Maria, Sofia and Isabella Saavedra Caycedo — along with their father Jair Saavedra, their mother Vicky Caycedo and their uncle Diego Caycedo were all pulled from the debris in the hours and days that followed. The parents were found embracing in their final moments, and Sofia and Diego were found dead too. Isabella’s body was the last to be pulled from the rubble, the triplets’ cousin Camila Martínez Sierra said.

Martínez Sierra wrote on Instagram that her heart was broken by the loss of her uncle Jairo and Vicky, and her cousins Sofía and Isabella, and asked people to stay united and help Ana. A family-organized GoFundMe campaign for Ana Maria has already received thousands of donations, and the campaign page says she has undergone surgery and now faces a long recovery process.

The family’s fundraising page said the triplets had recently graduated from university and worked together. It described them as people with “dreams, plans, and a whole life ahead of them,” and said the aim was to give Ana María “a solid foundation to recover physically and emotionally” and to restart her life without worrying about how to pay for what comes next.

Martínez Sierra also shared photos and videos of family gatherings, trips, parties and moments they spent together. She said that looking at the images now carried “a heart full of memories and a mix of love, nostalgia, and a pain I still don’t know how to explain,” and added that the earthquake had changed their lives “in a matter of seconds.”

The wider toll remains in flux

The broader impact of the earthquake is also described with different figures in the supplied reports. The National Unit for Disaster Risk Management, or UNGRD, said the death toll had risen to 288 and that 202 people were still missing. It also said 145,601 people across 57,516 families in 448 municipalities across 15 of Colombia’s 32 departments had been affected.

In that same tally, the UNGRD reported 4,018 injured people and 354 rescued alive from the rubble. It said 80,744 houses had been damaged and 12,504 destroyed, along with 66 collapsed buildings, mainly in Cali and Pereira, the capitals of Valle del Cauca and Risaralda. The agency also reported damage to 2,595 educational facilities, 260 health centers, 267 roads, 87 water systems, 55 bridges and five airports.

Another report on the disaster said the Saavedra Caycedo family were among at least 294 people confirmed killed, with 320 missing and nearly 4,000 injured. It also said more than 12,000 homes had been destroyed and over 100 buildings leveled. Rescue workers, volunteers and social organizations continued to operate in affected areas as authorities updated their assessment of the disaster.

The quake has remained a story of both individual loss and unresolved accounting: a family in Cali has been shattered, while the full scale of deaths, missing people and damage is still being updated as crews continue to work through the rubble.

Powerful earthquake kills dozens in eastern Indonesia

What happened

A magnitude 7.7 earthquake on Flores island in eastern Indonesia killed at least 47 people, damaged hundreds of buildings and forced evacuations, while rescuers struggled to reach some areas because landslides, power cuts and communication disruptions slowed the search.

The quake struck shortly before 05:00 local time on Saturday, and Indonesia’s disaster mitigation agency said the death toll was still rising as search and rescue teams got to work. The National Disaster Management Agency, or BNPB, said several people were injured to varying degrees. Officials also said the quake was shallow, with a depth of 15km, which can make surface damage more severe.

Deaths and damage across Flores

The worst-hit areas were spread across Flores, with BNPB saying Manggarai province had 24 fatalities, East Manggarai 17, Sikka three, and at least one death each in Ngada, Ende and West Manggarai. Emanuel Melkiades Laka Lena, the governor of East Nusa Tenggara province, said some people were killed in their sleep when rubble collapsed on them.

Damage was widespread. BNPB said hundreds of buildings in the region had been damaged by the quake and the aftershocks that followed. Later reports said more than 150 houses were severely damaged, along with dozens of schools, healthcare facilities, places of worship, offices and other public buildings. A port video from Maumere showed large chunks of concrete breaking off a terminal building onto passengers waiting to board an inter-island ferry, and some people were thrown into the sea when a gangway collapsed.

In Maumere, rescuers found 20 people dead, six severely injured and two trapped under rubble, according to the city’s rescue agency. Landslides had also cut off roads in parts of the island, making some remote places hard to reach. The area around Nagekeo, close to the epicentre, was among the hardest to access.

Tsunami warning, evacuations and aftershocks

Authorities issued a tsunami warning after the initial quake, and people in coastal areas moved inland as the sea retreated. The warning was lifted about three hours later after monitoring showed no significant sea-level rise. In some areas, tsunami waves of up to 1.5 metres were recorded.

The earthquake set off a long series of aftershocks. Authorities recorded 235 of them, including one of magnitude 6.1, and another strong tremor was reported in North Sumatra in western Indonesia. BNPB said the Sumatra quake was measured at magnitude 6.4, while the US Geological Survey put it at 6.9. There were no initial reports of damage or casualties from that second quake.

The shaking also pushed many residents outside. BNPB said about 2,000 people had evacuated or relocated on their own, and others remained outdoors because they were afraid to return to damaged buildings. One resident in Talibura said he fled into the hills after waking to what he described as very strong shaking, and another in Ruteng said he dragged his stove outside and cooked there because of the tremors.

Rescue work slowed by blocked roads and disrupted services

Rescue officials said the main task was to find people who might be buried under rubble, but the work was made harder by blocked roads, landslides and disrupted communications. In some places, power cuts and telecommunications problems complicated the response further. The region’s remoteness and mountainous terrain also slowed the assessment of the full extent of the damage.

BNPB said aid was being sent to affected areas, and the International Federation of Red Cross and Red Crescent Societies said preparations were under way to help about 2,000 people in temporary shelters. The government also sent aid packages weighing a total of 275 tonnes to the area. Meanwhile, local television showed patients being evacuated from hospitals and temporary treatment facilities set up outside.

A second earthquake later struck Sumatra, thousands of kilometres away in western Indonesia, but there were no initial reports of casualties there. The focus in Flores remained on reaching cut-off communities, checking damaged buildings and accounting for the missing as aftershocks continued to shake the island.

When my dog died, my grief was overwhelming and complicated

What happened

When Greg James described losing his chocolate labrador Barney, it resonated with one writer’s own experience of putting her dog Lara to sleep, and her account shows how pet bereavement can bring shock, guilt and a sense that other people do not always understand the loss.

Lara died towards the end of June after being put to sleep. She had just turned 11, and her owner says she naively expected her to go on forever. But by the end of April Lara was sleeping more, losing her appetite and sometimes seeming unsure of where she was. As her quality of life declined, her owner slept on the sofa for two and a half months so Lara would not be alone.

Lara’s final weeks

In those last weeks, Lara was treated like a much-loved family member. She had dog treats, favourite foods, washes and blow dries at home, and plenty of attention. On the last day, she had ice-cream and lay in the sunshine on the grass in the garden before the trip to the vet. The last thing she heard before she died was that she was loved.

The writer says it was one of the most difficult days of her life. She cried and cried, and even moving Lara’s food bowl became painful. Although she believed putting Lara to sleep was the kindest decision, she says she still wrestles with that choice. Six weeks later, she says she still expects to see Lara at the front door and sometimes imagines hearing her drinking water or walking around the house.

Why pet grief can feel so complicated

That mix of love, guilt and daily absence is part of what makes pet bereavement so hard, through other people’s experiences too. Annalisa de Carteret, a pet loss support manager at Blue Cross, says it can be “quite lonely” because society does not always understand the impact. She says people often ask themselves what they could have done differently, especially when euthanasia is involved: whether they acted too soon or too late.

Dr Caroline Ficker, a palliative vet, says guilt is a natural response because owners are responsible for their animals’ treatment. Gemma Champ, a writer from north London, described feeling “ridiculous for making such a fuss” when her cat Frankie died at home in January, until friends responded with their own memories and grief. That reaction made her feel the loss was real and that people were learning not to dismiss a relationship she described as profound and long-lasting.

When grief meets work

The story also shows how practical life can collide with mourning. After Greg James’s dog died, he said he would not be on his show the next day. Emma McNulty, whose terrier Millie died in 2019, said she was too upset to go into work but lost her sandwich-shop job when she could not find cover for her shift. She later launched an online petition asking employers to recognise pet bereavement.

The piece says no country mandates paid time off after the death of a pet, even though some employers are becoming more flexible. Blue Cross says its free helpline, email and web chat service helped more than 30,000 people last year. For the writer, the main lesson is simpler: the grief may fade, but the bond does not disappear.

That figure followed an earlier estimate that almost 500 people had been arrested on suspicion of starting wildfires in France since the start of the summer, as firefighters faced ferocious blazes during a record-breaking heatwave.

One of the latest fires hit the village of Luglon in the south-west region of Landes, where French authorities on Friday evacuated more than 525 people after flames tore through almost 1,100 hectares of tinder-dry pine forest in less than 24 hours. Gilles Clavreul, a regional official, said: “The situation is unfavourable and the fire remains raging,” adding that 500 firefighters were tackling the flames and six water-dumping aircraft had been sent to assist. Roads to the village were closed.

Luglon is about 62 miles south-east of Arcachon Bay and the Lège-Cap-Ferret peninsula, where two huge wildfires destroyed more than 50,000 hectares in late July and forced the evacuation of as many as 220,000 people.

A continent-wide emergency from Croatia to Belgium

The French fires were part of a wider emergency across Europe. In a Croatian holiday resort on Friday, a fire engulfed homes, killing one person, injuring 40 and forcing 1,200 people to evacuate, officials said. In the coastal town of Lokva Rogoznica on the Dalmatian coast, a wildfire driven by strong winds descended overnight, and police said they found the body of one person while searching burned areas on Friday afternoon.

Emergency shelters opened in several nearby towns. Of 36 people treated by ambulance services in Split, 14 were admitted to hospital and seven had life-threatening injuries, local health officials said. Slavko Tucaković, the regional chief fire commander, said there had been “huge material damage,” while prime minister Andrej Plenković said the blaze was “one of the worst fires in recent years” but that there were no confirmed fatalities so far.

In western Germany, more than 2,000 people fled their homes early on Friday morning to take shelter in a primary school after a wildfire in the nearby Hürtgen forest spread close to the village of Gey. Mona Neubaur, the deputy premier of North Rhine-Westphalia, said on social media that the forest fire threatened to hit the local community hard. Unexploded second world war ordnance was also hampering firefighting efforts, and the news agency dpa reported explosions as munitions detonated in the flames. German army tanks and helicopters were providing support.

In Belgium’s Liège province, residents were asked to evacuate their homes on Saturday afternoon as the biggest fire on the country’s record burned through the High Fens, Belgium’s largest nature reserve. In Greece, stranded holidaymakers were picked up from beaches threatened by flames, and in Spain the army rescued historic relics after a wildfire threatened them.

Extreme heat and fire warnings spread with the flames

The fires came as three in five Europeans faced temperatures of at least 30C on Friday, and about 150 million endured 35C or more. The EU’s Copernicus climate change service warned in its weekly outlook of “very extreme” conditions across a large area of central and eastern Europe, with other pockets over southern Britain, Ireland, northern France and southern Sweden.

The average high across western Europe on Friday was forecast to be 31.2C, which was 8C above what was typical from 1961 to 1990, according to Reuters Climate Monitor data. The temperature for Europe as a whole was 1.6C above the 1961-1990 norm.

The result was a string of fast-moving fire emergencies in places already under strain. In north-east Spain, an army unit removed the remains of three 11th-century Aragonese kings from the San Juan de la Peña monastery after it was threatened by a wildfire and took them to a museum in Huesca. The fire, which began on Monday, intensified on Thursday and had burned through more than 9,000 hectares, forcing the evacuation of 16 towns and villages. A much larger wildfire in southern Spain also worsened on Thursday, burning 31,000 hectares in Huelva and forcing about 700 people to leave.

In northern Greece, about 500 holidaymakers were evacuated from beaches on Thursday as a forest fire threatened Siviri and Fourka on the Halkidiki peninsula. The fire front was later brought under control, though crews remained on alert for flare-ups, and two injured firefighters were taken to hospital.

What happened

A shallow magnitude-4.8 earthquake jolted Granada province at 1:04 a.m. on Saturday, waking residents across much of Andalusia and leaving people checking for cracks, aftershocks and building damage as officials assessed what had broken.

Fermín Rodríguez, 39, woke when the bed he shares with his wife began to shake. Before they could react, dishes, pictures and objects fell from shelves, and when they switched on the light they saw cracks in the walls. “We felt fear, a lot of fear,” he said. Thousands of people noticed the tremor, which was initially reported as magnitude 5 and was felt across almost all of Andalusia.

What people felt across Granada and beyond

The quake lasted only a few seconds, but it was enough to wake Granada and to be felt in nearby provinces including Málaga, Jaén and Almería, as well as farther away in Seville, Murcia, Cáceres, Ciudad Real, Albacete, Madrid and Toledo. Its epicenter was in Alhendín, a town of about 10,000 inhabitants in the Granada metropolitan area.

Other residents described the same sudden shock. Josefa Crespo, 64, a civil servant in La Alhambra, said, “Everything moved and items fell from the shelves.” Mariló Vílchez, who lives in La Zubia in a house with an anti-seismic structure, said it was still overwhelming because “the whole house vibrated.” She added that it made her feel “completely at the mercy of something you cannot control.”

Cristóbal Gallardo, who owns the El Mercader restaurant, said, “Luckily it was only a scare. But it was intense. I don’t remember one this strong.” José Morales, a seismologist at the Andalusian Institute of Geophysics, also felt it in La Herradura, on the Tropical Coast, and said there are not many recent precedents of similar magnitude. He noted that accelerations were recorded that had not been measured before, except for exceptions like the Lorca earthquake in 2011.

Damage, inspections and official response

According to the Andalusia government, the quake caused structural damage to many buildings, including cornices, rubble and walls, but no casualties. The regional government activated the emergency plan and convened the Committee for Seismic Risk Assessments to evaluate the situation.

Firefighters and police worked to inspect damage and cordon off affected areas. Granada City Council said the reported incidents were mainly “minor” non-structural damage and did not pose a risk to people’s physical integrity. Municipal sources said the main inspection requests came from the south of the city and from Ogíjares, La Zubia and Armilla. The city also sent urban planning technicians to review some buildings in the Zaidín district after numerous calls from that area.

Several religious buildings were among the places affected. The Royal Federation of Brotherhoods and Cofradías of Holy Week said five religious temples suffered damage. The bell tower of San Miguel Church in Albaicín was affected, and in La Magdalena, in the historic center, some decorative elements fell, as did elements in the Basilica of Nuestra Señora de las Angustias, which was closed to the public because of the damage.

Granada’s best-known monument, the Alhambra, did not suffer damage. Its patronage said inspections by specialized teams and the fire department found no relevant problems in the palaces, walls, historic structures or landscaped areas, though visitor access was delayed until 11:00 once safety was confirmed.

Aftershocks kept people awake

The first shock was only the beginning of a long night. Hundreds of people went outside, nervous and scared, and stayed awake in squares, parks and open spaces while aftershocks continued. Rodríguez said later that they had not been able to sleep because each new tremor revived fears of another strong quake.

There were many aftershocks, though their frequency decreased over the day. The IGN recorded almost fifty in metropolitan areas such as Ogíjares, La Zubia, Dílar, Armilla, Alhendín and Gójar, where the largest aftershock reached magnitude 3.3. Juan Vicente Cantavella, director of the National Seismic Network, said the sequence now numbers around one hundred earthquakes, 13 of them felt by the population. More than 10,000 people reported feeling the quakes to the agency, and the Andalucía Emergency Service 112 received nearly 500 calls from frightened citizens.

The tremor also prompted fresh precautions. Antonio Sanz activated Operational Situation 1 to strengthen monitoring and coordinate resources with the provincial government and affected municipalities, and the Andalusian administration said it would use the Group of Volunteer Architects of Andalusia to help assess buildings. Officials also said they remained in permanent contact with the Andalusian Institute of Geophysics and the Spanish National Research Council as the seismic activity continued to be monitored.

The study proposes the Real Estate Enterprise Performance Evaluation Method (RE2PRM) for China’s real estate industry, using Decision-Making Trial and Evaluation Laboratory (DEMATEL) to address limits in traditional performance evaluation. It says the real estate sector has significantly influenced China’s economic development in recent decades, and that digital transformation has become more important for real estate firms because it can improve operational efficiency, customer experience, and competitive edge. RE2PRM evaluates financial performance, customer satisfaction, and market trends through a systematic index framework and causal relationship analysis.

Empirical findings from 500 survey respondents across China’s real estate sector found that RE2PRM achieved superior accuracy, user acceptance, and practicality compared with existing digital transformation methods. The study also reports that artificial intelligence, at 84%, and blockchain, at 82%, had the highest influential power among digital technologies, while big data analytics, at 61%, and AI, at 65%, showed the greatest relative importance.
