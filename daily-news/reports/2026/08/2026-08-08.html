What happened

Simon Levy’s conviction for murdering two women and raping another has triggered scrutiny of how police, prosecutors and courts handled a man who was already on bail and kept attacking women in London.

The convictions and the victims

A jury at the Old Bailey found Levy guilty on Friday of murdering Carmenza Valencia-Trujillo, 53, and Sheryl Wilkins, 39, in London last year, and of violently raping another woman in a car park. He was remanded into custody ahead of sentencing on 12 August.

The case also laid out a wider pattern of sexual violence. Levy attacked the three women while on bail for sex assaults on trains in London, and went on to carry out further assaults on public transport. By the time of the murders, he had already been convicted in February of sexual assaults on women in public transport incidents.

How Levy stayed free

Levy had first come to police attention in 2017, when a woman reported him for sexually assaulting her at an event in south London. In 2021, he received a three-year prison sentence for sexually assaulting two women in separate attacks at public events in London in summer 2018.

When he was first released on licence in 2023, he was classed as a high-risk sex offender. He was briefly returned to prison in May 2024 after being accused of rape and breaching his licence conditions. When he was finally released in August 2024, the Metropolitan Police took over responsibility for managing his risk and immediately downgraded him from high-risk to medium-risk.

The Met had already downgraded him as a risk to women before the attacks that followed. Levy was being monitored and risk-managed by the force while he continued to offend. BBC reporting said he was also freed on bail multiple times between late 2024 and summer 2025, even after charges and breaches of bail conditions.

Police, prosecutors and courts under pressure

The failures were not limited to one agency. In the same period, Levy kept moving through the system while continuing to attack women. The Met told the BBC it accepts that Wilkins’s life could have been saved, and the force said it had apologised to her family.

Deputy Assistant Commissioner Kevin Southworth said there had been a “collective system failure” and described the case as a “tragedy beyond words”. He also said there were opportunities to save some of the victims. Det Ch Supt Pete Fulton of British Transport Police said delays in his force’s enquiries were “absolutely wasn’t good enough” and were down to “incompetence” by two officers who have since been moved to other roles.

The Crown Prosecution Service also said its handling fell short. Lisa Ramsarran, the chief crown prosecutor, said: “Our actions fell short of the standards that victims, families and the public are entitled to expect.” She said there were points where prosecutors could and should have taken a more robust approach to bail.

Watchdog investigation and continuing questions.

Two Metropolitan Police officers are now being investigated by the Independent Office of Police Conduct over their handling of Levy. The watchdog said a police constable is under a gross misconduct investigation and a detective sergeant is under a misconduct investigation.

The IOPC said its inquiry began in January after a voluntary conduct referral from the Met and is still ongoing. It added that misconduct notices do not automatically lead to disciplinary proceedings.

The case has also drawn attention because Levy’s violence unfolded over years: on London public transport, in a car park, and while he was already known to police as a serial offender. One woman who was assaulted on public transport said learning that he had been on bail was worse than the assault itself, and said the attacks could have been prevented.

What happened

South Korea’s heat wave is expected to ease only slightly over the weekend, with rain reaching the east coast but western regions still forecast to stay sweltering. Seoul has already hit 40 C for the first time in eight years, and the bigger question is whether the showers will bring relief or just more humidity.

Rain moves in, but the heat does not fully break

Friday’s temperatures were still climbing toward 39 C in Seoul and Jeonju, with Incheon and Gwangju forecast to reach 38 C and Daejeon and Daegu 37 C. By 8 a.m., several major cities had already risen to around 30 C after another tropical night, and Friday — Ipchu, traditionally seen as the beginning of autumn — could become the hottest Ipchu ever recorded in Seoul. The previous record was 35.9 C in 2018.

The Korea Meteorological Administration has kept its highest-level heat warnings in effect for Seoul, parts of Gyeonggi Province, inland Gangwon Province and some southwestern areas. That warning reflects what the forecast suggests for the weekend: temperatures may dip a little, but there will be no real break from the heat.

Saturday should bring some clouds, with daytime highs still around 35 C in many places. Rain is expected before dawn along the east coast of Gangwon Province and in nearby mountain areas before spreading to parts of the North Gyeongsang coast and Jeju. Scattered afternoon showers of 5 to 40 mm are also forecast across Seoul, Incheon, Gyeonggi Province and inland areas nationwide.

Heavy rain on the east coast, sweltering air in the west

Sunday’s rain is expected to be concentrated along the east coast, in parts of the southeast and on Jeju Island. The Gangwon coast and mountain areas could receive 30 to 100 mm from Saturday through Sunday, with more than 150 mm possible in some locations. Northern coastal areas of North Gyeongsang Province may get 30 to 80 mm, while 20 to 60 mm is expected farther south.

That rain could bring flooding, landslides and swollen streams. It may also briefly cool conditions, but the forecast says the air could feel just as uncomfortable afterward because of higher humidity.

Western Korea is set to remain hot. Easterly winds crossing the Taebaek Mountains are expected to turn warmer and drier, keeping apparent temperatures near 35 C. Tropical nights are also expected to continue through the weekend, meaning overnight lows will stay above 25 C in many areas.

Seoul’s 40-degree reading and the growing toll

Seoul’s heat has already reached a new level. An automatic weather station in Nowon-gu, in northeastern Seoul, recorded 40.2 C at around 3:30 p.m. on Friday, according to the Korea Meteorological Administration. That was the first reading above 40 C at a Seoul observation point since Aug. 1, 2018, when Gangbuk-gu recorded 41.8 C.

The figure from Nowon-gu is not Seoul’s official temperature, which is measured at the weather station in Jongno-gu. That official reading reached 38 C on Friday, while the city’s record official high remains 39.6 C, set in 2018. Temperatures were even higher elsewhere in the country, with Hanam in Gyeonggi Province reaching 40.7 C, Gwangyang in South Jeolla Province hitting 40.2 C and Yeongwol in Gangwon Province reaching 39.8 C.

The prolonged heat has also taken a health toll. A total of 208 heat-related illness cases were reported Wednesday, bringing the cumulative number to 2,665, including 23 deaths, according to the Central Disaster and Safety Countermeasures Headquarters.

For now, the forecast points to only modest easing: rain in the east, lingering heat in the west, and another weekend of tropical nights for much of the country.

What happened

Iran’s attack on a U.S. military base in Jordan, which killed three American service members, was followed by an unusual Iranian outreach to Jordanians that paired threats, flattery, and blame-shifting as Tehran tried to recast itself in Arab eyes.

A strike, then an appeal

In late July, Iran struck a U.S. military base in Jordan and then the Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps issued a series of messages to the Jordanian people. “You know well that we have no hostility toward your country,” the IRGC said in a statement published on Iran’s Fars News Agency. “Rather, we love you, noble people who understand the suffering and injustice endured by the Palestinian people more than any other nation.”

A second message thanked unnamed Jordanians for providing targeting information for the Iranian strikes. A third appeared to answer Jordanian criticism of the operation by saying the United States, not Iran, had “violated your territorial integrity and sovereignty.” The turn from bombardment to affection was striking enough to stand out on its own, and it came after Iran had already shown it was willing to hit U.S.-linked targets across the region.

Iran has demonstrated that willingness through missile strikes on seven different Arab countries that host U.S. bases. At least three of those strikes were aimed at Jordan, including a surprise attack on July 28. The new outreach, then, was not a replacement for force so much as a parallel message: pressure first, persuasion second.

Why Jordan mattered

Tehran’s appeal to Jordanians fits a broader effort to convince Arab publics that an alliance with the United States is not worth the cost. That campaign, the evidence says, has been under way since the Islamic Republic’s founding in 1979, but the current push reflects what Iranian officials call a “new equation of power” in the Middle East, including Iranian control over the Strait of Hormuz.

Jordan is an unusual place for that campaign. Unlike Iraq, Lebanon, Bahrain, Kuwait, and Saudi Arabia, it has no significant Shiite minority that could serve as a social base for Iranian influence. But the calculation is not purely social. The evidence says Jordan houses radar and anti-missile systems that help Israel detect and deter incoming Iranian fire, while Jordan lacks the military capacity to retaliate against Iranian strikes.

Tehran also seems to be looking for a country where it can exploit tensions inside society. Jordan depends on the protection and patronage of the United States, yet some Jordanians have recently called for the expulsion of the U.S. military, a movement fueled in part by deep sympathy for the Palestinian cause. Some 60 percent of Jordan’s population is of Palestinian origin, and since the start of the Gaza war in 2023, street protests have regularly erupted in Jordan.

A split audience in Jordan

Those protests were supported by Jordan’s king and queen and by prominent government officials, whether out of conviction or because the public needed to let off steam. Queen Rania gave an interview to Christiane Amanpour two weeks after the Hamas massacre on October 7 in which she described Israeli actions as a “genocide,” a gesture that was widely seen in Jordan and Israel as setting a new tone of fury over Gaza.

At the same time, Jordanian demonstrators did more than protest. They pledged support for Hamas, and Islamist networks in Jordan began gaining strength. That helped lead the government to ban the local chapter of the Muslim Brotherhood last year. In late 2024, two Jordanians who were members of the Brotherhood carried out two cross-border terrorist attacks in Israel.

The Palestinian question has also shaped Jordan’s view of the wider conflict. Violence in Gaza has ebbed since a cease-fire took hold late last year, but Israel’s gradual, de facto annexation of the West Bank has become a deep concern in Jordan. The prospect of a fuller or even formal annexation raises fears in Jordan of a flood of Palestinian refugees that could upset the country’s fragile demographic balance and amplify anger at Israel.

For now, Iran’s effort to win over Jordanians looks more like a test than a breakthrough. A Jordanian security expert and columnist, Amer al-Sabaileh, said, “The Iranians believe they know what’s going on in Jordan. This could be a multilayered strategy—missiles, then propaganda, then maybe activating cells.” But the evidence also shows resistance to that pitch: Jordanian officials have taken a firm line against “Iranian aggression,” and columnist Hussein al-Rawasdeh wrote that “Jordan is subjected to Iranian aggression, full stop.”

The broader Arab audience may be even harder to sway. Michael Robbins, the director of the Arab Barometer, said recent polling in Arab countries suggests sympathy Iran gained earlier in the war evaporated after Tehran’s recent airstrikes on Arab countries. Ahmad Sharawi, a senior analyst at the Foundation for the Defense of Democracies, said Arab states face limited options and that their main response will remain “diplomatic condemnations.”

Farewell Estelle Ellis, who chased broken lifts, broken hospitals and broken promises

What happened

Estelle Ellis, the Eastern Cape reporter who made a career out of chasing broken lifts, broken hospitals and broken promises, died suddenly at 51, leaving colleagues and the families she wrote about with a sharp sense of loss after years of hard-edged reporting.

Ellis died on Wednesday evening, 5 August 2026, while she was with her parents in Jeffreys Bay. She would have turned 52 on 19 October. Four days earlier, on 1 August, she filed what turned out to be her last Daily Maverick story, about the Organisation Undoing Tax Abuse, or Outa, asking the high court for an urgent interdict to stop the rollout of the new Administrative Adjudication of Road Traffic Offences system, known as Aarto.

A life built in courtrooms and hospital corridors

Ellis grew up in Somerset West, went to primary school there and matriculated from Hottentots-Holland High School before studying at Stellenbosch University, where she earned a law degree, an honours degree in journalism and a diploma in future studies. She later completed further studies at the Stellenbosch University Business School.

That mix of law and reporting shaped the way she worked. She began as a court reporter at Die Burger, then moved to the Cape Argus, The Star and The Herald in what was then Port Elizabeth, now Gqeberha. In the Western Cape Division of the High Court, colleagues remember a reporter who was first to the printer, first to the affidavits and often better briefed than the lawyers in the room. She was also, by several accounts, generous with her copies and with her time.

Her legal training never left her, and her reporting did not stop at taking notes from the sidelines. When she could not get clear answers about broken lifts at a government hospital, she tested them herself. On one occasion, a lift stopped between floors with Ellis inside, trapped with a hospital porter and a corpse. That was the kind of reporting she became known for: she wanted to see the thing, not just hear about it.

Reporting on the people behind the numbers

Ellis eventually returned to the Eastern Cape, where she built a reputation as a persistent reporter on health, courts and municipal failures. She joined Daily Maverick in 2019 and ran its Eastern Cape coverage from a bureau she helped build almost from nothing. Colleagues said her work often began with a tip-off or complaint and ended as a major investigation because she kept asking what was really happening and who was responsible.

Her reporting on paediatric oncology drug shortages in the Eastern Cape, malnutrition, staffing shortages in the neonatal ward at Dora Nginza Hospital, the closure of frail care facilities and clinics, and the provincial medical scooter programme all followed the same pattern: she refused to move on until the people affected were seen and the failures were named. During the Covid-19 pandemic, her coverage recorded more than 300 deaths and neonatal staffing shortfalls in Eastern Cape hospitals.

She also helped launch Baywatch in 2019 with Angela Daniels, a Daily Maverick bureau focused on Nelson Mandela Bay. The project turned local problems in water infrastructure and municipal finance into national news. Ellis wrote that it had always been her dream to build a fully fledged bureau in the metro, and thanked colleagues for helping bring it to life.

Her work won multiple awards, including four Discovery Health Journalist Awards, the Discovery Community Builder of the Year Award, several Vodacom Journalist of the Year Awards and several Standard Bank Sikuvile Awards. But people who worked with her said she cared more about accuracy than speed, and more about getting it right than being first.

The person behind the bylines

Ellis’ colleagues and friends describe a reporter who was exacting, funny and deeply loyal. Angela Daniels said Ellis cared so much that she could not simply let things slide, while Karyn Maughan said Ellis took her to court as a young reporter and taught her to keep asking questions until she understood the story properly. Journalist Biénne Huisman remembered her as brave, bold and deeply empathetic, with a love of home life that included updates about her ginger cat, Finn.

She was also involved beyond the newsroom. Ellis served on the executive committee of Siyavuka for Kids, worked on efforts to establish a dedicated children’s hospital for the Eastern Cape, and stayed active in hunger relief work. She described her own mission around child nutrition as making sure children had enough to eat during the first thousand days of life.

Daily Maverick management called her death a devastating loss and said she told stories of the Eastern Cape “that almost nobody else was telling: the hospitals, the courts, the water crisis, the people the state forgot.” She is survived by her parents, Kobus and Engela Ellis, her sisters Tersia Ellis and Rene Hedderwick, her brother-in-law Richard Hedderwick, and her niece and nephew, Leah and Jacques.

For colleagues, the loss is not just of a skilled journalist but of someone who walked younger reporters into court, shared documents, asked the extra questions and kept going when officials wanted the story to stop. The work she left behind was built on that refusal to look away.

How Russian ballistic missiles are exploiting holes in Ukraine’s air defenses

What happened

Russia’s latest ballistic-missile barrages are exposing a gap in Ukraine’s air defenses, with high-speed strikes reaching cities before warning systems can help much and Kyiv still short of the US-made Patriot interceptors that can stop them.

On one night this week, more than two dozen ballistic and anti-ship missiles hit the Ukrainian capital region, and all of them slipped past Ukrainian defenses, killing at least 17 people and wounding dozens more. A separate attack on the night of June 30, concentrated on the Kyiv and Lviv regions included four anti-ship missiles, nine ballistic missiles, 61 cruise missiles and close to 300 strike drones, according to the Ukrainian Air Force.

That mix matters because Russia’s air campaign is not just one kind of weapon. Ukraine has also been hitting Russia’s oil refining capacity, causing long lines at fuel stations and setting commercial logistics centers alight, but Moscow has kept up long-range attacks on Ukrainian cities. The strikes that worry Ukrainian leaders most appear to be the ballistic ones, which can arrive with little or no warning.

Why ballistic missiles are so hard to stop

Ballistic missiles are different from the cruise missiles and drones Ukraine has spent much of the war learning to counter. Drones are relatively cheap and plentiful, and Ukraine has built a layered defense against the slower ones. Cruise missiles can fly close to the ground to avoid detection. Ballistic missiles, by contrast, boost upward on rocket power, follow a high arc, and then plunge toward their target at extreme speed.

The short-range Russian Iskander ballistic missile can fly at six to seven times the speed of sound and carry up to 700 kilograms of explosives. Russia has also fired the Oreshnik missile, an intermediate-range hypersonic weapon, on a handful of occasions, and Ukrainian officials say Russian forces have repurposed S-400 air-defense missiles as ballistic missiles to hit Ukrainian targets.

The difficulty for defenders is not new in warfare, but the tools to counter it have advanced. Nazi Germany’s V-2 rocket and the Scud missiles used in the Iran-Iraq war showed how such weapons can hit civilian areas. Today, the Patriot’s PAC-3 interceptor is one of the few systems that can knock down a ballistic missile, steering directly into its target and destroying it with kinetic energy — in other words, a bullet hitting a bullet.

Ukraine’s interceptor gap

Russia’s recent barrages are landing as Kyiv tries to replenish its supply of Patriot interceptors. Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelensky said in a statement Wednesday that ballistic-missile interceptors could have saved the lives of those killed in the latest attack. He said delays in supplying them, or a refusal to provide anti-ballistic systems, “lead directly to such horrific casualties and destruction.”

Zelensky also said partners that cannot yet send interceptors can help by imposing new sanctions, arguing that a significant share of Russia’s ballistic-missile production is still not under sanctions.

The shortage is shaping how Ukraine thinks about the wider air war. The country’s cities face three broad threats — ballistic missiles, cruise missiles and drones — and Russian attacks often combine all three. In recent strikes, air-raid sirens have sounded at the same time as impacts, underscoring how little time some weapons leave for civilians and air defenses.

What Kyiv is trying to do next

Kyiv has been pressing Washington to co-produce the Patriot system in an effort to rebuild its stock of interceptors. US President Donald Trump signaled on the sidelines of the NATO summit last month in Ankara that he might be willing to license Ukrainian co-production of the Patriot, but he later said, “We’re talking about it, but it’s a hard thing to give away that kind of technology.”

Ukraine’s concern is not only the pace of Russian attacks but also the shape of the threat. Russian short-range systems such as the Iskander have become the workhorse of the campaign, and Ukrainian officials say Russia is also augmenting its arsenal with North Korean ballistic missiles. In one cited case, a North Korean KN-23 missile — described as a counterpart to the Russian Iskander — demolished a house in Radushne in rural Dnipropetrovsk region, killing six people, including three children, with five more children still missing after the attack.

As Russia continues building its missile campaign, the practical problem for Ukraine is whether its air defenses can catch up fast enough. The latest attacks show that when ballistic missiles get through, the cost can be immediate and severe.

An international research group studied a 10-month overwintering mission at Concordia Station in Antarctica, tracking 12 crew members with questionnaires at four points during the period and wearable sensors that measured when people were physically close to teammates and how long those encounters lasted. The study was led by Jan Schmutz, a professor in the Department of Psychology at the University of Zurich, and Andrea Cantisani, a psychiatrist and research associate at the University of Bern. Concordia Station’s extreme isolation and winter temperatures as low as minus 80 °C (-112 °F) made it a stand-in for future extended missions to the Moon or Mars.

The researchers found that spending more time near other crew members was not consistently linked to better social outcomes. Participants with more frequent contact were also more likely to report increased conflict, greater mistrust, and lower performance. The crew gradually split into smaller subgroups over time, often spending more time with colleagues who shared their language or nationality. The researchers cautioned that the study showed correlations and could not establish cause and effect.

Scientists discover a hidden brain shift that begins around age 50

What happened

Researchers from the University of California, San Diego, the New York Genome Center and the University of California, Irvine found a major shift in the immune environment of the hippocampus, using NIH-funded research on postmortem tissue from 40 neurologically healthy adults ages 20 to 95. The team reported that the change appears to begin in midlife: microglia, the brain’s main immune cells, gradually decline from about age 50 to age 75 and appear to be replaced by cells with stronger inflammatory signals and traits similar to immune cells from peripheral blood. The finding challenges the long-standing assumption that microglia simply remain in the brain and renew themselves throughout life.

To reach that result, the researchers combined standard gene-activity measurements with methods that map the genome’s 3D structure and chemical modifications, or epigenome. First author Nathan Zemke said epigenetic signatures can show where a cell came from, not just what it is doing now. The study also found signs of age-related decline in cells that help maintain the blood-brain barrier, and widespread coordinated changes in genome organization across many kinds of brain cells.

The key to colonizing Mars may be hidden inside asteroids

What happened

Researchers at EPFL in Switzerland have calculated how asteroid mining could supply metals directly to Mars. Their focus is the supply problem facing a Mars colony: beyond food, water, and oxygen, a settlement would need large amounts of metal for habitats, machinery, and tools, plus replacement parts as equipment wears down. The source argues that regularly shipping all of that from Earth would not be a practical long-term solution, and says the researchers examined whether asteroids could help build a system to deliver those materials instead.

The team used a computer program to test thousands of supply-chain combinations, including the energy needed to travel between Mars and different asteroids, how much metal could be collected, and the fuel required for the trip. They identified some M type asteroids as sources of iron, nickel, and other useful metals, and included carbonaceous asteroids that could provide carbon and water ice to make rocket propellant in space. The study says the results point to specific asteroids that could be reached with existing spacecraft technology, but that success would depend on choosing the right target.
