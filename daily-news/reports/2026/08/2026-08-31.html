Devastating Factors in Chocó Humanitarian Crisis After Earthquake

What happened

In Chocó, armed violence, the August 10 earthquake and mining have combined into a humanitarian crisis that has confined about 3,000 people, displaced 652 families and left aid delivery stalled in rural and ethnic territories that remain hard to reach.

The earthquake was a magnitude 7.4 event with its epicenter in San José del Palmar, and the crisis has since deepened across Colombia’s Pacific department. The Office of the People’s Defender, the Defensoría del Pueblo, issued a public statement on August 29 urging national and local authorities to activate emergency response protocols immediately, saying the emergencies are hitting Afro-descendant, Indigenous and rural communities especially hard.

Violence after the quake

The Defensoría said security has deteriorated progressively since the earthquake, especially in the San Juan subregion. It said the Clan del Golfo has maintained a prolonged presence in communities such as San Agustín, in Sipí, blocking traditional production activities and moving toward Puerto Murillo in Middle San Juan.

It also said fighting between the Clan del Golfo and the National Liberation Army, the ELN, has been verified in territories of the General Community Council of San Juan, known as Acadesan, and in rural areas of Nóvita and Sipí. The confrontations have included drone attacks and explosive devices, with civilian casualties and mass displacement.

One of the sharpest flare-ups came on August 23, when fighting and drone attacks in Charco Hondo, Sipí, left three civilians injured and forced 65 families to flee to Barrancón. Preliminary reports cited by the Defensoría indicate that 652 families, about 3,000 people, remain confined in places including Barrancón, Charco Hondo, Charco Largo in Sipí, and Torrá, Cajón and Santa Bárbara in Nóvita.

Mining pressure and isolation

The crisis is not only about combat. The evidence also points to illegal mining with heavy machinery and mining tables advancing in sectors of Lloró, Bagadó and the Noanamá–Cucurrupí corridor. That activity is said to be affecting 18 communities, including 11 Afro-descendant and 7 Indigenous communities, for a total of about 3,800 people, or 920 families.

The environmental damage is also severe. The same evidence says the mining is causing deforestation, river contamination and biodiversity loss, while becoming another arena of dispute among armed groups that tax operations, extort miners and fight for control of extraction sites. The UN Environment Programme has documented that illegal mining in Chocó is responsible for 80% of the department’s deforestation, with mercury contamination affecting fish stocks that communities depend on for food and income.

The earthquake has made all of this harder to respond to. The lack of a definitive census after August 10 has prevented authorities from accurately assessing material damage, and many rural areas remain isolated. That has made it impossible to send and deliver basic humanitarian aid in some places, the evidence says, while logistical bottlenecks continue to delay assistance.

Aid gaps and a disputed picture

The National Unit for Disaster Risk Management, UNGRD, reported that 406,963 people in 196,614 families have been affected across 15 departments and 496 municipalities, with 331 deaths, 152 missing, 4,449 injured and 364 rescued as of August 28. In Chocó, the combination of seismic damage, armed violence and geographic isolation has left thousands without food, water, shelter or medical care, according to the evidence.

The Defensoría called on the Clan del Golfo and the ELN to comply with International Humanitarian Law and stop actions that endanger civilians. It also urged the national government, the Chocó Governor’s Office and UNGRD to activate humanitarian response protocols, guarantee safe mobility, provide food, health care and temporary shelter, and urgently complete the census of victims in isolated rural areas.

There is also a split between official and community accounts of the response. Chocó Governor Nubia Carolina Córdoba said on August 23 that the first phase of humanitarian aid delivery had reached 100% of affected municipalities. Community leaders dispute that claim, saying many rural and ethnic territories remain unreachable because of armed violence, damaged infrastructure and a lack of transportation.

What is Burnham doing to the early prison release scheme and why?

What happened

Andy Burnham has tightened England and Wales’s next early prison release scheme by blocking people convicted of unlawful killing from early release, a move meant to answer backlash over PC Andrew Harper’s killers while leaving the prison system under severe crowding pressure.

The change means some prisoners who would have been eligible from 1 October will now stay inside longer. Burnham said on Sunday that anyone convicted of unlawful killing will be excluded, and he conceded the decision means “hundreds” more people will remain in prison as prisons in England and Wales are already more than 97% full.

What Burnham has changed

The scheme itself is part of a sentencing change that means some adults serving standard prison sentences will be released earlier than originally planned. For prisoners on shorter determinate sentences, that can mean release after a third of their sentence instead of 40% or 50%; for another group, release moves from two-thirds to halfway through the term. Release does not end the sentence: people serve the rest in the community on licence, under probation conditions, and can be sent back to prison if they breach them.

Burnham’s Sunday announcement narrows who can benefit. He said people convicted of unlawful killing will be excluded, meaning the killers of PC Andrew Harper will not be eligible. The Guardian said the bar also covers historical grooming offences, any form of manslaughter, causing death by dangerous driving and causing or allowing the death of a child. The result, that paper said, is a reduction in the number due to be released early from up to 6,000 to about 4,600.

Why he did it

The immediate trigger was the anger over the prospect that Harper’s killers could have been included in the scheme. Harper died in 2019 after being dragged by a car while responding to a quad bike theft in Berkshire, and more than one million people signed a petition calling for his killers to remain behind bars. Burnham said he had heard what Harper’s family were saying, adding that nobody would want a situation where people guilty of killing a police officer were let out early.

But the change is also happening against a wider capacity crisis. Burnham said he had pushed the prison system “to the very limits of what’s possible” without risking collapse, and prison governors said the estate is already under acute strain. One governor in the Midlands described the repeated return of released prisoners as a “revolving door” and said, “The churn is what stresses me out.” Another governor in the north of England said the plan was “all short-term solutions and we need space now.”

Those governors said they are seeing the same inmates coming back after reoffending or breaching release conditions. One said they were frustrated by the rate of recalls, adding: “We see the same people coming back after being released early and reoffending again, or breaking their [release] rules.”

The pressure he is trying to relieve

The government says it needs the release scheme to make room because prisons are close to full. The BBC reported that by March this year more than 70,000 people had already been released under the 2024 early release scheme, yet there were still almost 87,000 people in prisons across England and Wales and just over 2,000 spaces remaining.

Burnham and the government have also pointed to other ways of creating space, including deporting foreign inmates and releasing some prisoners serving indefinite sentences, but the BBC said those steps are unlikely to create much room by the time the first batch of inmates are released in October. Burnham also said he would end imprisonment for public protection sentences during this parliament, but prison experts said that would take time and would not ease pressure quickly.

Governors warned that overcrowding is already affecting safety and management inside prisons. One said adding cells in existing prisons would require more staff, and staff shortages mean “prisoners are not living in a safe and decent environment.” Another said the lack of space is also driving gang tensions, with prisoners from different areas being held together in ways that can raise threat levels.

What happens next.

The justice secretary is expected to give further details on Tuesday, including how many people will be affected. The first batch of prisoners is due to be released early in October, but governors and prison leaders say the system still faces a serious squeeze before then.

What happened

Hundreds of Moroccans are waiting on the sidewalks of Ceuta for entry into the Loma Colmenar migrant reception area, while residents say the flow keeps growing and the queue outside the gate has become part of daily life.

The queue outside Loma Colmenar

Aldellah Zahar, 21, from Marrakech, smiles and flashes a victory sign even while lying on a sidewalk with a bandaged leg. When another young Moroccan reproaches him for showing energy, he replies: “Why shouldn’t I smile, if I’m in Spain?”

Zahar is among the hundreds of Moroccans who have gathered on the streets of this area in the last week, hoping that getting into the facility will improve their lives and help them stay in Spain. His friend Otman, 18, from Agadir, is waiting there too. When the possibility of expulsion comes up, Zahar says: “As for me, as for those of us here, we’d rather die than go back to Morocco.” Otman adds: “I’ll kill myself before returning; it’s like being stabbed in the back.”

What they want to enter is a large open-air parking lot known as “the embolsamiento,” beyond a barb wire fence. Until this summer, the apron was used by vehicles crossing to Morocco so they could wait their turn and avoid gridlock at the border. The government has prepared it so migrants can stop living on the street and access food, water, and basic hygiene measures.

Its first occupants arrived ten days ago. They were people who had been sleeping rough on beaches such as El Trampolín, or in the same neighborhood, until, as the report describes it, “the door closed.” Now there are about 1,300 people inside the enclosure, but for a week no one else has been admitted, according to data from the Loma Colmenar neighbors’ association.

Residents say the neighborhood is overwhelmed

Rafael García, the neighborhood president, says the situation has created a pull factor and estimates that between 4,000 and 4,500 migrants are surviving on the streets of the neighborhood, including minors, women with children, and adults. He says people are fighting for places and food and throwing stones on the road over conflicts among themselves. He also says everything is moving “very slowly” and that he has seen no changes in recent days.

“Every day more come, you can walk through the neighborhood and you see them,” García says. “We demand that they pick them up and do with them what they have to do. We have more than 200 children; the children are in the street.”

The Loma Colmenar facility has more than twenty military huts, shaded areas, and toilets. On Saturday, work to improve and expand it was underway, including the construction of a large tent. García was among about 200 Ceutí residents who took to the streets to protest that day, although they did not manage to reach the neighborhood. He says the protest was peaceful and that police did not let them through.

Some residents describe a neighborhood life under strain. One neighbor says she has been stressed for a month, cannot sleep or eat, and asks migrants to stop shouting late at night, making video calls, and leaving mattresses in the doorway during the day. Another says she feels surrounded.

At the same time, around ten migrants consulted say military personnel are telling them to go to the facility. The first three who say they will be allowed in once the door opens — Issam, 22; Usiel, 25; and Yahia, 23 — say they have been there for more than a week. They say they were forced off El Chorrillo beach and told to come there. “We don’t know when they’ll let us in, but we haven’t moved. If you leave, they take your place,” one of them says.

Waiting for the gate to open

Zahar says he crossed into Ceuta by swimming because he wanted to “be part of Europe.” He also says soldiers told him to leave the place where he was sleeping near Ceuta University Hospital and wait at Loma Colmenar. “I already came to ask when they were setting it up and they told us to go,” he says. “They’ve told us that when the huts are finished, they will let us pass.”

He tries to understand the hostility some residents feel. “No Ceuta resident is a bad person,” he says. “They have the right to protect their sisters; I haven’t had a bad moment with any of them in this time.” He also shows photographs on his phone of jobs he says he has done: farmer, industrial machinery operator, and disc jockey. Looking at images of himself in Ceuta and in better-dressed photos on his phone, he asks: “Would you say this boy is in Europe?”

What happened

A Lancaster County Amish family learned only after the fact that their newborn son’s death had been pulled into Pennsylvania’s measles fight, where state officials, political opponents and anti-vaccine figures were arguing over what caused it and whether the death should have been publicized at all.

Last Tuesday, Pennsylvania’s governor, Josh Shapiro, held a press conference to announce the state’s first two “measles-associated” deaths in 35 years. Both deaths were in Lancaster County, home to the country’s largest Amish community and the part of the state hit hardest by a months-long measles outbreak. The family at the center of one of those deaths did not know any of that when they were told. They also did not know that Shapiro’s political opponents were accusing him of using the deaths for political gain, or that the county coroner later said an infant who tested positive for measles had died from a ruptured spleen. Robert F. Kennedy Jr., the secretary of Health and Human Services, then mocked Shapiro on X, calling the press conference “unseemly” and writing, without evidence, that “the deaths may even have been altogether fabricated by one of the Governor’s hopeful staffers.”

A family cut off from the news

The father came out of his barn wearing a straw hat, black trousers and suspenders over a long-sleeved blue shirt, while one of his similarly dressed sons worked a horse-drawn plow nearby. Like most Amish people, he said, his family does not watch TV or use the internet, and they do not read the newspaper either. That is why they had not heard about the press conference, or about the public argument that followed it, when the subject of the death reached them.

He asked that his family’s names and exact location not be revealed, to protect their privacy. His son has not been publicly identified as the newborn whose death has been the focus of so much controversy. But when the father heard the details being discussed, he said the case sounded like his son. The family’s grief, not the political clash, was what filled in the rest of the story.

Measles spread through the household

About a month before the baby was born, John and Anna became aware that measles was spreading in their community. Lancaster County had recorded 210 cases at last count. Besides John, no one in the family had been vaccinated against the virus. John said he had heard that this strain of measles was not dangerous for pregnant women, though he was not sure whether to believe it. Anna worried about what measles might do to her baby, but she thought she could not avoid exposure. She was pregnant, and the virus was already moving through the area.

When they spoke about vaccination, neither John nor Anna treated it as a political statement. Anna said she was not sure why her parents had not taken her for shots, and John said the family simply did not do it. Pennsylvania allows children to attend school without being vaccinated if their parents object on religious or moral grounds. The Amish Church has no official doctrine against vaccines, but the vaccination rate among the Amish tends to be low; the state health department estimated last year that only 25 percent of the Amish community in Lancaster County had received the measles, mumps and rubella vaccine.

Soon, the illness reached their home. One of the older children got sick, and then the rest followed. Two became dehydrated and needed IVs. Anna became extremely ill, spiking a fever of 102 degrees, struggling to keep down food and fluids, and able to speak only in a whisper. On August 13, she went to a clinic for an IV because of dehydration. The next day, she went into labor.

The baby was delivered at a birthing center by the same midwife who had delivered Anna’s other children and, remarkably, Anna herself. He weighed seven pounds and was born a little after 5 p.m. on August 14. The family knew immediately that something was wrong: he had a pulse, but he was not breathing. Paramedics arrived within minutes, gave him oxygen and tried chest compressions with two thumbs, but nothing worked. Less than half an hour later, it was over.

What the coroner said, and what remains unresolved

John and Anna said they assumed Anna’s severe measles played a role in their loss, and the midwife told them the same thing. But the public debate quickly centered on another detail: a ruptured spleen. The Lancaster County coroner, Stephen Diamantoni, later told reporters that his office had performed an autopsy on an infant who tested positive for measles, but that the cause of death had been a ruptured spleen. He said publicly that the spleen was not enlarged and that the death was not related to measles. He also said the investigation was still ongoing and he was not sure when it would be finished.

That uncertainty has left the family waiting. John said he called the coroner’s office on Friday but was told the investigation was still under way. He and Anna were surprised that details of the case were being discussed while they were still waiting to hear from the coroner and hoping to read his report when it was done. The body was returned to them on August 17 and buried in a cemetery a few miles from their home. There, a fresh mound of dirt is marked by flat wooden stakes, one with the word “sillborn” scrawled on it, the missing “t” and the father’s name beneath it.

The confrontation centered on the Strait of Hormuz, where the U.S. said Iranian forces were preparing to use sea mines. The move also came after weeks in which the U.S. had said it had removed mine threats from the strait. Jordan’s army said it intercepted eight missiles that breached the kingdom’s airspace at dawn on Monday. The army’s statement, as quoted by state Petra News Agency, said air defense systems intercepted the missiles. BBC said Jordan reported no injuries at the bases, and the U.S. official said there was no damage or casualties at U.S. sites.

Iran’s Revolutionary Guards spokesman, Hossein Mohebbi, called the attack on Larak a “strategic and fatal mistake” and said the “enemy will pay the consequences of this miscalculation in both the economic and military fields,” according to BBC reporting. The same Iranian reporting said the Guards vowed “response and punishment.”

The sequence left the two sides in another exchange tied directly to the Strait of Hormuz: the U.S. said it struck launchers preparing to use sea mines there, and Iran said it responded with missiles at U.S. targets in Jordan.
