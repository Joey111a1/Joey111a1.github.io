Who are the M23 Rebels in eastern DRC?

What happened

M23’s renewed offensives have deepened insecurity in eastern Democratic Republic of the Congo, with control of key cities, mass displacement and election tensions raising questions about how far the rebellion can still shape the country’s political future.

In the eastern DRC, the March 23 Movement, or M23, is described as an armed group that imposes its authority on local communities through violence, sexual assault and terror. The movement’s story is tied to the long-running instability of North Kivu and the wider conflict in the country’s east, where fighting has repeatedly spilled into politics, displacement and cross-border accusations.

From the Kivu conflict to the March 23 agreement

M23 emerged from renewed tensions after the Kivu Conflict, a localized war in eastern DRC along the border with Rwanda that began in 2004. The Armed Forces of the Democratic Republic of the Congo, or FARDC, faced several armed groups, including the National Congress for the Defense of the People, or CNDP, founded in 2006 by the politician and Tutsi militia leader Laurent Nkunda.

The DRC is a multi-ethnic country, and the Tutsis are an ethnic group primarily living in the Kivu region. After five years of conflict, the DRC state and the CNDP signed a peace agreement on March 23, 2009. Under that deal, the rebel group agreed to become a political party in exchange for the release of imprisoned members, better living conditions for Tutsi communities, and three ministerial posts after the 2011 presidential election.

The account says the authorities did not comply with those terms, and former CNDP rebels who had joined the national army under the peace agreement revolted. In April 2012, they formed M23, using the March 23, 2009 signing date as the name of the movement.

Defeat, return and accusations of outside support

M23 resumed its armed campaign and became the target of accusations in Kinshasa. Officials there said Rwanda and Uganda supported the movement, while both countries denied any involvement. One anonymous Congolese officer described frontline encounters with fighters who, he said, had passed through Rwanda, but those claims remained part of the dispute around the rebellion.

By November 5, 2013, backed by UN peacekeepers, FARDC had won what the account calls a historic victory over M23, and the rebels fled to neighboring countries such as Uganda and Rwanda. That defeat did not end the story. In March 2022, almost a decade later, M23 made a military comeback.

According to UN experts cited in the account, the movement returned with Rwanda’s ongoing support and launched offensives against strategic positions held by the Congolese army. M23 leader Bertrand Bisimwa said on X on March 25, 2022, that the group had tried to avoid another war and blamed the Congolese government for choosing conflict instead.

Elections, displacement and the 2025 escalation

The rebellion’s return fed directly into the run-up to the December 20, 2023 general elections. The electoral roll revision went smoothly across most of the country, but voter enrollment was much harder in the east, leading some political figures and civil society organizations to ask for an extension there. The account says M23’s comeback increased insecurity in the province with the second-largest voter count.

Displacement also mounted. Quoting OCHA on November 19, 2023, the account said renewed fighting beginning in October had already displaced more than 450,000 additional people, while North Kivu, South Kivu and Ituri together hosted more than 5.5 million displaced persons. A later editor’s note added that Félix Tshisekedi was re-elected with 73 percent of the vote in the December 20, 2023 elections.

The conflict did not stop there. In early 2025, M23 launched new offensives and took complete control of Goma and Bukavu, the capitals of North and South Kivu, pushing the number of internally displaced people above 7 million. The account presents the rebellion as part of a wider security crisis in eastern DRC, with consequences that continue to reach beyond the front lines.

WHO chief warns DR Congo Ebola outbreak ‘far from being under control’
global_front_pageglobal_south_and_exploitedpublic_healthmaterial_harmanchor
What happened

The WHO is warning that DR Congo’s Ebola outbreak remains far from under control after cases passed 5,000 and deaths topped 2,300, with officials saying the virus is spreading quickly, chains of transmission remain hidden, and the risk of wider spread is still high.

Following an emergency meeting on Tuesday, WHO Director-General Tedros Adhanom Ghebreyesus called on the international community to help contain the outbreak. “We must be frank: The epidemic is far from being under control,” he told the meeting. “It had a big head start, and we are still playing catch-up.”

The emergency comes as WHO says there is no approved vaccine or treatment for the Bundibugyo strain behind the current surge. The outbreak is also the DRC’s 17th, and WHO said Tuesday marked the second meeting of its emergency committee since the agency declared the situation a Public Health Emergency of International Concern.

Why WHO says the outbreak is still dangerous

Tedros warned that the outbreak is spreading at “unprecedented speed” and said the locations of many deaths suggest transmission chains that have not yet been identified. “The epidemic will continue until every chain is found and broken,” he said.

One account said the outbreak has already killed more than 2,320 people, while another said the country had recorded more than 2,300 deaths out of nearly 5,000 cases. WHO’s concern is not only the toll so far, but the possibility of further spread beyond the DRC. Tedros said the virus is “moving faster than any previous Ebola outbreak” and warned that the risk of national and international spread remains high.

The outbreak has been reported in six DR Congo provinces, including areas near the border with South Sudan, and in neighbouring Uganda. WHO’s public health risk level is assessed as “very high” inside the DRC, “high” for Uganda and other bordering nations, and “low” for the rest of Africa and the world.

Communities, transport routes and the response problem

WHO said response efforts are being complicated by conflict in the mineral-rich DRC, misinformation, distrust of health workers and cultural traditions. Tedros said the outbreak is being fuelled by insecurity and displacement, along with intense population movement along roads, rivers and mining routes.

The WHO chief said the biggest concern was where people are dying: at home, in their communities, outside treatment centres, and outside known contact lists. That, he said, points to chains of transmission that have not yet been found. The agency has also said the emergency has been harder to tackle because there is no approved vaccine or treatment for the strain, even though clinical trials are under way.

WHO medic Thierno Balde said motorbike riders have become a focus of the response because of their transport work, including carrying patients and dead bodies. He said hand-washing stations and disinfectant have been made available, but the new goal is to recruit them as surveillance and response officers as WHO pushes harder to engage communities.

What remains unresolved

There is still no clear timeline for when the outbreak will be brought under control. Balde said WHO and partners hope the scale-up will have an impact in the coming months, but “cannot put a timeline” on containment.

The evidence also shows the response is still in motion: the first volunteer to receive an experimental vaccine for this strain got the jab in the United Kingdom last month, and hundreds of thousands of doses produced in India have already been manufactured and are ready for immediate use if trials succeed.

#CancelCoal: SCA to hear government appeal over 1 500MW new coal plan
global_south_and_exploitedpublic_healthpolitics_and_powerclimate_disasterregional expansion
What happened

The Supreme Court of Appeal will hear a government challenge to a 2024 ruling that stopped plans for 1 500MW of new coal-fired power, reviving the #CancelCoal fight over whether South Africa can still add coal capacity without properly weighing children’s rights, health and environmental harm.

The appeal over 1 500MW

The case returns to court after Electricity and Energy Minister Kgosientsho Ramokgopa appealed a high court ruling that halted the proposed procurement of 1 500 megawatts of new coal-fired power under the 2019 Integrated Resource Plan. The amount is equivalent to about three to four large coal-fired power plants.

In that 2024 ruling, the court said the record was “ominously silent” on the effects of the extra coal power on the environment and the health of the nation, particularly children. It declared the relevant decisions unconstitutional, unlawful and invalid, and set them aside. The court also found no evidence that the minister or the National Energy Regulator of South Africa had adequately considered the health, air-quality, environmental and children’s-rights implications of the additional coal capacity.

The respondents — the African Climate Alliance, Vukani Environmental Movement in Action and groundWork — are set to return to court on Wednesday to defend that ruling. The hearing begins at 10am.

Children’s rights and energy planning

At the centre of the appeal is whether the government must specifically consider children’s rights and interests when making major energy decisions whose consequences could last for decades. The respondents say it must. They argue that sections 24 and 28(2) of the Constitution required the minister and Nersa to look at how the coal decision would affect children, and they point to the absence of any children’s-rights impact assessment.

They also say the decision records contain no meaningful consideration of children or youth, and argue that the new coal plan could limit constitutional rights including those to a healthy environment, life, dignity, equality, food, water and healthcare. On their version, the government did not show that additional coal was necessary for energy security or more cost-effective than alternatives.

Ramokgopa takes the opposite view. In his heads of argument, he says coal cannot be treated as constitutionally impermissible simply because it produces greenhouse gas emissions. He argues that domestic law and international instruments that have become part of South African law do not require zero carbon emissions, and that government must reduce emissions while balancing environmental concerns against the need for a reliable electricity supply. He says coal was needed to provide reliable baseload power when the decision was made.

The minister also says the challenged procurement has not begun, so there was no basis for concluding that the decision had violated or threatened constitutional rights. He argues that courts should not substitute their own judgment for government’s decisions about the country’s energy mix. He also says the court wrongly required the government to apply constitutional protections concerning the best interests of children to an energy policy decision, because the Integrated Resource Plan and ministerial determination “deal with energy and not ‘matters concerning the child’” and do not directly cause “harm, maltreatment, neglect, abuse or degradation of children”.

Why the case still matters

The appeal comes against a changed electricity landscape. South Africa’s 2025 IRP, gazetted in October 2025, makes no provision for new coal-fired generation. Even so, the respondents say the case remains significant because it could clarify the legal obligations of decision-makers in future energy and environmental decisions.

That wider question is also tied to how the government uses evidence about coal’s effects. The respondents’ papers quote the minister’s answering affidavit as acknowledging that “the historic impact of coal fired power generation on climate change and human health is … not disputed”. They say the government still failed to show that the extra coal was needed, or that its costs and benefits had been properly weighed.

The dispute also reaches the question of “clean coal” technologies. The respondents say the government did not establish whether options such as carbon capture and storage were technically and economically feasible, and their court papers describe a departmental “desktop study” as ultimately consisting of a single Wikipedia printout on carbon capture and storage.

The minister’s side says the final 2019 IRP increased the coal allocation from 1 000MW in the 2018 draft to 1 500MW after consultation, and argues that the high court was wrong to say there had been no consultation on the additional coal. The respondents dispute that too, saying the final allocation was materially different from the proposal put out for comment.

For both sides, the appeal is about more than one procurement decision. The minister says setting aside the decisions was an “academic exercise” because no coal power stations have been built under the challenged allocation. The respondents reply that constitutional rights can be protected before harm occurs, and that preventing future harm was the point of the case.

Chris Griffiths, the study’s joint senior author and a professor at the University of Oxford and Queen Mary University of London, said traffic pollution damages children’s health and development and called the work the “strongest evidence yet” on preventing those harms. He said ambitious clean-air zones should be a priority for cities with traffic-related air pollution. Stephen Burgess, a professor of biostatistics at the University of Cambridge who was not involved in the research, said it was not clear the changes were truly attributable to Ulez rather than other factors, including the Covid pandemic’s impact on pollution levels.
