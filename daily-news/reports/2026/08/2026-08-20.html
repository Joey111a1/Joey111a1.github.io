Malnutrition fuels measles toll

What happened

A study of 354 children treated for measles at Bangladesh Shishu Hospital and Institute found widespread malnutrition and poor breastfeeding histories among patients, highlighting how the outbreak has hit the most vulnerable children as Bangladesh faces a severe measles surge.

Prof Mirza Md Ziaul Islam, the hospital director, and Mustafizur Rahman, senior director of icddr,b, disclosed the findings at an event at the hospital yesterday. The team from Bangladesh Shishu Hospital and icddr,b said it conducted the study between March and July, collecting nasopharyngeal swabs, blood samples, and demographic, clinical and vaccination information from children aged 0 to 14.

Malnutrition among the children admitted with measles

Among the children under five who were admitted with measles, about two-thirds were malnourished, according to the study. Looking only at the 308 children aged 0 to 5, 37 were severely underweight and 163 were moderately underweight, which meant 65 percent had severe or moderate malnutrition.

The feeding history was also poor for many of the patients. The study found that 135 children, or 38 percent, had not been breastfed properly in the first six months of life. Eleven of the children died of measles, and nine of them, or 82 percent, had not been exclusively breastfed.

Many patients were too young for routine vaccination

The study also found that more than 40 percent of the patients were between three and nine months old, meaning they were not yet eligible for the routine measles vaccination schedule. Under the routine programme, children receive measles vaccines at nine and 15 months; during the emergency vaccination campaign, the eligibility age was lowered to six months.

Among the 146 children under nine months, 142 tested positive for measles. Of the 126 children who were eligible for vaccination but had not received the shots, 122 tested positive. The study said 48 children had received one dose and 21 had received both doses, though 21 of those fully vaccinated children were admitted with measles-like symptoms and, the researchers said, should be investigated further.

The findings land in the middle of a major outbreak

The hospital study comes as Bangladesh is dealing with what health workers described as its worst measles outbreak in recent memory. The evidence cited 922 deaths among patients with measles or measles-like symptoms and 1.27 lakh hospitalisations so far.

According to the Directorate General of Health Services, around 1.64 lakh measles and suspected measles cases had been reported till yesterday this year, while only 132 cases were reported in 2025. Health experts blamed disruptions in routine vaccination and the failure to mount a timely special campaign for creating an immunity gap, and said malnutrition among children made the situation worse.

The researchers recommended ensuring measles-rubella vaccination for all children, improving vaccination strategies using immunological evidence, expanding clinical and epidemiological research, and turning that evidence into policy to support measles elimination.

The great European drought devours the continent's heart: harvests at lows, historic restrictions, and a stagnant economy

What happened

Europe’s drought has moved from scorched parks to a wider crisis that is hitting water supplies, farming, transport and power, while leaving officials in France, Britain, Italy and Hungary scrambling over how far the damage will spread.

The water shortage has brought historic declines in river discharges and water resources, and millions of people are seeing restrictions for the first time in countries less used to scarcity. The heat has also become deadly: about 25,000 Europeans have died so far this summer, including more than 9,500 in Germany during the last week of June, while France has entered its fifth heatwave in a row.

Restrictions and shortages spread

France is one of the hardest-hit countries. According to the French Ministry of Ecological Transition, 70% of the country is under some form of water restriction, and 40,000 people in 80 municipalities already have supply cuts and must be served by tankers or bottled water. About 67 departments are at crisis level, where water is reserved for priority use and activities such as filling pools, watering sports fields or washing cars are banned.

The drought has come alongside the hottest and driest summer since records began. Precipitation was 67% below average, July was the third driest since 1959, and 94% of groundwater reserves were below normal levels. The Geological and Mining Survey said the condition of aquifers is deteriorating, and more than a thousand watercourses showed interruptions in flow or were dry.

France’s Minister for Ecological Transition, Monique Barbut, gathered the crisis cabinet last week to examine measures against the “particularly intense drought and the effects of climate change, which continue to worsen pressure on water resources.” She asked prefects to issue water-restriction orders without delay when needed and said containment must be the first response for individuals, agriculture and industry.

Rivers, shipping and power are under strain

The drought is also disrupting movement and energy. Cargo ships carrying essential goods to Rotterdam via the Rhine are traveling at 30% capacity to avoid running aground. In Hungary, the country’s only nuclear plant has been halted, with the cooling water in the Danube having disappeared. In Italy, authorities have already asked Switzerland for water to prevent losses in rice fields.

The economic damage is spreading beyond the water sector. The crisis is affecting food prices, goods transport, energy production and growth forecasts for major economies, with the short-term hit to continental GDP put at at least 50,000 million euros. France’s minister said the combined impact of heatwaves, fires and drought would cost the country between 10,000 and 15,000 million euros, equal to 0.3 to 0.5 percentage points of GDP.

Britain is facing its own severe strain. Authorities have formally declared drought in 71.3% of England after the driest July since records began in 1836. Only 14% of England’s rivers are at levels considered normal for this time of year, while 22% have “exceptionally low” flows, and reservoirs are falling, with five already at “exceptionally low” levels. The British prime minister, Andy Burnham, described the situation as a “powder keg.”

Agriculture is taking the hardest hit

The sharpest blow has fallen on agriculture. Wheat production in the EU and the United Kingdom is expected to fall by 24% this year, costing producers around 2,000 million euros. France has already promised aid to the sector, and Sébastien Lecornu said no farmer would be left alone to face the crisis.

The pressure is even clearer in France, where the main agricultural union, FNSEA, forecasts a 40% fall in corn harvests, rising to 70% in some departments. It also warned of excessive mortality in poultry farms after the intense heatwave at the end of June. Lecornu promised urgent measures and aid for farmers in next year’s budget.

Britain’s agriculture is also suffering. Production has been reduced because crops have had to be harvested at the earliest point in the season in 20 years, and the government has prepared contingencies for what it calls the “worst reasonable scenario,” including limits on non-essential uses such as watering sports fields, washing vehicles and filling private pools.

What happened

Ceuta’s nurses say they are facing a surge of acute gastroenteritis, scabies, impetigo and tuberculosis cases while working with too few staff, and they warn that overcrowding and poor access to basic resources are pushing the city’s health system toward collapse.

Every day, they say they are dealing with more than 200 cases and having to double shifts and work overtime. The Ceuta Nursing College says the pressure is greater than the city can absorb, including in beach settlements where monitoring is “more complex,” while the Ministry of Defense has also acknowledged a scabies outbreak among military personnel stationed there.

Infections spreading in crowded conditions

The diseases being seen in Ceuta include more than 200 cases of acute gastroenteritis, 20 of scabies, 21 of impetigo and 4 of tuberculosis, according to the evidence provided by nurses. One of the nurses on the ground, Alejandro Artero, said the pattern has been predictable: first traumas and wounds, then skin infections such as scabies, and later respiratory diseases.

Artero said other problems have followed, including worsening chronic illnesses such as diabetes and asthma, mental health disorders that cannot be treated normally, and more gastrointestinal cases linked to sanitation conditions. He also said some people are drinking irrigation water, and argued that infections were bound to appear.

The nurses’ professional college says the workload has left health workers “physically and psychologically exhausted.” Rosa Fuentes, identified as the Ceuta president, said there are not enough means to address the crisis and called on the central government to declare a state of emergency.

Tracking patients has become part of the problem

Beyond the raw number of cases, Artero said one of the main difficulties is finding patients again to complete tests and follow up over several days. He said many are dispersed around the city and some avoid identifying themselves for fear of being located by authorities.

“There are patients we know three times and all three times they give different names,” he said, adding that this makes it harder to monitor them properly. He used tuberculosis as an example, saying four cases have been diagnosed but that there may be many more because diagnosis requires tests and, in some cases, locating the patient later.

The Ceuta Nursing College said the figures do not include cases from the beach settlements, where oversight is harder. The broader picture, as described by the nurses, is of a city where care is being provided under conditions that make both treatment and tracing more difficult.

Debates over migrants, the military outbreak and who should act

The Spanish Epidemiology Society has urged people not to stigmatize migrants. Its president, Maria João Forjaz, told EFE that “thinking they bring diseases is medically incorrect and, in addition, dangerous,” and said the underlying problem is the lack of basic resources. She said overcrowding helps spread skin and respiratory diseases, while lack of food and drinking water can drive gastrointestinal problems.

Defense said the scabies cases detected among soldiers stationed in Ceuta were “a common infection in humid areas” and said all those affected were receiving the treatment recommended by health authorities after diagnosis. It also said measures had been taken to treat the infected and hygienize facilities. The ministry said the outbreak had nothing to do with the military’s intervention in the humanitarian crisis in the autonomous city.

The Ministry of Defense said 24 soldiers had been diagnosed with scabies, including two cases that predated the mass migrant entry. Health Minister Mónica García, who visited Ceuta over the weekend, acknowledged that the system there is “stressed” but denied a collapse and warned against blaming migrants as disease carriers, calling that link “unfair and xenophobic.”

Medical unions had already warned about the strain. CESM said reception and assistance resources in the city were “maxed out” and described the situation as one of “national emergency.” Florentino Pérez Raya, president of the General Council of Nursing, called for “a single command in Ceuta” to lead the response and for the resources and supplies that the territorial emergency requires to be allocated.

In the first 15 days of the crisis, Ceuta’s health system treated 5,800 migrants, according to Health data. Of those, 3,500 were assisted in primary care and 2,300 in hospital, with pressure easing over time from 1,149 on the first day to around 300 in later days.
