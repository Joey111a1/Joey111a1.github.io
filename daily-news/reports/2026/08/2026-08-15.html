Evacuations spread from Croatia to Spain

In Croatia, a wildfire driven by strong winds descended on the tourist town of Lokva Rogoznica on the Dalmatian coast overnight. The EU’s Copernicus climate change service warned of “very extreme” wildfire conditions across a large area of central and eastern Europe, with other pockets over southern Britain, Ireland, northern France and southern Sweden.

Reuters Climate Monitor data showed the average high across western Europe on Friday was forecast to be 31.2C, 8C above what was typical from 1961 to 1990, while Europe as a whole was 1.6C above the 1961-1990 norm. What happened

Drought-hit farmers in England are getting £65m in new support as Andy Burnham tries to answer pressure over the climate crisis, but the package has already drawn questions over whether it will do enough for water-stressed farms and reservoir building.

Relief for farms under drought pressure

Most of the new money will go to the sustainable farming incentive, or SFI, which pays farmers in England to support nature. Officials said the extra £50m for that scheme will take the total SFI budget to £290m, with the new money prioritised for areas facing the greatest need during the drought.

The package also changes how some farmers can use their land. Those who are paid to look after nature on parts of their farms, and who would normally be barred from cutting or grazing that land until later in the year, will now be able to do so where grass is scarce without losing payments. Burnham’s expected message is that the state should help farmers keep producing food when dry weather makes normal farming harder.

He is due to visit a farm in the south-west of England on Saturday, where he is expected to say that farmers there brought in the earliest harvest of their working lives and saw yields come in well below what they should have been. He is also expected to point to lower income, higher costs and no rain in the forecast as part of the reason for the emergency support.

Reservoirs, water access and the push for a broader response

The announcement includes £15m for building on-farm reservoirs so farmers can have supplies during future dry summers. But campaigners warned that the £15m is reallocated money from existing funds and would only cover a “fraction” of the reservoirs needed.

Burnham and officials also set out changes meant to make water easier to access where it is most needed. That includes simpler arrangements around how much farmers can take from the environment and changes to the speed at which licences are given. Downing Street is also seeking a cross-government response to the drought, bringing together action on farming, water and infrastructure, along with more flexibility for food producers.

The pressure on reservoir capacity was already part of the debate. MPs had urged Burnham to speed up the rate at which new reservoirs are built, given that five have “exceptionally low” levels at present. Officials said they were supporting nine new reservoirs being developed.

Burnham said after visiting the scene of a fire in Stourbridge in the West Midlands on Friday that “there’s no point denying where we are” and that the country needs to “face up to the changing climate.” He added that the Cobra meeting earlier this week had helped mobilise resources for individuals, communities, and fire and rescue services.

What happens next

The immediate next step is Burnham’s farm visit in the south-west on Saturday, where he is expected to make the case that “the ability to grow our own food is national security” and that farmers “should not be carrying that risk alone.” The debate around reservoirs, water access and longer-term support for dry summers is likely to continue as farmers cope with the effects of the drought.

Their fourth sister was killed when her body was blown to pieces, and their mother was seriously injured and later died in hospital.

Fatima has already undergone multiple surgeries on her foot and jaw, which was dislocated in the blast. She also lost an eye. Abu Sittah now has to remove more shrapnel from her face and back to reduce the risk of infection.

Fatima and her sisters in Beirut

Days earlier, Fatima was in a hotel in Hamra, in the Lebanese capital, with her older sisters Malak, 16, and Kawthar, 14, while they were being treated at the American University of Beirut Medical Centre. Their father, Hassan, 60, was cooking moudardara in the apartment as the girls sat together making bracelets and phone straps and eating BBQ-flavoured crisps.

He said children rarely arrive with only one injury; more often they have head injuries, fractures, burns and other severe trauma.

He estimated that around 80 percent of his patients required intensive care, and said children often need 12 to 16 surgeries before adulthood. As they grow, scarred and injured tissue does not expand at the same rate as the rest of the body, so reconstruction has to be repeated.

The cases are part of the more than 400 children in Lebanon treated by the Ghassan Abu Sittah Children’s Fund, which he established in January 2024 to help provide medical treatment in Gaza and Lebanon. He said the injuries confronting medical teams have continued to grow despite the US-brokered ceasefire on 16 April.

For Fatima, Malak and Kawthar, the immediate future is still defined by treatment and recovery. Abu Sittah’s next steps include removing more shrapnel, finishing amputations where needed, and carrying out further reconstruction so children can move toward rehabilitation.

What happened

South Africa’s Constitutional Court has set aside Shell and Impact Africa’s Wild Coast exploration right, siding with communities who said they were shut out of decisions over land and sea that shape their livelihoods, culture and spiritual life.

The case began with an exploration right granted to Impact Africa in 2014 and later renewed; Shell bought a 50% participating interest in 2021. A high court found the grant and renewals unlawful and procedurally unfair, and the Supreme Court of Appeal upheld those findings but suspended the setting aside of the right while a third renewal application was pursued. Communities and environmental organisations took that remedy to the Constitutional Court.

Consultation that came too late

Justice Jody Kollapen said the original consultation process had serious failures. The court found that thousands of community members were excluded from decision-making and that, more than a decade later, telling them they could now be consulted would not cure the original violation. Kollapen said consultation was about more than giving people a chance to express an opinion, and that Impact could not reasonably have believed meaningful consultation had occurred.

The judgment went beyond the fate of one exploration right. Kollapen placed the dispute in South Africa’s longer history of dispossession and the struggles of coastal communities to protect their land, marine resources and ways of life from extractive activities. He said natural resources were shared commons important to present and future generations, yet vulnerable to economically driven development. The court also said the relationship between Wild Coast communities and the sea extended beyond nutrition and income, with the ocean central to livelihoods, ancestral practices, traditional healing and spiritual life.

The court rejected the Supreme Court of Appeal’s approach of sending the matter back for another renewal process. It said a fresh and properly considered decision would require a new application, a fresh consultation process and a new environmental management programme. It also said the SCA had given too much weight to the roughly R1.1 billion Shell and Impact Africa said they had invested, and rejected Shell’s argument that it was an innocent party because it had not carried out the original consultation.

For Sinegugu Zukulu of Sustaining the Wild Coast, the ruling was a major victory after years of opposition. Nonhle Mbuthuma of the Amadiba Crisis Committee called it a “very clear victory” for Wild Coast communities and a “serious defeat” for Shell and Impact Africa, while saying it did not necessarily mean offshore exploration was permanently barred.

Shell said it had noted the judgment and remained committed to “responsible offshore exploration, meaningful stakeholder engagement and environmental stewardship.”

The paper frames multimodal design as a long-horizon agentic process: a fixed model sits inside a design harness, and a separate meta-harness keeps revising that harness using rollout feedback. In AutoDesign, the meta-harness optimizer guides a code agent to update the harness recursively, with the goal of matching human design priors rather than leaving the workflow static.

The system is organized as two nested loops. The inner loop handles artifact generation and revision through a designer module and a critic module; the outer loop runs across tasks, looks at trajectories and evaluation scores, proposes bounded harness changes, and accepts them only when training performance improves and development performance does not decline. The final optimized harness, called DesignHarness, is described as having four main stages: source ingestion, iterative generation and revision, feedback from a rule-based validator and a VLM-based critic, and finalization.

The paper also says AutoDesign can run with optional human guidance. In that mode, a user can add directional natural-language input when the optimizer appears to have plateaued, so the update proposal is conditioned on that guidance as well as the task traces and scores.

PosterBench is where the gains are measured

To test the framework, the authors focus on academic paper-to-poster generation and introduce PosterBench, which has a 100-paper Main Track spanning five disciplines and a shared 10-paper subset called PosterBench-mini for controlled evaluation. The benchmark scores posters across seven dimensions: faithfulness, coverage, density, visual evidence, layout, readability, and aesthetics.

On the PosterBench Main Track, AutoDesign posts the highest score at 78.32 and surpasses the closed-source commercial system Claude Design by 7.45 points. Across seven controlled code agent-model configurations, attaching the learned DesignHarness raises the average PosterBench Score from 54.99 to 67.39, a gain of 12.4%. The paper also says the optimized harness improves all coding agents on PosterBench by 5.0 to 19.6 points, with the largest gain reported for DeepSeek V4 Pro with Claude Code.

The mini benchmark shows the same pattern in a more tightly controlled setting. AutoDesign reaches 81.46 with Codex, compared with 75.87 for the native Codex baseline, and 74.56 with Claude Code, compared with 69.55 for the standalone baseline. The paper also presents a cost-performance trade-off: LongCat-2.0 reaches 55.13 at about $0.27 per poster, while GPT-5.5 reaches 81.46 at $10.02 per poster.

Human review and the claimed limit of the system

The preprint includes a system-blind human study with 11 volunteer reviewers who compared AutoDesign, Claude Code, OpenDesign, and Claude Design on the 100 source papers. AutoDesign gets the highest Bradley–Terry point estimate at 64.0%, with a 95% interval of 55.2% to 77.8%. Its tie-adjusted preference scores are 61.3% against Claude Code, 63.1% against OpenDesign, and 67.6% against Claude Design.

The paper also highlights a fully autonomous long-horizon run in which the system executes 253 tool calls and 11 editing turns in 40 minutes for under $3, and it says that run reaches average conference-poster quality in human evaluation. At the same time, the reported validation is limited to academic paper-to-poster generation, even though the authors present the approach as a broader design pattern for multimodal-in, multimodal-out systems.
