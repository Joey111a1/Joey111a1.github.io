Death tolls rise across several districts

Palestinian health officials said two people, including a child, were killed in a strike on a residential building in Gaza City, while another person was killed near Jabalia in northern Gaza.

Another account said a father, mother and their nine-year-old son were killed in a strike on a house in Khan Younis, and a man and woman died when an apartment block was hit in Deir el-Balah. In parallel, medical staff said at least 18 Palestinians had been killed in strikes from dawn across Gaza City, Deir al-Balah and Khan Younis, describing the day as one of the deadliest in weeks.

What happened

Ceuta’s shops and terraces reopened as most of the migrants who entered the Spanish enclave this week moved back toward Morocco, but the city remained defined by the dead, a disputed toll and a question that still divides residents: who caused the surge and who is responsible for its aftermath?

By Sunday, the streets no longer looked like a city on edge. Most of the 60,000 migrants who swam, scrambled and then charged into the tiny Spanish exclave in North Africa had walked back to Morocco, though some remained in the hills around the city. At the same time, Spanish civil guard ships continued searching for bodies in the waters near the stretch of urban beach that had been breached days earlier.

A city reopening, but not at ease

Shops had reopened, and the usual mix of residents and tourists in the city of 84,000 were filling the open-air terraces, restaurants and cafes. Even so, the mood stayed unsettled. Ceuta, which prides itself on being home to four cultures, was still grappling with the aftermath of the border surge, and locals were disagreeing about what caused it and who exactly should be blamed.

That argument ran through everyday conversations in town. Mohamad Abdelkader Driss, a 44-year-old waiter at a fried fish restaurant, said: “It’s not the young men’s fault.” He added: “If you open the border, of course everyone comes in, but the young men aren’t to blame. The authorities are responsible.” Some migrants who returned to Morocco told a similar story, describing a border that was effectively open and saying authorities did little as they watched thousands of people cross.

The improvised morgue keeps filling

While the city reopened above ground, bodies continued to arrive at Ceuta’s provisional morgue in the old military hospital. The facility was installed after the massive influx of migrants last Thursday and is now equipped to hold up to 200 bodies. It continues receiving the dead from those who crossed the Tarajal pier or tried to swim along the border.

The figures around the death toll do not match. Spanish authorities said Sunday that at least 72 migrants died, including some who drowned and others who were killed in a stampede to cross a breakwater barrier. Ceuta’s autonomous city government, led by the Popular Party’s Juan Vivas, has counted 88 fatalities. On the Moroccan side of the border, Morocco’s Interior Ministry said 11 deaths had been recovered, including 10 by drowning, while details were not released about the other.

The old military hospital remains sealed. Access is blocked to unauthorized people, and Guardia Civil officers, Army personnel and forensics workers are kept out of view behind a wall. Guardia Civil officers have continued working at sea to recover bodies. Funeral home sources said many of the deceased are teenagers, some women, Moroccans and sub-Saharans.

The bodies are not all from the pier stampede on Thursday. Some appear more deteriorated because arrivals at Ceuta’s coast had been ongoing for more than two weeks, and the swim across can leave victims submerged for a time. Currents carry them to different points of the city, where they are found later.

Blame, claims and the political backlash

The surge also pushed the dispute far beyond Ceuta. Morocco’s Interior Ministry said Sunday in a televised statement that around 40,000 people headed toward Ceuta last week during the crossing. The ministry said those who entered the exclave were misled by online misinformation, human trafficking networks and incorrect interpretations of legal and administrative information.

Rabat’s account is not the only one shaping the debate. Spanish authorities described unauthorized arrivals on Thursday and Friday of 50,000 to 60,000, mostly young men, saying they swelled Ceuta’s population by more than half. The humanitarian crisis caused by the sudden rush of people has reignited immigration debates in Europe and beyond.

That argument was visible in the streets as far-right Spanish politicians and well-known right-wing influencers arrived in Ceuta over the weekend. Their presence sparked counterprotests, including from members of the city’s large Muslim population, many of whom are of Moroccan descent. On Saturday evening, Alvise Pérez, a far-right Spanish politician and member of the European Parliament, called for migrants to be expelled. He later had to shelter in a bar as residents banged on the windows, before police escorted him away.

A 20-year-old Ceuta resident and student, Noor Ahmed, said the protest and the arrival of figures like Pérez were opportunistic. “They just want to take advantage of the humanitarian crisis Ceuta is currently experiencing,” she said. Santiago Abascal, the leader of the far-right Vox party, visited Ceuta on Sunday and called the arrivals “an invasion.” He accused the Spanish government of failing to protect the border and demanded “drastic” measures.

Returns and a border still under watch

Even as the city tried to settle, the border remained in motion. The Government delegate, Miguel Ángel Pérez Triano, said authorities would work to speed up files for people who entered irregularly in recent days so returns could be carried out “as soon as possible.” Ceuta has almost 3,000 security and armed forces personnel deployed “for as long as necessary.”

Detentions continued in the streets, unsettling residents who spent Sunday on restaurant terraces. People who were caught were taken to police cars and are likely to be moved to police stations for identification and expulsion proceedings. Others had moved to Benítez beach and toward the Center for Temporary Migrant Stay, while some slept on the hillside by the wetlands to avoid detection.

The border itself stayed tense. Tarajal’s fog returned on Sunday, and the Guardia Civil officers patrolling the pier introduced a 500-meter buoy rope and a submerged mesh to hinder swimming crossings and allow denial of those who arrive by sea, in line with the Supreme Court’s recent ruling. During the night, several returns reportedly took place, and the Army controlled about ten people trying to enter by sea.

Ceuta still had not returned to normal. The dead were still being brought in, the returns were still being processed, and the city’s residents were left with a crisis that had changed the streets as quickly as it had emptied them.

The trade-offs in practice

The researchers said the same mathematical strain shows up in other systems too. In Germany, preserving both local representation and proportionality caused the Bundestag to expand sharply before reforms were introduced in 2023. In the UK, the 2024 general election produced the country’s least proportional result on record, with Labour winning 63% of the seats after receiving 33.7% of the vote.

Klausen and Sebastian Tim Holdum of the University of Copenhagen described the result as an impossibility theorem: once the number of parties gets high enough, one of the three principles has to give. They argue that this is a mathematical constraint, not corruption or conspiracy, and that countries can still choose which compromise they prefer.

The paper also proposes an alternative called “geographically ranked guaranteed proportionality.” Under that method, each party would get seats strictly in line with its national vote share, while constituencies would be ranked by how strongly candidates performed locally. The authors say that would preserve proportionality, but weaken regional representation, because a candidate could win locally and still miss out if the party had already filled its quota elsewhere.
