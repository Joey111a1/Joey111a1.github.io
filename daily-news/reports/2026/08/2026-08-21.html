What happened

Imran Khan was moved from Rawalpindi’s Adiala jail to a private hospital in Islamabad after Pakistan’s top court ordered an independent medical board to examine him, escalating a long-running dispute over the jailed former prime minister’s health and whether he should receive treatment outside prison.

Khan, 73, was transferred early on Friday to Shifa International Hospital under tight security. His Pakistan Tehreek-e-Insaf party confirmed the move but did not offer further details. The government did not immediately comment, although Parliamentary Affairs Minister Tariq Fazal Chaudhry said hours earlier that it would fully comply with the Supreme Court’s ruling. Security around the hospital was then tightened, and a senior official said hundreds of police and paramilitary personnel were deployed in the area.

The court order that led to the transfer

The move followed a Supreme Court order on Tuesday directing authorities to send the imprisoned former leader to hospital within two days. The court said an independent medical board should assess Khan’s condition and include his personal physician. Before that ruling, Adiala jail’s superintendent had submitted a report on Monday that described an August 10 assessment by a cardiac board.

That report recorded fluctuating blood pressure and anxiety, which it said was linked to Khan’s restricted contact with his family. The government challenged the order, but officials later said preparations for the transfer were completed on Thursday. CNN also reported that Parliamentary Affairs Minister Tariq Fazal Chaudhry said the government had approached the Supreme Court to seek clarification on whether the medical relief granted to Khan applied only to him or could also be extended to other prisoners.

Health concerns and repeated court requests

Khan’s family and his party have repeatedly asked courts over the past year to intervene in his medical care, especially over concerns about the vision in his right eye. The Supreme Court had twice declined similar requests to move him to hospital since February. In one earlier case, the Islamabad High Court ordered an assessment by a medical board instead.

The health dispute sits alongside Khan’s wider legal troubles. He was voted out of office by parliament in April 2022 and has been in prison since August 2023, when a court convicted him in a case involving the sale of state gifts received while he was prime minister. He and PTI have denied wrongdoing and said the prosecutions are politically motivated. The evidence also says he and his wife, Bushra Bibi, have since been convicted in several other cases.

What happens next

Khan’s lawyer, Uzair Bhandari, said earlier that the former prime minister is expected to remain in hospital until at least his next court hearing on September 16. CNN said it was still unclear whether he would be returned to prison after the examination or allowed to stay at the hospital for medical treatment, while Khan’s party and family have demanded that he be allowed to remain there.

For now, the transfer marks the most significant movement in his custody since his detention began in August 2023, and it has drawn fresh attention to his health, his legal fights, and the court’s role in deciding what treatment he receives next.

Kyiv was hit through the night and into Thursday

Ukrainian authorities said the “massive” assault struck Kyiv and the surrounding region overnight and on Thursday, leaving at least 17 people dead and dozens wounded. In the capital, local authorities said strikes damaged a hospital, a school, a preschool, residential buildings and several warehouses across the city.

Kyiv mayor Vitali Klitschko said Russia had “purposefully” hit critical infrastructure in the latest strikes, and warned that hot water supply had been disrupted. CNN producers in the city heard several explosions and saw smoke over Kyiv as an air raid alert stretched on for hours, while Reuters video showed massive fires over the capital. The attacks continued during the day, with three separate air raid alarms sounding on Thursday.

The toll also reflected damage outside the city centre. Rescue workers pulled two people from the rubble and continued working through the aftermath, while emergency services said more than a dozen locations across Kyiv were affected. In one account from a resident, the missiles narrowly missed a home by about 10 minutes before the road and nearby buildings were hit.

There was also a direct dispute over what the Russian strikes were aimed at. Russia’s defence ministry said on Telegram that it had launched a “massive strike using high-precision weapons” against military facilities, a transport and logistics center, and warehouses in Kyiv and the broader region. Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelensky said the Russians had “prepared for a long time” and combined missiles and drones “to inflict as much damage as possible on civilian infrastructure.”

The barrage spilled beyond Ukraine’s borders

The same wave of attacks also reached Moldova and Romania on Thursday. Romania said an explosive-laden naval drone believed to have been launched by Russia targeted one of its offshore natural gas projects.

Romania’s defence ministry said radar systems detected aerial targets near the Ukrainian border overnight, prompting the scrambling of two fighter jets and a helicopter. It later said a drone entered Romanian airspace near Galați before crashing in an unpopulated area in Tulcea county. Moldova’s president, Maia Sandu, said her country’s airspace was again violated when “a drone came down on Moldovan territory.”

Those incidents pushed the assault into a wider Black Sea security problem. Romanian Foreign Minister Oana Toiu said European allies were “analyzing” how they could support Ukraine’s air defence, but added that they were not doing enough collectively. European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen called the naval drone an example of “hybrid warfare” and said an escalating campaign of threats was underway.

Air defences are now part of the story

The strikes also highlighted Ukraine’s shortage of interceptor missiles. CNN reported that Ukraine has been relatively successful at intercepting the large numbers of drones Russia uses, but that Moscow has repeatedly exploited Kyiv’s near-zero stocks of Patriot interceptor missiles, which are needed to defend against faster-moving ballistic missiles.

Zelensky told CNN earlier this month that Ukraine only has 10% of the interceptor missiles it urgently needs from the United States and other allies. In the same interview, he said, “This year we have two and a half times less interceptors than we had in 2025.” He also said Russia had two times more ballistic missiles per month than before.

That shortage mattered in Thursday’s attack because it came during a barrage that Ukrainian authorities said combined ballistic missiles, cruise missiles and drones. The Ukrainian air force said dozens of ballistic, cruise and hypersonic missiles and 168 drones were launched overnight, and later reported shooting down 41 of 46 cruise missiles and 145 drones, while none of an unspecified number of high-speed ballistic missiles was intercepted.

By Thursday evening, the immediate picture was one of damage still being counted, fires still being put out and airspace still under strain. The cross-border drone incidents in Romania and Moldova made clear that the effects of the attack did not stop at Kyiv’s edge.

What happened

US Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent has warned that a new sanctions push is meant to squeeze Iran’s economy hard enough to force a political reckoning, while leaving open the question of how far Washington is prepared to go if pressure alone does not work.

Bessent told CNBC that the US wanted to “squash” the Iranian economy and said, “We are going to collapse this regime.” He urged allies and “the rest of the world” to choose sides, adding: “You are either with us or against us.” He also said the planned pressure campaign would “minimise the need” for a “large-scale kinetic restart” of the conflict, but gave no details of the measures Donald Trump’s team is preparing.

The remarks came after Trump warned that countries offering “any type of lifeline” to Iran’s Islamic Republic would face “tremendous economic consequences.” Bessent said the coming penalties would be part of “the greatest co-ordinated economic isolation in the history of the world,” and said the Treasury would go after anyone continuing to do business with Iran, including by transferring money or buying its oil. He also said further details would be made public at a press conference on 24 August.

Pressure campaign, not a return to war

The sanctions threat is being cast as an alternative to renewed fighting. The US and Israel started a war with Iran in February, saying the strikes were meant to stop Tehran developing nuclear weapons, which Iran has consistently denied. Iran responded by attacking Israel, US bases across the Middle East and Gulf Arab allies of the US.

Since then, Iran has largely closed the Strait of Hormuz, the waterway through which some 20% of the world’s oil and liquefied natural gas used to transit, and prices on world markets have moved sharply. The US and Iran then agreed ceasefires in April and again in June, both aimed at facilitating negotiations to end the conflict. But the truce has been repeatedly broken and talks appear to have stalled.

Bessent’s comments fit a broader shift in Washington toward economic pressure. He said the US was not spelling out the full plan yet, but that the goal was to make pressure on Iran so severe that it would reduce the need for another military escalation. He also urged China to support the effort, saying Chinese energy needs made the Gulf important to them.

Oil prices, shipping and the stakes for Iran

The economic backdrop is part of the story. Middle East Eye reported that Bessent spoke as the war’s toll on energy markets was building. Brent crude was up 2% on Thursday, trading at $93.41 a barrel, and the BWET exchange-traded fund, which lets investors bet on shipping rates, had risen 98% over the past month. The war has also upended global shipping, with tanker charter costs jumping.

Bessent said he was not sure why oil had “popped up” when asked about the rise in prices. Iran has continued attacking vessels in the Strait of Hormuz, while the Houthis in Yemen have imposed a maritime blockade against Saudi Arabia in the Red Sea. Saudi Arabia, which relies on the waterway to export oil, has been forced to pipe crude to the Mediterranean via Egypt.

Iran’s economy has long been under sanctions, and the BBC noted that the government has not collapsed despite years of pressure. That history hangs over Bessent’s latest threat: Washington is promising a far more severe campaign, but the immediate question is whether it can force the result Trump wants without pushing the conflict back into war.

The window for measurement was tiny — about a billionth of a second — but the researchers were able to capture atomic structure, temperature, density and optical reflectivity, including X-ray diffraction signals.

The experiments also answered the Sandia puzzle differently than expected: the carbon stayed in the diamond structure until it melted, with no intermediate phase appearing. Millot said the sample may not have had time to change when it experienced only a single shock.

The practical implications are substantial. In inertial confinement fusion, the result suggests LLNL’s National Ignition Facility may be able to use slightly slower initial shocks and still fully melt the diamond capsule, making the fuel more compressible. Models predict that could potentially triple fusion energy gain if other loss processes are controlled. The improved melting measurements should also help planetary scientists model how Neptune and Uranus formed and how their interiors evolved.

LLNL says future work will use NIF to study diamond under even more extreme conditions, including later stages of fusion implosions and multiple shock waves.

What happened

University of Iowa researchers have shown that the frontoparietal cortex, the brain’s information hub, shifts how it talks to the rest of the brain during uncertain decisions, offering a closer look at how people sort out changing information and why the network matters.

The human brain is constantly processing huge amounts of information, and even familiar tasks like driving require several mental jobs at once: remembering the route, recalling how to control the vehicle, and reacting to surprises such as a road closure or shifting traffic. The frontoparietal cortex is thought to play a major role in that process because it receives signals from across the brain, decides what matters most, and helps coordinate the response.

Shifting connections under uncertainty

To study that system, the researchers combined computational modeling with brain imaging and focused on how the frontoparietal cortex behaves when people face uncertainty while making decisions. They recruited 38 participants between the ages of 18 and 35 and had them learn links between combinations of colors, faces and scenes and specific responses, including pressing a button with either the index or middle finger on either hand.

The researchers later changed those learned pairings. Participants then had to notice that the associations had shifted, learn the new ones and respond with the correct button, hand and finger. That change introduced uncertainty, allowing the team to track how the frontoparietal cortex adjusted its connections as people tried to figure out what had happened.

The findings suggest the network does not communicate with the rest of the brain in a fixed way. Instead, its connections shift according to the type of information needed during different stages of a decision. Kai Hwang, associate professor in the Department of Psychological and Brain Sciences and the study’s corresponding author, said the work shows in more detail how the frontoparietal cortex operates, including what kind of information it extracts from other systems and how it uses its connectivity pattern to integrate information coming from different areas of the brain.

Hwang said the team observed that the network dynamically changes how it communicates with other brain regions depending on what information is needed at each stage of a decision. The results could help guide future research into neurological and psychiatric conditions in which this exchange may function differently, including attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder and schizophrenia.
