Unmasked: the doctor who helps put prisoners to death
global_front_pagepolitics_and_powerpublic_healthmaterial_harmanchor
What happened

Mark Fowler, a Tennessee doctor with 25 years’ experience, walked into Riverbend maximum security prison’s death chamber and tried to help carry out an execution that ended in pain, blood and a shutdown attempt that has now pulled his role in lethal injections into public view.

Inside the death chamber

Fowler was called in after the execution team struggled to find a vein in Tony Carruthers, a 57-year-old man strapped to a gurney in Nashville. Under bright lights, people in medical gowns prepared needles and sterile tubing, and Fowler was asked to establish an IV line for pharmaceutical drugs. The setting looked like a hospital, but it was the death chamber at Riverbend maximum security prison.

The line Fowler was trying to place would have carried a lethally high dose of the sedative pentobarbital. Before he applied lidocaine, he asked whether “the patient” was allergic — a choice of words that stood out because Carruthers was not a patient and the room was not an operating room. Fowler then attempted to set a central line, an intravenous catheter inserted into the major vein below the collarbone, first numbing the area and then cutting with a scalpel and piercing the vein with a large needle.

Carruthers, who was condemned to death for a triple murder in 1994 that had no forensic evidence and that he has always said he did not commit, was forcibly strapped to the gurney. Standing a few feet away, his lawyer Maria DeLiberato said she recoiled when she heard the doctor refer to her client as “the patient.” “He called Tony ‘the patient’,” she said. “And he was trying to kill him.”

The doctor behind the mask

Fowler is described as a rare kind of execution physician: one whose identity has become public. In most cases, execution doctors are protected by secrecy laws, but Tennessee corrections officials inadvertently identified him when a warden announced the death of another prisoner, Byron Black, last August.

The disclosure has already had consequences. Two months after his name was released, Fowler was subpoenaed by lawyers challenging Tennessee’s lethal injection protocol. During that deposition, he warned his questioners that he hoped they would not leave him exposed in the press. Before the public could learn more, he declined to answer detailed questions by phone, saying the inmate was entitled to privacy and dismissing the lawyers’ concerns as “a lot of nonsense.”

Fowler’s path to the death chamber was unusual. He studied public administration, then law, and spent three years as a public defender before switching careers around 1994 and retraining as a doctor at the UT Memphis college of medicine. After graduating in 1999, he worked first as an ER physician, then as a family doctor. Today, much of his income comes from medical directorships, including weight loss and aesthetic clinics and a wound care facility.

His role in executions appears to be longstanding. In his deposition, Fowler said a previous Tennessee death penalty physician asked him in 2018 to accompany him to an execution so he could take over when the doctor retired. He is paid $3,000 for each execution he attends, far below the amounts cited for some other states. In an NBC News interview after the failed Carruthers execution, Fowler said: “Every attempt was made to minimize the defendant’s discomfort. No one wants Carruthers to suffer.”

Pain, objections and what comes next

Carruthers’ lawyers say what they saw did not look like minimization. DeLiberato said Fowler took over once he entered the chamber, directing the team and repeatedly trying to find a vein in Carruthers’ left foot before moving to the more difficult central line. She and other lawyers objected when they learned Fowler had said in deposition that he had not placed a central line in about 13 years. “We knew the last time he had set a central line was 13 years ago, so we said, ‘Object! He’s not qualified!’” said Kit Thomas, a deputy chief in Nashville’s capital habeas unit.

The warden overruled the objection and told Fowler to do his job. Fowler proceeded, but the effort failed after about an hour and 15 minutes, with repeated punctures in Carruthers’ arms, feet and shoulder. DeLiberato said she heard him moaning, “it hurts, it hurts,” and saw blood seeping from the puncture. The execution was then called off, and Carruthers was sent back to his cell.

Later, DeLiberato visited him and filed a court declaration saying he was sweaty, pale, tearful and in shock and pain, and could barely stand or walk. Carruthers told her Fowler “was hurting me, and knew he was hurting me.” His sister, Tonya Hervey, has since filed a complaint with state health authorities alleging Fowler violated medical ethics and caused her brother excruciating pain.

The failed execution has also sharpened questions around Tennessee’s next lethal injection. Darrell Hines is scheduled to be executed next Thursday, and his lawyers argued in court filings that if Fowler returned, the same botched procedure could happen again. They said there would be “an intolerable risk” that Hines would suffer because of “insufficiently qualified medical personnel.” On Thursday, the Tennessee supreme court denied a motion that would have forced the state to disclose whether Fowler would be on duty next week. For now, that leaves the doctor once more behind the curtain, even as the dispute over his role in executions moves into the open.

What happened

Heat-related illness cases in late summer have nearly tripled over the past decade, and this year’s heat is still expected to keep pushing patients into late August, with new data also showing a higher toll in deaths.

Late-summer cases have climbed

An analysis by a research institute under the interior ministry, using data from the Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency (KDCA), found an annual average of 215 heat-related patients from “ipchu” to the end of August in 2011-2015. Ipchu falls on Aug. 7 this year and marks the beginning of autumn on the traditional calendar.

The annual average then rose to 658 cases in 2016-2020 and 638 cases in 2021-2025. Deaths linked to heat-related illnesses also increased over the same period, from an annual average of 3.3 patients in 2012-2015 to more than four patients over the decade from 2016 to 2025.

The figures point to a clear late-summer burden even before the hottest stretch of the season is over. In 2024 alone, 1,299 people suffered from heat-related illnesses and 12 died from ipchu to late August.

This year’s heat has not eased

Heat waves are expected to continue until late August this year, with daytime highs forecast to range from 31-35 C across the country. On Friday alone, 202 people were treated at emergency rooms nationwide for heat-related illnesses, and three died, according to the KDCA.

The government has operated a heat-related illness monitoring system since May 15. Under that system, 3,089 heat-related illness cases have been reported, including 26 deaths.

The data suggest that late summer remains a particularly dangerous period, when the seasonal marker of ipchu has passed but high temperatures continue to strain health services and emergency rooms.
