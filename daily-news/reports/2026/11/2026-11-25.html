Land mine casualties soar in Syria, two years after war
global_front_pagematerial_harmeconomy_and_businessanchor
What happened

Explosive ordnance is killing and injuring people returning to homes and farmland across Syria, while also putting farming and recovery at risk. A recent blast in the north-west killed an 11-year-old girl and seriously injured her brother; United Nations figures show children make up a substantial share of the casualties recorded since Bashar al-Assad’s fall.

In Umm Khuzaym, the children were tending their family’s goats when they triggered a landmine in a field behind their home. Their father said clearance teams had visited several times, and residents believed the fields were safe. But mines and other explosives may be scattered well beyond former front lines, making it difficult to know which areas are clear, said Katrin Atkins, research lead at Landmine Monitor.

The figures cover different periods and monitoring records. Landmine Monitor recorded 1,602 people killed or injured by mines and explosive remnants in Syria during 2025, more than 50% above the previous year; only Myanmar recorded more. Separately, the UN Mine Action Service has logged at least 1,556 explosive-ordnance incidents since 8 December 2024, causing 2,701 casualties: 935 deaths and 1,766 injuries. Children account for 995 victims, about 37%. Experts estimate the overall toll may be under-reported by 25–50%.

The Landmine Monitor report attributes part of the rise to refugees and displaced people returning and using fields again. According to Simon Jackson, the Halo Trust’s Syria programme manager, farming, agriculture and shepherding are linked to over 60% of the casualties the clearance charity records. People also risk explosions while collecting scrap or salvaging materials.

Survivors can face lasting harm. Majid Abdul Rahman, 27, was injured when his car struck an explosive device near Damascus in March 2025. More than a year later, he still had difficulty walking and could not return to work, leaving him struggling to support his family.

The Defence Ministry says its teams have handled over 315,000 mines and unexploded munitions since late 2024. Without a countrywide assessment of contaminated land, the extent of the threat and its impediment to farming remain unknown.

An analysis by Russian opposition outlets Mediazona and Meduza estimates that more than 720,000 Russian troops have died since Russia’s full-scale invasion of Ukraine began in 2022, including deaths not yet recorded and people reported missing; the figure points to a far higher toll than confirmed records alone.

A separate report described the Rosstat count as approaching 500,000; that figure concerns recorded deaths, not the broader estimate.

Mediazona and Meduza said they accessed leaked Rosstat data after a breach attributed to the hacker groups Cyber Partisans of Belarus and Silent Crow. Mediazona’s database contains 160,000 people reported missing by relatives since 2023; the outlet says only about 10% of those reported missing survive. CNN said it could not independently verify the figures.

The data also indicate that casualties are unevenly distributed, with poorer areas and ethnic minorities experiencing significantly higher rates, according to the analysis. The NEST Centre, which also obtained the data, identified 6,947 records of foreign nationals killed in Ukraine, most from Kazakhstan, Uzbekistan, Tajikistan, Kyrgyzstan and Turkmenistan.

The estimate is not a count of confirmed deaths: it combines recorded losses with statistical projections and missing people assessed as likely dead. Mediazona and Meduza’s analysis puts the scale of Russian military deaths well above the figure available from recorded data alone.

Myanmar, Syria top list as global landmine casualties hit 5-year high of 7,000
global_front_pagematerial_harmpolitics_and_poweranchor
What happened

Landmine and unexploded-ordnance casualties worldwide reached 6,929 in 2025, the highest annual total in five years. Civilians made up about 84 percent of those killed or wounded, and children accounted for half of the casualties. Myanmar and Syria recorded the largest national death counts.

The International Campaign to Ban Landmines’ Landmine Monitor 2026 report found that Southeast Asia had the largest number of deaths, largely linked to Myanmar’s civil war. The 2025 tally in Myanmar was 1,891 deaths, the most of any country for a third year running, as both government forces and armed groups deployed landmines. The report found that an average of five people were killed or wounded by mines there each day, and that contamination affected some level of almost two-thirds of the country’s townships. Myanmar and nearby countries, including Cambodia, Laos and Vietnam, together registered 27 percent of worldwide mine deaths in 2025, although they account for only 8 percent of the world’s population.

Syria recorded 1,602 deaths in 2025, with casualties rising by more than 50 percent. The report linked the increase to civilians returning in large numbers after Bashar al-Assad’s removal in 2024. Yeshua Moser-Puangsuwan, a co-author of the report, said the returns were driving the toll and that Syria needed to expand mine-clearance capacity.

The report also highlighted pressure on the 1997 Mine Ban Treaty. Finland, Estonia, Latvia, Lithuania and Poland withdrew, citing regional security concerns; Ukraine notified the United Nations that it would suspend implementation, although the treaty’s provisions do not allow such a move. Landmine Monitor said Ukraine had also deployed mines; Russia, which is outside the treaty, has relied extensively on them during its invasion of Ukraine.
