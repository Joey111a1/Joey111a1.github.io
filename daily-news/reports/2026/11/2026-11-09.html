science_cs_aipolitics_and_powerglobal_south_and_exploitedpublic_healthclimate_disastertechnology_and_aiscience anchorpreprint_not_peer_reviewed
What happened

AI agents struggled to complete open-ended physics projects in a new benchmark spanning theory, computation and experiment: the strongest configuration passed only 46 of 168 attempts, with failures attributed mainly to weak scientific reasoning and expert judgment rather than basic physics knowledge.

FrontierPhysics, introduced in an arXiv preprint dated 4 November 2026, evaluates whether AI agents can carry out research from an initial high-level assignment through a scientific manuscript. The benchmark contains 56 tasks created by 42 physicists across nine areas of theoretical, computational and experimental physics. The tasks were drawn from the contributors’ own research projects and accepted after general review, domain review and analysis of early agent failures.

Unlike benchmarks that pose fixed questions, test isolated skills or provide a paper to reproduce, these tasks require agents to investigate the literature, choose a research approach, run computations or experiments, validate their findings and explain the results. Each task produces both machine-checkable outputs—such as calculated values, simulations or processed data—and a manuscript expected to present the motivation, methods, results and conclusions.

Testing research quality as well as correctness

The evaluation combines deterministic software checks with rubric-based review. Verifiers compare structured outputs against reference results, while reviewer agents assess the research process and manuscript against general and task-specific criteria written by domain experts. The rubrics examine issues including literature use, evidence-based claims, result validation, scientific presentation and attempts to exploit weaknesses in the evaluation.

A rollout passes only if it clears the deterministic tests and every required blocker criterion. Qualitative criteria then affect the overall publication-quality score. This design means that an agent can produce numerically correct results but still fail because its research is poorly motivated, unsupported or communicated. Conversely, a polished manuscript cannot compensate for an incorrect result or a failed essential requirement.

The benchmark’s accepted tasks came from 182 initial submissions. The authors report that 18 tasks were based on projects represented in papers co-authored by task contributors, including four connected to papers published in *Nature*. The original projects reportedly required an average of about 270 days per task and more than 8,000 hours of research in total. These figures describe the source projects used to construct the tasks, not time spent by the AI agents during evaluation.

The experiments covered 22 models in 26 configurations. Each model attempted all 56 tasks three times, with four GPT configurations also tested at a higher inference setting, producing 4,364 rollouts overall. Runs used several agent interfaces, including Codex CLI, Claude Code CLI, Grok Build and Antigravity CLI, with a uniform 10-hour limit for each rollout.

Low pass rates and recurring weaknesses

Overall pass rates remained below 30% for every tested configuration. Claude Opus 5.5 had the highest reported rate, passing 27.4% of its 168 attempts, or 46 rollouts. The result indicates that current systems can sometimes complete demanding research workflows, but usually do not satisfy all correctness and research-quality conditions in the benchmark.

The authors manually examined the trajectories and outputs from 168 GPT-6 Astra rollouts. Their analysis identified rigorous reasoning as the most frequent weakness, including circular self-checking and failure to account for edge cases. Agents could validate a mistaken assumption with a method that depended on that same assumption, or produce code that worked on expected cases while missing hidden ones.

Other common problems involved scientific judgment and literature work. Agents often failed to locate or synthesize key references, explain why a project mattered or support manuscript claims with appropriate evidence. They also struggled to identify the most important source of experimental error or to recognize the underlying cause of a debugging problem, even when they appeared to understand the relevant physics. Instruction-following failures were another reported category.

The researchers concluded that these systems’ main limitations were insufficiently rigorous thinking and limited expert intuition, rather than a general lack of physics knowledge or inability to execute tasks. The finding is based particularly on the manually analyzed GPT-6 Astra sample; the authors caution that other model-and-harness combinations may fail differently.

Reliability checks and the preprint’s status

The evaluation itself was not treated as error-free. During review, the researchers identified nine GPT-6 Astra rollouts that had been incorrectly failed by the verifier or rubric process. Correcting those cases raised that configuration’s reported pass rate from 14.9% to 20.2%. They also tested the grading rubrics against authors’ own papers or manuscripts for 33 tasks with suitable reference material; those materials averaged 96.2% under the corresponding rubric criteria.

FrontierPhysics v0.1 is presented in a preprint and has not been peer reviewed. Its results therefore describe the authors’ benchmark design, grading procedures and reported experiments rather than an independently validated measure of AI research ability. The paper argues that future evaluations will need more open-ended tasks and methods that can recognize partial progress, exploratory reasoning and unexpected scientific findings rather than judging only whether an agent reaches a predefined endpoint.

Random Is Almost Enough? Stable and Low-Cost Benchmark Compression via BestRand
science_cs_aipolitics_and_powerscience anchorpreprint_not_peer_reviewed
What happened

Evaluating large AI models on full benchmarks can be expensive, so researchers from Bosch Research and Carnegie Mellon University propose BestRand, a method that uses smaller test subsets while aiming to limit occasional large scoring errors.

A preprint dated 31 October 2026 examines benchmark compression: estimating how a model would perform on an entire benchmark by testing it on only a subset of items. George Ling, Shanshan Zhong, Lijin Yang, Hao Yang, Nanxiang Li and Zhongzhan Huang argue that ordinary random sampling is usually accurate, but not reliably so. Their analysis finds that most random subsets produce small errors, while a minority create much larger deviations, producing a long-tailed error distribution.

The researchers link this instability to correlations among foundation models. Models that share architectures, training data or other design characteristics may succeed and fail on many of the same items. As a result, evaluating more similar models does not necessarily eliminate the risk that a subset is unusually easy or difficult. Their theoretical treatment describes how subset size, model-score correlation and item difficulty influence this risk. The authors say numerical experiments broadly match the theory, while noting that the assumptions used are approximations to benchmark data.

BestRand preserves random sampling’s low-cost principle but adds a selection step. It creates multiple candidate subsets from a larger pool, runs a small number of inexpensive probe models over that pool, and chooses the candidate whose probe scores most closely represent the pool’s average. Target models are then evaluated only on the selected subset, avoiding a full-benchmark run for every candidate.

The preprint reports validation across more than 70 text, multimodal and agent benchmarks, with an extension to vision-language-action evaluation. Against the comparison methods used in the study, BestRand reduced high-error risk by 50.2% and average estimation error by 16.4%, while requiring 51.3% of their construction cost. The authors also report a 21.8% average reduction in out-of-distribution estimation error across changes in score range, model family and evaluation time.

These are results from a preprint rather than a peer-reviewed study. The authors say the code and data are publicly available on GitHub.

Turns Matter: Advancing Streaming Video RL via Turn-Level Credit
science_cs_aiglobal_south_and_exploitedpolitics_and_powerscience anchorpreprint_not_peer_reviewed
What happened

Streaming video models must repeatedly choose whether to answer or keep watching, but current reinforcement-learning methods can give every decision the same trajectory-level credit, rewarding redundant replies or penalizing useful follow-ups. TurnWise proposes a turn-specific alternative intended to improve both timing and response quality.

The method is presented in an arXiv version 1 preprint dated November 4, 2026. Researchers from institutions including Fudan University, Shanghai Innovation Institute, JD.com, Stanford University and others argue that streaming interaction differs from offline video understanding because each action changes the context for later decisions. A model may answer, or generate an explicit waiting action, at each point in an incoming video stream.

The work focuses on Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO). In the approach examined by the researchers, one advantage derived from the complete trajectory is applied across all turns. That can allow an early successful answer to raise the training signal for a later repetition, even when repetition is penalized by the overall reward. Conversely, a useful follow-up may inherit a negative signal after earlier mistakes.

TurnWise separates those decisions in two ways. First, it estimates each turn’s remaining return by retaining the actions taken up to that point and treating later decisions as silence, then comparing the resulting score with the full trajectory. This removes reward already accrued before the current decision from its learning signal. Second, its turn-mean optimization averages token losses within each turn and then averages across turns, so a long textual response does not receive more weight merely because it contains more tokens than a short waiting action. The framework uses neither a learned critic nor additional rollouts.

The researchers report that TurnWise outperforms earlier reinforcement-learning approaches in experiments on ProactiveVQA, OVO-Timing and StreamingBench, using two Qwen backbones. They also report that turn-mean aggregation improves response timing and reduces repetition compared with using turn-level credit alone. These are claims from the preprint’s experiments; the paper has not been peer reviewed.

Scientists squeeze record-breaking X-ray power into an incredibly tiny spot
science_generalscience anchorattributed source material (republished)
What happened

Researchers at European XFEL produced hard-X-ray pulses lasting about 300 attoseconds and focused them into a spot measuring 180 by 330 nanometres. The result set a reported record for pulse intensity and fluence, potentially enabling observations of electron motion during chemical reactions.

The experiment took place in April 2026 at the European XFEL facility, using hard X-rays—light with extremely short wavelengths. An advanced Kirkpatrick-Baez (AKB) optical system concentrated each pulse through four reflections: one mirror focused the beam vertically and another horizontally. The resulting focal area was roughly 100,000 times smaller than the cross-section of a typical human hair.

The researchers tested the beam on a thin copper sheet. The X-rays removed electrons from copper atoms’ innermost shells, leaving vacancies that other electrons filled while emitting further X-rays. At the achieved intensity, this emission became amplified spontaneous emission, with the light increasingly directed forward rather than spreading in many directions. The signal also eventually saturated as pulse energy rose, a pattern consistent with exceptionally intense illumination.

These observations provided evidence supporting peak intensities above 10²⁰ watts per square centimetre, calculated from the pulse energy, duration and focal-spot size. Ichiro Inoue of the University of Tokyo, the study’s first author, described the result as a record for a hard-X-ray attosecond pulse.

The reported findings have been published in *Optica*. The immediate scientific value is the ability to study nonlinear interactions between intense X-rays and matter, including how atoms and electrons respond under extreme conditions. Researchers say the technique could eventually help them observe electron dynamics inside atoms and molecules and perhaps influence electron motion during chemical reactions.

The AKB system used for the experiment was transported from Japan. European XFEL expects its own system in early 2027, after which researchers using its MID instrument are expected to gain access to similarly brief, intense X-ray pulses.

Stronger flu shot linked to nearly 55% lower Alzheimer's risk
science_generalpublic_healthpolitics_and_powerscience anchorinstitutional_press_release
What happened

A study led by UTHealth Houston researchers links high-dose influenza vaccination with nearly 55% lower Alzheimer’s risk among adults 65 and older, compared with standard-dose vaccination. The finding suggests a possible connection between vaccine-activated immune responses and brain health, but does not establish that the stronger shot prevents the disease.

Researchers analyzed data from nearly 200,000 older men and women who received either a high-dose or standard-dose flu vaccine. Those given the high-dose formulation were less likely to develop Alzheimer’s disease, the researchers reported in *Neurology*. By contrast, earlier research had tied standard-dose vaccination to a 40% reduction, while this analysis found an association of nearly 55% with the high-dose shot.

The comparison became possible partly because demand for high-dose vaccines can exceed supply, meaning some older adults receive standard-dose shots instead. Designed for older adults with less responsive immune systems, the high-dose formulation delivers four times the influenza antigen found in standard-dose versions and aims to elicit a stronger immune response. According to the institutional account, the CDC lists high-dose influenza vaccines as a preferred choice for adults aged 65 and above.

The association appeared particularly strong among women. Researchers said the results add to earlier work from the same UTHealth Houston team, which linked inactivated influenza vaccination with a lower risk of Alzheimer’s. The newer study was prompted by questions from local public health officials about whether vaccine dosage might affect that relationship.

The findings matter in the context of Alzheimer’s disease, which the account describes as the leading cause of dementia. In 2025, the disease affected over 7 million Americans at least 65 years old, or roughly one in nine in that age group; projections indicate the figure could more than double by 2050.

The researchers emphasized that their results show an association, not cause and effect. The study does not establish that receiving a high-dose flu vaccine directly prevents Alzheimer’s, and further research is needed to determine whether the immune response to vaccination affects biological processes involved in the disease or whether other factors explain the difference.

Mysterious gas leaks suggest Mars may still be volcanically alive
science_generalclimate_disasterscience anchorattributed source material (republished)
What happened

Hydrogen chloride detected above fractures near Mars’s Alba Mons volcano, together with surface changes seen from orbit, suggests heat and volcanic processes may persist underground. The finding could identify hidden pathways for gases—and possibly environments with chemical energy—without showing that Mars is erupting today.

Researchers led by Alexander Trokhimovskiy found unusually high concentrations of hydrogen chloride in early summer over fractures on the slopes of Alba Mons. That timing is significant because Martian hydrogen chloride generally peaks during northern winter and falls to very low levels in summer, a pattern associated partly with dust and water cycles. The repeated appearance of the gas in the same volcanic region therefore points to a source beyond ordinary seasonal atmospheric chemistry.

The data were gathered by the European Space Agency’s ExoMars Trace Gas Orbiter. Its Atmospheric Chemistry Suite detected the gas in a relatively small area after winter frost disappeared, while the orbiter’s CaSSIS camera showed narrow, apparently fresh fractures—some extending hundreds of metres—and unusual colour changes around them.

The researchers suggest that the cracks may channel gases upward from an inactive magma chamber located below Alba Mons. One possible mechanism is seasonal: Martian winter temperatures can fall to about -120°C, allowing ice to seal the fractures. When temperatures rise, the ice may sublimate, reopening routes for gases to escape. The discoloured material around the fractures could consist of minerals deposited by those emissions, analogous to deposits around volcanic gas vents on Earth.

Researchers examined atmospheric chemical processes that could produce hydrogen chloride without volcanism, but the evidence led them to favour a connection between the gas and a subsurface volcanic system. Daniel Mège, who co-led the *Science Advances* research, said the results suggest Mars kept sufficient internal heat for volcanic activity until the recent past and may retain it today.

The evidence neither demonstrates an eruption is happening now nor confirms life beneath Mars’s surface. No new eruption or fresh lava flow has been directly observed, although orbital images indicate that some Martian volcanoes erupted within the past few million years and surface instruments have recorded marsquakes.

If hydrothermal systems remain active where underground heat meets groundwater, they could provide warmth, chemical energy and protection from Mars’s hostile surface conditions. That makes the Alba Mons fractures a potential target in the search for microbial habitats. The immediate scientific significance is narrower: the gas and fracture observations offer a possible indicator of hidden volcanic activity that future investigations must test.

Scientists discover hidden brain changes linked to common sleep disorders
science_generalpublic_healthscience anchorinstitutional_press_release
What happened

An analysis of 57 adult brain-imaging studies links several sleep disorders with structural differences in regions involved in attention, decision-making and emotional control, offering possible clues to everyday cognitive effects while leaving the direction of the relationship unresolved.

Researchers at Florida International University’s Center for Children and Families compared findings across multiple disorders rather than examining one condition at a time. The work, published in *Scientific Reports*, covered dyssomnias such as insomnia and sleep apnea, as well as parasomnias including sleepwalking, nightmare disorder and sleep terrors.

Across both broad groups, the analysis found reductions in the thalamus, a deep-brain structure involved in processing information and sustaining attention. Affected changes included the pulvinar, which helps direct attention and support cognitive control. The researchers said these differences could help explain problems such as slower reactions, impaired decision-making and more errors, although the analysis identified associations rather than proving that disordered sleep causes the structural changes.

The patterns were not identical across conditions. Parasomnias were also associated with changes in the posterior cingulate cortex, which is involved in motivation, decision-making and emotional regulation. The analysis did not find a consistent structural pattern unique to dyssomnias.

The findings could eventually support earlier identification of sleep problems and treatments tailored to the brain systems associated with particular disorders. They may also help researchers investigate whether interventions should address cognitive and emotional effects as well as sleep quality.

The direction of the relationship remains an open question: disrupted sleep might contribute to brain differences, pre-existing differences might increase vulnerability to sleep disorders, or both processes could occur. The FIU team plans to study individual parasomnias in more detail, including sleepwalking and REM sleep behavior disorder.

A nuclear blast could save Earth from an asteroid, but there's a catch
science_generalclimate_disasterscience anchorattributed source material (republished)
What happened

Researchers led by Sandia National Laboratories physicist Nathan Moore have modeled how an asteroid’s irregular shape could determine whether a nuclear X-ray blast deflects it from Earth—or produces far less useful movement. The simulations show that blast direction can change both the asteroid’s trajectory and rotation substantially.

A nuclear explosion would not need to destroy an incoming asteroid to protect Earth. In the scenario studied, its X-rays heat parts of the surface, vaporizing material and creating a recoil force that nudges the asteroid onto a safer path. The effectiveness of that push depends on where the radiation strikes.

The study, published in *Icarus*, examined irregular asteroid shapes rather than treating the objects as smooth spheres. In one simulation, irradiation from one direction changed an asteroid’s speed by 3.9 millimeters per second, while radiation from another direction produced a change of 0.8 millimeters per second—nearly a fivefold difference in deflection velocity. For another simulated asteroid, the change in rotation varied by a factor of 17 depending on the X-ray direction.

That variation matters because a planetary-defense mission would need to redirect an asteroid without breaking it into multiple dangerous fragments. A single large object may be easier to manage than several pieces that could remain on threatening trajectories. The model estimates how much surface material would vaporize and calculates the resulting push, rotational change and surface damage.

To make those calculations practical, Moore and colleagues combined Sandia’s ALEGRA code, used for fast, high-energy experiments, with SPARTA, a direct simulation Monte Carlo code for weaker, slowly changing forces. The researchers say the resulting algorithm is more than 1,600 times faster than earlier approaches, allowing detailed three-dimensional asteroid shapes to be modeled alongside the relevant physical calculations.

The simulations remain limited. The model can represent rock breakup at the surface but cannot yet accurately predict whether the entire asteroid would fragment. It also does not capture every interaction triggered by an intense X-ray burst, making the behavior of a real asteroid difficult to forecast precisely.

A future mission would require detailed observations of an asteroid’s shape from multiple directions, as well as highly precise placement of a nuclear device. If an object were discovered on a collision course with limited warning, obtaining that information and positioning the device could be difficult. The research therefore offers a tool for assessing one possible defense strategy, not a demonstrated operational solution.

Ancient Siberian tomb reveals surprising secrets of cannabis use 2,800 years ago
science_generalglobal_south_and_exploitedpolitics_and_powerclimate_disastereconomy_and_businessscience anchorinstitutional_press_release
What happened

Researchers investigating an elaborate Scythian tomb in Tyva, southern Siberia, found about 1,000 cannabis seeds and cannabinoid traces in human hair. Together, the evidence suggests the plant was used for intoxicating effects during burial rituals around the early eighth century BC, while leaving its precise method and potency unresolved.

Gino Caspari led an international team that recovered the seeds from the nearly 2,800-year-old tomb; he is affiliated with Bern's university and the Max Planck Institute of Geoanthropology. Radiocarbon dating of six seeds, combined with other dating evidence, placed their deposition in the early eighth century BC. Prominent Scythians were buried there, as members of a culture associated with the Eurasian steppe.

The seeds alone could not show whether cannabis was used for intoxication: they may have come from wild plants, and ancient communities also used cannabis for fiber and oil. Stronger evidence came from human hair preserved in the central burial chamber. High-resolution mass spectrometry detected chemical signals consistent with cannabinol, which can form when THC oxidizes, along with weaker signals matching THC. The researchers said these results indicate cannabis use, but cannot establish how much was consumed or whether it was smoked, eaten or taken another way.

The plant remains were found within an elaborate ceremonial setting. Earlier work identified 18 deliberately positioned horses, while later excavations found several dozen more sacrificed horses and scattered human bones. The team led by Caspari says that, jointly, the seed and hair findings are the earliest currently known directly dated archaeological evidence of cannabis use linked to intoxication.

The findings do not show how potent the ancient cannabis was or whether Scythians cultivated varieties for higher THC levels. Researchers are now sequencing exceptionally well-preserved plant DNA to investigate cannabis domestication and its spread.
