‘Risk multiplier’: super El Niño looms over Cop31 as climate pattern fuels global disasters
global_front_pagematerial_harmanchor
What happened

A super El Niño event, described as the strongest in at least 1,000 years, is adding urgency to climate-finance negotiations at COP31 in Turkey. Its weather effects have already been linked to wildfires and crop failures in Indonesia, coastal inundation in the United States, drought in Sudan, and flooding in Ecuador and Somalia. In the Marshall Islands, drought has struck a country heavily dependent on rainfall for water, while rising seas continue to threaten homes and drinking supplies; the government has declared a state of emergency.

The most severe consequences are forecast as El Niño peaks in December and affects the following year. Expected impacts include heatwaves, crop shortages, excessive or insufficient rainfall, coral bleaching and more humanitarian emergencies. The UNHCR forecasts up to 3 million displacements across Kenya, Somalia, South Sudan and Uganda, while maize production in Kenya is already reported to be down 40%. Wealthy countries agreed last year to triple adaptation finance for vulnerable nations, but only a fraction of the $305 billion needed annually by 2035 is being delivered. Marshall Islands climate envoy Kathy Jetn̄il-Kijiner said the country has limited capacity to respond as the drought compounds existing pressures.

World’s top court to rule if Myanmar committed genocide against Rohingya
global_front_pagepolitics_and_powermaterial_harmanchor
What happened

The International Court of Justice (ICJ) is due to deliver its judgment on Thursday on whether Myanmar violated the 1948 Genocide Convention through its treatment of the Rohingya, a decision that could shape accountability for survivors and influence other genocide litigation.

Judges in The Hague have spent seven years considering allegations brought by Gambia, which says Myanmar’s repression of the Muslim minority breached the convention. No state has previously been held responsible for genocide by the court. One pivotal legal issue is whether the evidence establishes the requisite intent to destroy all or part of the Rohingya.

The case concerns the military campaign that drove hundreds of thousands of Rohingya into Bangladesh nearly a decade ago. Legal Action Worldwide, which represents survivors, says repeated attacks, village burnings, killings and sexual violence indicate a coordinated attempt to destroy the group. Rohingya witnesses who travelled from camps in Bangladesh described killings and mass graves during closed-door hearings in January. Myanmar denies committing genocide and says its operations targeted an insurgency.

Bangladesh hosts over 1 million Rohingya, whereas estimates put the population still in Myanmar below 600,000. Supporters of the case say a finding against Myanmar should pressure states to restrict arms transfers and jet-fuel supplies, although the ICJ lacks direct enforcement powers. The judgment is also expected to carry significance for South Africa’s separate genocide case against Israel.

The creeks are dry, rain is contaminated and food scarce. What lies ahead for the people of PNG’s Ahus Island?
global_south_and_exploitedmaterial_harmanchor
What happened

Papua New Guinea’s Ahus Island is facing an immediate water and food emergency after extreme heat and drought disrupted fishing, emptied wells and contaminated rainfall. Researchers say the crisis is leaving households hungry, shortening school days and undermining a community’s long-standing efforts to adapt to climate change.

During a research visit in October, Ahus appeared deserted. Josh Cinner, director of the University of Sydney’s Thriving Oceans Research Hub, said residents were lying in their homes to conserve energy and water. School had been reduced to half days because of the heat, while fishing had fallen sharply: people lacked both the water and energy needed for the physically demanding work.

The drought is affecting the wider region of Papua New Guinea. On neighbouring Manus Island, markets have little food available, while Ahus fishers have few catches to trade. The islands’ interconnected economy, based partly on exchanging fish and other goods, is therefore breaking down.

When rain eventually arrived, smoke from bushfires in Borneo left it too contaminated for villagers to collect safely, Cinner said. They disconnected their water-catchment systems, while wells remained dry. Some residents have had to cross the channel to seek water on Manus, although they do not have rights to the mainland’s supplies; local creeks there are also drying.

A recent survey by the researchers found that only 7% of Ahus households had enough drinking water, while 81% said they could not cope with climate change. Michelle Suri, who lives on the island, said almost every household was facing hunger as heat and water shortages reduced fishing, gleaning and other daily activities. Some people have stayed in the sea during the day to keep cool and conserve energy.

The community had spent years preparing, including by building sea walls, installing water tanks and working with development and conservation organisations to diversify livelihoods. But the severity of the current drought has shaken confidence in those measures. Cinner said relocation options were limited because most land in Papua New Guinea is held under customary ownership, leaving Ahus residents with few alternatives if conditions continue to worsen.

What happened

China is expanding care for preterm babies through prenatal risk screening, neonatal intensive treatment, specialized nursing and follow-up services. Preterm birth—before 37 completed weeks—can involve breathing, infection and nutritional risks; the WHO estimates that 15 million babies are born preterm each year.

The National Health Commission reported an infant mortality rate of 3.8 deaths per 1,000 live births in 2025; China aims to reduce it below 3.5 by 2030.

China: Promoting New Energy Storage to Enter the Market-Oriented Development Stage (Ruicai Jing)
china_domesticpolitics_and_powereconomy_and_businesstechnology_and_ai_societyregional expansion
What happened

China has set out a plan to move its new energy storage industry into market-oriented development, with proposed expansion for renewable-energy bases and power grids intended to improve electricity integration, supply assurance and system stability.

The National Development and Reform Commission and National Energy Administration recently jointly issued an Implementation Plan for the 15th Five-Year Plan, shifting storage from a scale-up phase toward market-oriented development. The plan covers scale, applications, technology, market mechanisms, industry management and industrial development.

The policy builds on rapid expansion already reported in China. By the end of 2025, installed new energy storage capacity had reached 136 million kilowatts, more than 40 times the capacity added by the end of the 13th Five-Year Plan. Projects using compressed air, flow batteries, sodium-ion batteries and flywheels had come online, while grid-forming storage projects had also been implemented. Storage utilization reached 1,195 hours in 2025, supporting renewable-energy consumption, power supply and grid stability.

The plan sets a 2030 target of 300 million kilowatts of national storage capacity, with an average duration of about three hours. It proposes roughly 50 million to 60 million kilowatts in new-energy bases and about 140 million kilowatts of grid-side independent storage capable of supporting peak demand.

Its proposed applications include desert and offshore wind-power bases, wind-solar-hydrogen projects, distribution networks, microgrids, virtual power plants, direct green-electricity supply and zero-carbon industrial parks. On technology, it calls for continued lithium-ion development, wider use of sodium-ion storage, and the transition of compressed-air, flow-battery and flywheel systems from pilot to commercial applications.

A National Energy Administration official said the period presents both opportunities and challenges. The authorities also plan to strengthen implementation guidance and follow-up assessment while improving policies supporting the industry.

The study, published in *Cell*, examined how its availability affects the immune system’s ability to identify abnormal or infected cells. In laboratory experiments, arginine shortages disrupted production of MHC-I proteins, which display cellular material to T cells. When fewer of these molecular signals reached the cell surface, cancerous or virus-infected cells could become harder for immune defenses to detect.

The researchers then tested dietary arginine in mouse disease models. Mice given more arginine developed fewer colon cancer tumors and experienced less severe influenza and SARS-CoV-2 infections than mice on lower-arginine diets. Additional arginine also improved outcomes when it was given after influenza infection had already begun, rather than only before exposure.

The team says a moderate amount comparable to that in a couple of over-the-counter supplement tablets restored MHC-I production in its experiments. The findings raise the possibility of testing arginine alongside cancer immunotherapies or for people at elevated risk from respiratory viruses. The researchers also point to aging and inadequate nutrition as situations in which arginine availability may decline.

However, the reported benefits were observed in cells and mice. The evidence does not establish that arginine supplements prevent or treat cancer, influenza, or COVID-19 in humans. Further human research would be needed to determine whether the immune effects and disease outcomes translate into effective or safe treatments.

A common osteoporosis drug blocked spinal damage in a surprising new study

What happened

Researchers at the Universities of Edinburgh and Bristol found that a bisphosphonate, a drug class already used to treat osteoporosis, prevented abnormal spinal mineral buildup in genetically altered zebrafish. The early finding points to possible treatments for disc degeneration, but has not yet been confirmed in humans.

In the study, published in *Communications Biology*, the researchers examined zebrafish bred without a functioning copy of a gene linked to collagen IX, a protein that helps maintain the structure of spinal discs. As the fish aged, the tissues between their vertebrae began to deteriorate, followed by mineral deposits and fusion between neighboring vertebrae.

The sequence offered a possible explanation for how spinal degeneration develops. The supporting layer around the developing spine began breaking down before mineralization appeared, suggesting that loss of this framework may trigger later hardening. The researchers also found changes in fat metabolism, phosphate regulation, vitamin A signaling and mTOR, a system involved in cell growth and nutrient responses.

A possible treatment target, not yet a human therapy

When the team tested interventions, a bisphosphonate blocked the abnormal mineral buildup in the fish. Reducing the animals’ food intake and using drugs that suppressed fat metabolism also reduced spinal fusion. These results identify several biological processes that could be explored in future treatments, rather than demonstrating that any of these approaches will work in people.

Spinal disc degeneration affects the flexible structures that cushion the vertebrae and can contribute to pain, stiffness and reduced mobility. The evidence says no medication currently stops or reverses the degenerative process, leaving surgery as the established long-term option for some severe cases.

The researchers said the findings could help explain why genetic changes lead to spinal damage and show how zebrafish can be used to investigate the disease and test potential therapies. However, the experiments were conducted in fish, and the evidence does not establish that the same mechanisms operate in humans or that osteoporosis drugs can safely slow spinal degeneration.

Vitamin C could help block cancer-linked chemicals in your stomach, study finds
science_generalpublic_healthscience anchorinstitutional_press_release
What happened

University of Waterloo researchers used a mathematical model to examine how vitamin C may affect reactions involving nitrates and nitrites in the digestive system. Nitrates and nitrites are present in foods such as bacon and salami, and they also occur in fruits and vegetables grown where soil or water has been polluted. In the stomach, they can participate in nitrosation, a process that produces chemicals researchers suspect may contribute to cancer development.

The model simulated interactions across the salivary glands, stomach, small intestine and blood plasma. According to the simulations, vitamin C eaten alongside foods containing either nitrate or nitrite could limit the production of compounds that may be harmful. Simulations also indicated that taking vitamin C after meals could moderately reduce nitrosation products, particularly after eating processed meats. The paper in the *Journal of Theoretical Biology* suggests that the amount of vitamin C consumed, eating schedule, gastric conditions and microbes in the mouth may help account for differing dietary-risk findings. However, the results come from mathematical simulations, not clinical trials, and further research is needed to establish whether the predicted chemical changes reduce cancer risk in people.

Empathy-Aware Relational Graph Networks With Self-Distillation for Multimodal Emotion Recognition in Conversation

What happened

The proposed EASDG framework addresses multimodal emotion recognition in conversation by representing dialogue as a relational graph that couples temporal dependence with cross-modal interaction. It adds node-level empathy embeddings to provide cues for affective responses and emotional transitions between speakers, while targeting limitations in methods that mainly integrate modalities at the perception level and do not fully model empathy consistency or heterogeneous modality contributions.

EASDG also uses graph-based self-distillation: single-modal subnetworks provide complementary supervision for the multimodal graph to improve modality-specific representations. Experiments on the IEMOCAP benchmark reached weighted F1 scores of 86.00% and 72.49% in the four-way and six-way tasks, respectively, while the MELD result reached 67.53%. The evidence describes these results as superior performance and says ablation analysis confirmed a synergistic effect between empathy embeddings and self-distillation in improving multimodal emotion-recognition performance.

Scientists discover a bizarre paradox among endangered penguins
science_generalpolitics_and_powerclimate_disasterscience anchorinstitutional_press_release
What happened

A University of Otago-led study has found sharply diverging outcomes among New Zealand’s endangered Erect-crested penguins: one island population has remained stable, while another has fallen by more than 40%, exposing how species-wide averages can conceal a serious conservation crisis.

Erect-crested penguins breed only on the Bounty and Antipodes island groups, about 800 kilometers southeast of New Zealand’s South Island. Researchers used drone surveys from 2022 to 2024 to photograph and count breeding colonies across both groups, producing what the study describes as the first comprehensive survey of the species. The research was published in *PLOS ONE*.

The Bounty Islands, covering just 50 hectares, held about 27,800 breeding pairs—nearly as many as the roughly 29,800 pairs on the Antipodes Islands, which are 40 times larger. Historical comparisons found that the Bounty population has stayed essentially unchanged for at least 25 years, while the Antipodes population has declined by more than 40% since 1995.

Researchers believe several pressures may be interacting at the Antipodes. Skuas prey on penguin eggs and chicks, and shrinking colonies may have weakened the birds’ collective defense. Klemens Pütz of the Antarctic Research Trust suggested that the collapse of Eastern Rockhopper penguins, leaving fewer than 1,000 breeding pairs on the Antipodes, may have pushed skuas toward Erect-crested penguins. More destructive storms, landslides and different feeding routes could add to the disparity: Antipodes penguins travel into the Antarctic Polar Frontal Zone, while Bounty birds remain closer to potentially more sheltered waters.

The researchers call this split “Schrödinger’s Penguin.” They warn that combining both populations could improve the species’ overall classification from Endangered to Vulnerable, masking the Antipodes decline and the Bounty population’s exposure to industrial fishing.
