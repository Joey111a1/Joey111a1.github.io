What happened

More than 5,000 people have died in the Democratic Republic of Congo’s ongoing Ebola outbreak, which has affected eight of 26 provinces and produced more than 10,300 confirmed cases, while suspected infections in northeast Nigeria have prompted a new public-health investigation.

The outbreak emerged in the DRC in May and became the country’s deadliest in its history in August, public health officials said. The health ministry said its epicentre remains in Ituri province in the northeast, a conflict zone that makes containment harder.

Government Ministry of Health data put the toll at 5,026 deaths among 10,351 infected people, a lethality rate of nearly 50 per cent. The Bundibugyo strain is responsible for the outbreak; no approved treatment or vaccine is available for it, although vaccine trials are under way.

The response in the DRC is complicated by gaps in detection and contact tracing: authorities said only about 12 per cent of confirmed cases had been identified among previously known contacts, while many deaths occurred in communities. Health workers have also faced attacks, including the killing of a nurse responding to an Ebola alert and the deaths of two Congolese doctors earlier in the week.

The WHO office in Africa called for stronger control measures, improved access to affected areas and expanded community outreach. Testing, treatment and vaccine trials are under way as health authorities respond to transmission spanning the DRC and other African countries.

The process begins with an input image. The updater selectively adds new Gaussian primitives where the current memory does not adequately cover the observed scene. It identifies areas with insufficient opacity or high reconstruction error, then allocates new memory there. This is intended to make memory scale mainly with the spatial extent of the world being explored, rather than with the number of generated frames.

The system also stores VAE latent features alongside the geometry. A jet traveling farther through the collision’s dense center should lose more energy than one escaping near the edge. This imbalance, known as jet quenching, is a key indicator that the particles crossed QGP.

The STAR analysis found a 20% reduction in high-energy particles associated with this energy loss. The suppression was weaker than in gold-nucleus collisions, as expected from oxygen’s smaller size, but the researchers said it was still clear evidence that QGP had formed.

Oxygen nuclei contain eight protons and eight neutrons, making them substantially smaller than the gold nuclei traditionally used at RHIC. Their relatively spherical shape also made it easier to compare direct, central collisions with grazing impacts, which produced fewer particles and were not expected to generate the same plasma.

The result matters because earlier studies of small collision systems had found coordinated particle motion, or collective flow, without establishing whether QGP was responsible. Jet quenching provides a more definitive test, according to the researchers. The new measurement therefore places a lower boundary on the conditions known to produce this primordial form of matter.

Oxygen collisions at CERN’s Large Hadron Collider have also shown collective flow and jet quenching, but those experiments used energies above 5,000 billion electron volts—far higher than RHIC’s 200 billion. Comparing the two facilities may help physicists determine how collision energy affects the temperature, density and persistence of the plasma.

The findings were published in *Physical Review Letters*.
