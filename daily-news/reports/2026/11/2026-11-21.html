‘They’re struggling for oxygen’: children pay the price as countries allow measles to surge again
global_front_pagematerial_harmpolitics_and_poweranchor
What happened

Measles is returning in countries that had brought it under control, putting children at risk of severe illness and death. The loss of elimination status in the US, Mexico and Bolivia highlights a wider decline in vaccination, while outbreaks also threaten children too young or medically unable to be vaccinated.

International health officials said this week that the three countries had lost measles elimination status after the same strain circulated within their borders uninterrupted for more than a year. Earlier this year, elimination status was also lost by Uzbekistan, Spain, Armenia, Austria, Canada, the UK and Azerbaijan. In Kampala, Uganda, paediatrician Eric Wobudeya says measles has returned to his hospital after a period of about a decade without cases; he treats infants whose lungs and airways have been affected, including by pneumonia.

WHO figures put the 2024 global toll at an estimated 11 million infections, with children younger than five making up most cases, and 95,000 deaths. Infection can weaken a child’s immune system for months or years and lead to complications including brain damage, hearing loss or blindness. Since March, Bangladesh has recorded nearly 240,000 suspected measles cases and 1,282 deaths in the outbreak.

Worldwide, vaccination levels remain short of 95%, the threshold for preventing outbreaks. Among eligible children, first-dose coverage stood at 84% in 2025, compared with 77% for a second dose. Experts point to misinformation and Covid-era disruption to routine services, but also to practical barriers such as appointment access, language and unstable housing. In Hackney, east London, 58% of five-year-olds beginning school in 2025 had completed the two-dose MMR course. Children who are too young or medically unable to receive the vaccine depend on broad community coverage for protection.

Experts fear that aid cuts by wealthy countries, including the US and UK, could worsen access in poorer countries. Since 2022, the Reach consortium has vaccinated more than one million children in areas that humanitarian teams had been unable to access in Somalia, South Sudan, Sudan, Ethiopia, Nigeria and Chad.

What happened

Colette Woronoff, 92, died after suffering severe hyperthermia in her Besançon apartment during France’s 2026 heatwave. Her family had tried to help her cope with the heat; a modelling study estimates at least 50,000 heat-related deaths across 854 European cities that summer.

At about 1 a.m., Woronoff’s father called their child to say she had fallen and was convulsing. An emergency doctor found her temperature was 42.6C and warned that her condition was fatal. Hospital staff cooled her with ice baths, and her temperature fell, but doctors told the family that the heat had damaged her heart, liver, lungs and brain. She died on Wednesday, after being admitted on Sunday.

The apartment had no air conditioning, though windows on opposite sides could be opened to create a breeze. Her family had reminded her to drink and shower, and a sister had visited that Saturday. In retrospect, the family member recounting her death said Woronoff had opened the windows but not the shutters, and had not followed the cooling advice. They wondered when guidance for heatwaves should include taking vulnerable people’s temperatures.

Woronoff was still independent in many ways and had few health problems beyond walking with a cane. Her family lived nearby, and care workers visited regularly, but they had no suitable room to move her into: the available rooms in their homes were up stairs she could not manage. The account also describes funeral homes as overwhelmed by the number of deaths.

A modelling study of 854 European cities estimated that at least 50,000 people died because of the 2026 summer heat. The scientists said two-thirds of heat-related deaths resulted from additional warming caused by fossil-fuel pollution and destruction of nature. The study’s estimate gives a wider scale to the heatwave in which Woronoff died.

What happened

In Bunia, eastern Democratic Republic of the Congo, Sylvain Kabona Tibasima is trying to protect his surviving relatives after Ebola killed 46 members of his extended family. His efforts to promote precautions unfold amid continuing infections, displacement and distrust that complicate containment.

The outbreak is caused by the Bundibugyo strain. Six months into the epidemic, more than 11,000 cases and nearly 5,400 deaths had been reported in DR Congo; aid groups said the actual toll could be higher. According to the Africa Centres for Disease Control and Prevention, people under 18 make up over one-third of those who have died. The World Health Organization reports a fatality rate of close to 50% for the strain; no targeted therapy or licensed vaccine is available, although clinical trials are ongoing. Health authorities have recorded infections in Kenya and Nigeria as well.

Tibasima’s sister Sylvie died a week after being diagnosed with Ebola on the day her daughter Birungi was born. The newborn contracted the virus and died in early September. Tibasima, 44, says some relatives initially believed the disease was caused by sorcery, while visits to sick people and touching bodies before burial helped spread infection. Trained in prevention through his work as a driver for Welthungerhilfe, he warns relatives about transmission. His own household avoids gatherings and funerals, keeps its distance from others and uses frequent handwashing and disinfectant; his six children have remained healthy.

The family recently feared that his 18-year-old daughter Malaika had Ebola after she developed possible symptoms. Tibasima took her to a treatment centre, while the rest of the household isolated at home. She tested negative. His children now spend much of their time indoors, worried as friends and cousins die.

Containment is also difficult in a city affected by conflict and displacement. Welthungerhilfe’s Ebola coordinator says insecurity, displacement and suspicion of authorities hinder diagnosis and contact tracing. Since the outbreak started in May, records show over 80 assaults on medical personnel and centres providing treatment. New cases are still being reported at 400 to 500 a week, though infections have started to decline in some areas, including Bunia; the outbreak remains far from controlled.

What happened

At COP31 in Antalya, Turkey, negotiators entered the conference’s expected final day with references to fossil fuels and climate-adaptation finance removed from a draft deal, while an electrification target remained. The changes leave the strength of the agreement—and the support it may offer vulnerable countries—at issue.

Delegates from more than 190 countries, excluding the United States, which has withdrawn from the climate effort, had spent two weeks negotiating. The first draft, published Monday, called for an immediate shift away from fossil fuels; by Thursday, that language had been removed. Environmental advocates condemned the omission, while oil-producing countries have resisted references to fossil fuels. At COP28 in 2023, countries agreed to call for a transition away from them, but that language has not since been repeated or strengthened.

A proposed $120 billion target to help developing countries adapt to climate change also appeared in the first draft and was dropped by Thursday. Climate-vulnerable countries have sought more funding for disasters they face disproportionately, despite contributing relatively little to the problem.

The Turkish presidency’s proposed “35 by 35” target—to raise electrification from 23% to 35% by 2035—remains in the draft, but does not specify that the added electricity must be renewable. Critics say electrification without a clear move away from fossil fuels would send an incomplete signal. The draft also acknowledges that warming may exceed 1.5 degrees Celsius and calls for limiting the scale and duration of any overshoot. A UN assessment cited in the negotiations says current policies put the world on track for at least 2.8 degrees of warming; the planet is already about 1.4 degrees warmer.

Talks were set to continue Friday and could run into the weekend. Decisions require consensus, allowing any one country to block them. Panama told the presidency it would formally object to a deal that fails its criteria, including a cap on oil production—an especially difficult demand for oil-producing states. CNA Explains: What is Indonesia's new asset forfeiture law and can wealth be seized without a criminal conviction?
global_south_and_exploitedpolitics_and_powermaterial_harmregional expansion
What happened

Indonesia’s parliament has passed an asset-forfeiture law allowing authorities to seek court-approved confiscation of suspected criminal assets without first obtaining a conviction in specified cases. It could reach proceeds held by others, but third parties can challenge claims to their property, and assets cannot be permanently transferred to the state without a final court ruling.

The House of Representatives passed the law after years of debate, with discussions resuming in January 2026. It creates both conviction-based forfeiture and a non-conviction route for cases in which a suspect or defendant has died, fled, suffers a permanent illness, or cannot be identified or located. Non-conviction cases require assets worth at least 1 billion rupiah, calculated at the time they were acquired.

The law covers criminal proceeds, property used to commit crimes, assets whose owners cannot be identified, and substitute assets when original proceeds cannot be recovered. Transferring assets to relatives or others does not shield them if authorities establish a link to an offence. Even public facilities made with criminal proceeds may be confiscated, though they must continue serving their existing functions. The law applies to corruption and six other offence categories, including drug crimes, terrorism, human trafficking and certain forms of fraud. It excludes illicit enrichment, or unexplained increases in public officials’ wealth; Indonesia Corruption Watch criticised that omission.

A person whose property is wrongly targeted can object in court. Prosecutors must establish the link to crime, while a buyer claiming the property must show legal ownership and good faith. If the objection succeeds, the property must be returned; affected parties may also seek compensation, subject to stated exclusions. Courts must hear objections openly, and the law provides appeal deadlines.

The president is to establish agencies to manage confiscated assets and oversee enforcement. Sandra Sahelangi, a senior adviser at Flint Global, said implementation and institutional independence would shape public trust and investor confidence. Indonesia Corruption Watch also questioned the agencies’ independence because the president would establish them. The law is due to take effect 12 months after promulgation, provided both agencies have been established; implementing regulations are also due within 12 months.

The experiment is expected to continue collecting measurements into the early 2030s, helping researchers refine the decay’s measured frequency. What happened

Researchers at the University of Arkansas have 3D-printed small, interlocking bricks from a material designed to resemble lunar soil, without adding a binder. The experiment points to a possible way to make construction components from material on the Moon, but the blocks are currently too weak for demanding structural uses.

The team used Lunar Highlands Regolith Simulant, a commercially available powder designed to resemble samples returned by NASA’s Apollo missions. It is not actual lunar dust. Because real lunar material is difficult to obtain, the researchers used the substitute to test whether regolith-like particles could be fused directly into solid objects.

Their method, laser powder bed fusion, lays down a thin layer of powder and heats it with a concentrated light source until particles fuse. The platform then lowers so another layer can be added, building the object step by step. The team used a relatively low-power light source to make small bricks, a few centimeters across, including cross-shaped and H-shaped pieces that fit together.

Binder-free bricks remain too weak for construction

Earlier attempts to print structures from lunar regolith had used binders to hold the particles together. Those materials could improve strength, but would have to be transported from Earth. In this experiment, the simulated regolith particles bonded directly to one another, with no added binding agent.

The resulting blocks can support themselves, but they have more internal pores and lower structural strength than objects made with binders. First author Cole McCallum said he would not yet rely on the process for structures expected to bear other loads. Senior author Wan Shou said the team still needs to establish how printing would work in the Moon’s low gravity and vacuum, and to improve the bricks’ strength and durability.

The study was published in *NPJ Advanced Manufacturing*. The researchers say the approach could eventually help NASA make building materials on the Moon, reducing the equipment and supplies that would need to be transported from Earth. That remains a potential application, not a demonstrated lunar construction capability: the experiment used simulated material, and the blocks need further development before they could serve in practical building.

A study presents a Bayesian machine-learning framework to estimate stars’ masses and radii from commonly available observations, addressing the fact that most exoplanet-host stars lack direct measurements of these properties.
