What happened

The International Court of Justice has ruled that Myanmar’s military committed genocide against the Rohingya, giving international legal recognition to crimes that drove mass killings and the flight of hundreds of thousands, while refugees and Myanmar’s pro-democracy forces say justice still depends on accountability and change.

Millions of stateless Rohingya remain in camps, and the ruling has advanced demands for their return. The United Kingdom has imposed sanctions on senior Myanmar military officers, while China criticized the court’s jurisdiction and warned the decision could undermine dispute settlement.

Millions of wives in South Asia unprotected from marital rape - study
global_front_pagepolitics_and_powermaterial_harmanchor
What happened

A new report from Samaj, a regional alliance of women’s rights organisations, says millions of women and girls in South Asia lack legal protection from rape within marriage. It finds intimate partner violence in the region’s eight countries is 35% higher than the global average, and points to sharply different laws and penalties across the region.

India, Afghanistan, Sri Lanka and Bangladesh are among the countries that have not criminalised marital rape, the study says. Across South Asia, the Maldives alone imposes the same punishment for rape within marriage as for other rape. Nepal and Bhutan criminalise it but impose lesser penalties. More than 100 countries worldwide have outlawed marital rape.

In India and Bangladesh, the relevant legal exception dates to 1860; in Sri Lanka, it dates to 1885. Ranjeeta Silwal of Equality Now, which is part of Samaj, says these laws largely reflect British colonial rule. Campaigners argue that forced sex is rape regardless of whether the perpetrator is a spouse. Experts warn that marital sexual violence damages women’s physical and psychological, emotional and sexual well-being.

The legal gaps also take different forms. Pakistan repealed its marital-rape exemption in 2006, yet no superior court has ruled definitively that rape laws cover spouses; the report says a recent high-court decision gave husbands broad immunity for sex without consent. Under an Afghan penal code introduced in January, a husband can be prosecuted for violence against his wife only if it leaves her with a fracture, an open wound or extensive bruising; conviction brings a 15-day prison term. It does not mention marital rape.

Ten petitions contesting the law remain before India’s Supreme Court; the justices were set to open their concluding hearings on 9 December. The government and other opponents argue that criminalisation would be excessively harsh and could damage family and religious values. Silwal cautions that changing the law alone may not ensure justice: Bhutan criminalises marital rape, but treats it as a petty misdemeanour punishable by one month to a year; in Nepal, a husband convicted of raping his wife faces up to five years, compared with six to 20 years for other rape.

Canada’s Supreme Court has largely upheld Quebec’s Bill 21, keeping in place restrictions on visible religious symbols for many public-sector workers. The ruling leaves Muslim, Sikh and Catholic employees and job-seekers facing a law challenged over its effects on religious freedom, equality and access to public employment.

Passed in 2019, Bill 21 bars workers including teachers, police, judges and lawyers from wearing items such as hijabs, turbans, kippahs and crosses while on the job. The court ruled that Quebec could use the Constitution’s notwithstanding clause to shield the law from most challenges under the Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms. The clause allows governments to override certain Charter protections.

The judgment also said courts can assess whether a law is consistent with the Charter even when the clause has been invoked, but the judges did not make that assessment in this case. The court found that Bill 21 infringes democratic rights by preventing Quebec’s elected officials from wearing face coverings for religious reasons. Several justices dissented on parts of the decision, arguing that the law conflicts with protections for religious freedom, expression and equality.

Chief Justice Richard Wagner framed the law as part of Quebec’s historical commitment to state secularism. Civil-liberties groups have said the restrictions disproportionately affect Muslim women who wear the hijab and exclude them from parts of public life. Stephen Brown, head of the National Council of Canadian Muslims, said the ruling leaves some people barred from entire employment sectors because of their identity. Prime Minister Mark Carney said his government would study the decision’s implications and stressed that legislatures should respect the rights of minorities.
