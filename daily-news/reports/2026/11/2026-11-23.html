Proposed defence laws are described as ‘draconian’ and a ‘war against democracy’. Why are people worried?
global_front_pagepolitics_and_powermaterial_harmanchor
What happened

Australia’s Senate has passed a defence bill that would criminalise entering or photographing designated defence zones and give officials search and detention powers. Critics warn its broad reach and short review could affect peaceful protest and people unaware they have entered a zone; the bill still needs lower-house approval.

Introduced in late October, the bill passed the Senate on Thursday and was expected to pass the lower house during parliament’s final sitting week of the year. If enacted, photographing a zone—or a person or object in it, entering or leaving it—could carry up to seven years in prison, a $152,880 fine, or both. A minister could designate not only established bases but also temporary, emergency or mobile sites, potentially including a military vehicle on a public road.

Some offences would impose strict or absolute liability, meaning prosecutors would not need to establish intent, knowledge, recklessness or negligence. Defence security officials and law enforcement could search people and their devices, with or without consent, and detain them on reasonable suspicion that they are entering a zone. The bill also permits necessary force to prevent serious injury or death and allows authorised people—including contractors and foreign military personnel—to assist officials. A minister could issue capability declarations permitting specified conduct that would otherwise be unlawful, subject to reasonableness and proportionality requirements and exclusions for serious misconduct.

Civil liberties groups and progressive politicians say the powers risk curbing protest and were reviewed too quickly. Greens leader David Shoebridge warned they could expose peaceful protesters at the Pine Gap signals intelligence base to imprisonment. Digital Rights Watch argued in its inquiry submission that broad definitions might enable AI data centres to be designated as defence sites, restricting protests against their construction. The Law Council of Australia also criticised the rushed review, warning it could leave consequences poorly understood.

The government says defence laws need updating for a changed strategic environment, evolving technology and closer cooperation with foreign militaries. Assistant Defence Minister Peter Khalil said the changes would support national security and AUKUS while retaining safeguards and parliamentary accountability. The bill’s scope and the strength of those safeguards are central to the concerns raised by its critics.

What happened

A Duke University team has developed Tideway, a system intended to make large mixture-of-experts (MoE) language models run faster on consumer PCs whose GPU memory and system RAM cannot hold all model weights. Its aim is to reduce delays caused when inference must repeatedly fetch those weights from an SSD.

The system targets a growing practical constraint: even a powerful consumer GPU may not have enough video memory for a large model, while buying enough additional RAM to keep all its weights available can be costly. When weights spill across GPU VRAM, host RAM and SSD, transfers can slow both the wait for the first response and the production of later tokens. The researchers argue that existing systems manage these memory tiers too separately, leading to repeated transfers and inefficient execution.

Tideway instead coordinates VRAM and host RAM as one pool, with the SSD serving as backing storage. It changes how that pool is arranged between the two main inference phases. During prefill, which processes the prompt before producing a response, the system streams weights in chunks and protects useful experts in RAM from eviction. During decode, when the model produces tokens, it caches routed experts in VRAM and RAM; when an expert is displaced from VRAM, Tideway can preserve it in RAM rather than discard it and later reload it from SSD.

The execution design also addresses the changing location of experts. Tideway uses asynchronous transfers and adapts CUDA Graph execution—a way to replay GPU work—to paging, aiming to reduce transfer delays and the CPU overhead of managing each token. The authors say this lets the system retain efficient GPU execution even when the full expert pool cannot fit in host memory.

In tests on an NVIDIA RTX 5090 with 32 GB of VRAM and host-RAM budgets from 16 to 128 GiB, the team evaluated three open-weight MoE models across 270 fixed-shape configurations, with prompts of 4K–128K tokens and outputs of 128–2K tokens. Against the fastest baseline, Tideway averaged 1.97 times the end-to-end speed, with average improvements of 2.40 times in time to first token and 1.61 times in time per output token. In replays of eight multi-turn coding-agent sessions, it reduced total request-processing time by up to 3.14 times. These are the authors’ reported results from their evaluation, not evidence of performance across all consumer hardware or workloads.

The work was posted to arXiv on November 10, 2026, and is marked as a preprint under review. The authors say the source code is publicly available.

Researchers argue that self-improving language-model agents often fail to build on useful changes because their evaluations cannot reliably distinguish real improvements from noise. Agent evolution typically keeps the underlying language model fixed while changing external components such as prompts, skills, memories or the surrounding agent framework. A proposer generates a change; the system tests it and keeps it if its score is higher. The researchers’ “Resolution Hypothesis” is that this process is limited less by the ability to propose improvements than by the ability to recognize them.

To investigate, the team studied seven evolution methods across six models and four benchmarks, including Terminal-Bench, ALFWorld, SpreadsheetBench and OfficeQA. They repeated runs and evaluations to separate variation in an artifact’s actual performance from noise in its measured score. The main experiments covered 1,030 independent evolution runs and more than 2.1 million test trajectories; the wider study used over 10 million language-model calls.

The authors found that, on Terminal-Bench, selectors chose the better of two candidate artifacts only about half the time. In repeated evolution rounds, such mistakes can compound: a change may be retained because it scored well by chance, rather than because it improved performance. At the method-comparison level, typical score gaps were often smaller than variation between runs of the same method. Using common single-run evaluation practice, the researchers say the best of four text-based methods was identified only 33% of the time on Terminal-Bench, compared with 25% for a random choice.

They also audited large gains previously reported on 12 benchmarks. In 27 model-benchmark comparisons with gains above three percentage points, the researchers say a single instruction recovered a median 90% of the reported gain; those gains disappeared under repaired evaluations. They interpret this as evidence that some apparent advances can come from behaviors an evaluation rewards broadly, rather than from many robust improvements.

The authors conclude that progress may require evaluations that detect smaller changes reliably, alongside better proposal methods. The work is an arXiv preprint dated November 17, 2026, and has not been peer reviewed.
