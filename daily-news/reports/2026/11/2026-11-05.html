Words Caught in the Heart: Simulating Person-Specific Expression for Self-Opaque Clients in Psychotherapy
science_cs_aiglobal_south_and_exploitedpolitics_and_powerpublic_healthtechnology_and_aiscience anchorpreprint_not_peer_reviewed
What happened

A preprint proposes Pandora, a psychotherapy client simulator designed to represent how distress, trust and perceived safety shape what a person can express at a given moment. Its evaluation suggests that modeling individual language patterns can make simulated clients less generically articulate and less recognizably artificial.

Psychotherapy clients may feel or recognize a problem before they can explain it coherently. The paper argues that many language-model simulators instead rely on clinically organized case descriptions, which can produce replies that explain a client’s difficulties more clearly than the client currently understands them. That mismatch matters in systems intended for therapist training and evaluation, where an unusually articulate simulated client could make concerns easier to identify than they would be in an actual conversation.

Pandora separates the stages between experience and speech. It updates affective, relational, defensive and reflective states after each therapist intervention, then builds a subjective appraisal of what is happening and an attribution for why it may be happening. It plans the response’s content, stance and conversational purpose before a separate expression planner determines how the response should sound.

The expression planner draws on language patterns extracted from the simulated client’s earlier utterances, including habitual hedging, emotional explicitness, discourse markers, sentence length and other stylistic tendencies. These patterns are combined with the client’s current psychological and conversational state. A hesitant client might therefore answer a question directly while still using uncertainty or limited emotional explanation. The final wording is produced by a client-focused large language model using these intermediate plans.

The researchers compared Pandora with five baseline systems on 115 cases drawn from four counseling and motivational-interviewing datasets: 97 Chinese-language cases and 18 English-language cases. They report that Pandora achieved the strongest expression similarity and overall disclosure-alignment scores among the compared systems.

A separate study examined 45 matched disclosure items through ratings by four evaluators. Pandora was judged to sound AI-generated in 7.2% of ratings, versus 43.3% to 67.2% for the baselines. Removing its expression-planning stage increased that rate to 35.6% and reduced expression similarity. The authors interpret these results as support for explicitly planning both what a simulated client says and how that client says it.

The findings come from an arXiv preprint rather than peer-reviewed research. They support the framework in the evaluated setting; the evidence does not establish how it would perform beyond these datasets and comparisons.

arXiv #1
Think Less, Locally: Local Preference Learning for Reasoning Compression
science_cs_aiglobal_south_and_exploitedpolitics_and_powertechnology_and_aieconomy_and_businessscience anchorpreprint_not_peer_reviewed
What happened

A new preprint proposes a way to reduce the cost of long reasoning-model outputs by teaching models to decide locally when further thought is unnecessary, reporting shorter completions on mathematics and code tasks without sacrificing—and sometimes improving—accuracy.

Large reasoning models can continue generating calculations, revisions and checks after they have enough information to answer. The authors, from MOEKLINNS at Xi’an Jiaotong University and WeChat AI at Tencent, argue that this “overthinking” is not only a problem of how long a complete reasoning path is, but also of the model’s decision at each intermediate state: whether to continue or conclude.

Their method, LoCo, or Local Compression, uses the model’s own reasoning traces as training material. It identifies possible overthinking points, keeps the reasoning prefix fixed, and generates shorter verified continuations from that same state. The original and shorter continuations then form state-specific preference pairs for Direct Preference Optimization (DPO). This is intended to teach selective stopping rather than reward short outputs indiscriminately, preserving longer reasoning where a problem still requires it.

Training data was generated entirely from mathematical traces. The researchers evaluated the resulting model on five mathematics benchmarks—MATH-500, AMC23, Olympiad, AIME24 and AIME25—and three code-generation benchmarks. They report reductions in completion length of 41.7% to 45.4% for mathematics and 23.4% to 36.3% for code, while maintaining or improving accuracy. LoCo also achieved the strongest area-under-the-accuracy-versus-token-budget curve on all three code benchmarks, according to the preprint.

The reported mathematical results show the largest gains on harder tasks. On Olympiad, AIME24 and AIME25, LoCo both reduced token use by roughly 44.6% to 45.4% and exceeded the base model’s accuracy and AUC in the authors’ evaluation. By comparison, the cited baselines either compressed less or showed larger accuracy losses as difficulty increased.

The same model was trained only with mathematical traces but reportedly transferred its efficiency gains to code generation. The authors interpret this as evidence that LoCo changes a general preference about when to stop reasoning, rather than adding task-specific knowledge. The work is a preprint and has not been peer reviewed; its authors say they plan to release the code and model publicly.

arXiv #1
MoviEdit: Long-form Multi-Shot Video Editing Made Easy
science_cs_aiscience anchorpreprint_not_peer_reviewed
What happened

MoviEdit is a preprint framework from researchers at Adobe and KAIST for editing long, multi-shot videos while keeping an intended change consistent across cuts, viewpoints and subject appearances. It combines automatically generated training data, a two-stage video model and a dedicated benchmark, with reported gains over open-source systems.

MoviEdit addresses a problem that conventional video editors generally handle only over short, single-shot clips: applying an instruction across a sequence in which scenes, camera angles, motion and subjects change. The authors describe the challenge as more than a matter of processing additional frames. Editing shots independently can break subject identity or visual consistency, while processing the entire source and target videos together creates a large computational burden.

The system supports both text-guided editing and edited-frame-guided editing. In the latter setting, a user supplies an edited reference frame, which can come from an arbitrary frame rather than only the beginning of a video. The authors also report that the method can generalize to videos longer than those used during training.

Training data without manual annotation

To address the lack of paired long-form editing data, the researchers developed three automatic data-generation pipelines. They use synchronized multi-camera footage, real-world cinematic clips and generated multi-shot content to create source–target pairs with cross-shot correspondence. The pipelines cover pixel-aligned edits, subject localization and semi-pixel-aligned transformations.

The resulting dataset contains approximately 87,000 filtered paired editing videos; the paper’s training-data table lists 86,619 filtered pairs from 205,228 raw pairs and 61,251 raw clips. The examples cover edits such as adding, removing, swapping or changing the appearance of objects, as well as subject highlighting, environment changes, stylization and medium changes. Vision-language-model quality checks filter pairs for edit success, subject consistency, prompt alignment and video quality. The paper says the data are produced without manual annotation.

MoviEdit is trained alongside a proprietary collection of short, single-shot editing pairs. The model uses a diffusion-transformer mixture-of-experts backbone, a frozen multimodal text encoder and a video variational autoencoder. The authors fine-tuned the DiT parameters across 64 NVIDIA H200 GPUs, first for text-guided video-to-video editing and then for edited-frame-guided editing.

A global draft followed by detailed rendering

The model uses a cascaded bidirectional–autoregressive design. A Draft Editor first processes a spatially and temporally reduced version of the complete video with bidirectional attention. This produces a lightweight edit draft that establishes a consistent appearance and edit across the full sequence, including shots that are far apart in time.

A Render Editor then generates the full-resolution result in chunks using causal, autoregressive diffusion. Each chunk uses recently generated context, the global draft and the corresponding source-video segment. This arrangement is intended to retain the draft’s long-range consistency while limiting the memory demands of full-sequence processing.

The paper identifies appearance drift and oversaturation as problems in naïve autoregressive editing over long horizons. It reports that draft conditioning reduces this drift. For faster inference, the system also uses key-value caching and distillation: the Draft Editor is reduced to 12 denoising steps and the Render Editor to four. In the reported test at 540p and 361 frames on eight H100 GPUs, the distilled system took 11.8 seconds, compared with 364.1 seconds for the full temporal-attention variant.

Benchmark results and what they measure

The researchers introduce MoviEdit-Bench, which contains 64 generated multi-shot videos and 128 editing scenarios across eight categories. The benchmark includes recurring subjects, crowded scenes, mixed human–animal scenes, visual-medium changes and global scene edits. It evaluates both text-guided editing and edited-frame propagation, including references from first and arbitrary frames.

Its measures cover preservation of unrelated content, edit localization, subject preservation, cross-shot consistency, prompt alignment and video quality. Edited-frame-guided tests additionally assess fidelity to the reference frame. The benchmark combines pixel-level and embedding-based measures with vision-language-model judgments, including whether the requested edit remains correctly localized after a cut.

In the authors’ reported comparisons, MoviEdit achieved the strongest overall result among the evaluated systems for text-guided editing on MoviEdit-Bench, with an overall edit-success score of 0.790. For edited-frame-guided editing, it recorded 0.803 on the first-frame subset and 0.786 when references could come from arbitrary frames. The paper says it outperformed the evaluated open-source models and performed comparably with proprietary systems such as Aleph 2.0, although the individual metrics showed different strengths across models. The authors also report consistent results on the external FiVE-Bench and VPBench evaluations.

These findings are claims from arXiv preprint version 1, dated 2 November 2026, and have not been peer reviewed.

arXiv #1
Factorized MLLM Embeddings for Composed Image Retrieval
science_cs_aitechnology_and_aieconomy_and_businessscience anchorpreprint_not_peer_reviewed
What happened

Composed image retrieval asks a model to find a target image from a reference image plus an instruction describing how it should change. A preprint introduces FactEmbed, a method that separates interpreting that edit from building a searchable embedding, and reports higher retrieval scores than prior systems on CIR benchmarks.

The work targets a weakness in a common MLLM-based approach: compressing the reference image and modification text directly into one vector and training that vector to match the target image. The authors argue that this gives the model no explicit intermediate mechanism for deciding which visual attributes should change and which should remain unchanged.

FactEmbed instead inserts a small set of learnable factorized latents before the model’s end-of-sequence token. These latents are intended to represent separate aspects of the requested target. The model first uses them to infer the result of the edit, then combines their information into a global embedding for retrieval.

Turning an edit into structured visual factors

The method uses an intermediate “target imagination” objective. A lightweight decoder attached to an intermediate layer attempts to reconstruct target-image features from the factorized latents. It receives a coarse layout prior, so the latents are encouraged to represent residual, fine-grained information rather than merely reproduce the overall scene arrangement.

The same reconstruction objective is applied to the query and target sides. For the query, the model must infer the target’s residual details from the reference image and modification text without seeing the target. For the target, the objective anchors the latents to comparable factors, allowing the two sides to be matched.

FactEmbed also applies a factor-level contrastive loss. Instead of comparing only one query vector with one target vector, it uses Sinkhorn optimal transport to match sets of factorized latents flexibly. A separate instance-level contrastive loss trains the end-of-sequence representation used for broad retrieval. The authors additionally distill spatial information from the decoder into deeper MLLM attention layers so that factor structure is retained closer to the final embedding.

The implementation uses eight latents and fine-tunes Qwen2.5-VL-7B-Instruct with DoRA adapters. Training proceeds in stages: first shaping the latents through reconstruction, then jointly optimizing reconstruction, factor matching and global retrieval. A later augmentation stage generates additional modification descriptions for hard negative images, following the pipeline used by ReCALL.

One embedding for search, factor evidence for reranking

At inference, a single forward pass produces both the global end-of-sequence embedding and the factorized latents. The global vector searches the image gallery, while the latents are mean-pooled into one additional vector used to rerank the top 100 candidates.

This design avoids storing and comparing the full set of latent vectors for every gallery image. In the paper’s comparison, full factor matching produces the best top-one result but has higher storage and search costs. The proposed efficient reranking comes close while retaining two vectors per image and requiring no additional encoding pass.

The reported results show gains across the evaluated datasets. On the CIRR test set, FactEmbed reaches 56.60% Recall@1, compared with 53.76% for ReCALL. On the refined CIRR benchmark, it reports 68.99% Recall@1, ahead of ReCALL’s 67.41%. On refined FashionIQ, its average Recall@10 is 71.13%, versus 69.48% for ReCALL. The paper also reports improvements over systems using general-purpose multimodal embeddings and other composed-retrieval methods.

The authors find that the individual components contribute differently. Target imagination alone does not consistently improve top-one retrieval, while combining it with factor-level contrastive training, attention distillation and efficient reranking produces stronger results. The full system, including augmented training examples, reaches 56.60% Recall@1 on CIRR and 57.71% average Recall@10 on the original FashionIQ validation set.

The paper notes that results are substantially lower on the original CIRR and FashionIQ benchmarks than on refined versions, which it attributes to ambiguous annotations and evaluation artifacts in the original datasets. FactEmbed is presented as outperforming the compared methods under both evaluation settings, but the work is currently an arXiv preprint dated 3 November 2026 and has not been peer reviewed.

MIT scientists break a 20-year rule and unlock never-before-seen crystal structures

What happened

MIT researchers and collaborators have developed a way to assemble nanoparticles into previously unseen crystal structures by using highly flexible, electrically charged molecular connectors instead of rigid links. The approach could expand the design of materials for photonics, catalysis, electronics, medicine, energy and agriculture.

The work, described by MIT in a November 4, 2026 institutional release, challenges roughly two decades of conventional thinking about nanoparticle assembly. Researchers have generally treated rigid molecular connectors, or ligands, as essential for holding particles at controlled angles. The MIT team instead allowed the connectors to bend and reorganize as nanoparticles came together.

The connectors are attached to nanoparticle surfaces and carry positive or negative charges. Opposite charges attract, drawing particles together, while the flexible molecular chains adjust their positions during assembly. That movement affects how many neighboring particles each nanoparticle can bind to, a property known as valency.

Experiments led by MIT PhD candidate Nicholas Sbalbi showed that changing the particle sizes and the lengths of the flexible molecular chains altered the resulting structures. By reducing the number of connections between neighboring particles, the researchers produced more open arrangements than the densely packed forms that commonly emerge when nanoparticles assemble.

These ordered networks are known as nanoparticle superlattices. Their structure matters because the arrangement of a material’s smallest components helps determine its electrical, optical and chemical behavior. The researchers’ method is intended to give scientists more control over structure without having to predefine every bonding direction or extensively redesign each particle’s shape and surface.

Traditional strategies include changing nanoparticle shapes or making selected surface regions sticky. The MIT release says such methods can be complex, highly specialized and difficult to adapt for larger-scale production. Flexible connectors could offer a less rigid route to creating varied structures, although the release does not establish that the approach is ready for commercial manufacturing.

The team sees possible applications in photonics, chemical catalysis, electronics, medicine, energy technologies, agriculture and chemical manufacturing. More open structures could be useful where densely packed nanoparticles limit the properties or functions a material can provide. The findings are described as appearing in a paper that will be published in *Nature Materials*; the evidence presents the applications as potential rather than demonstrated technologies.

What happened

A study of 46 children in the United States found that those eating more ultra-processed food had stronger reward-related brain activity when viewing appealing foods, and that this response was associated with greater increases in body fat over the following year.

Children reporting higher ultra-processed food intake showed more activity in the dorsal striatum, a brain region involved in reward and motivation. They also generally recorded larger increases in body mass index, body-fat percentage and waist circumference after one year. The researchers plan to investigate the question through an intervention giving children more minimally processed alternatives. The ongoing BrainChild study will also continue tracking participants’ brain development, diets and body composition through puberty and into early adulthood.

Permanent daylight saving time has a dark side, scientists warn
science_generalpolitics_and_powerpublic_healthscience anchorinstitutional_press_release
What happened

Permanent daylight saving time could add at least 2 billion school trips taken before sunrise across the United States, University of Washington researchers warn. The projected shift could increase children’s exposure to dark traffic conditions while reducing morning sunlight important to sleep and biological rhythms.

Permanent daylight saving time would move winter sunrise one hour later, leaving mornings darker from November through March. The issue has gained momentum in Washington, D.C.: the House of Representatives approved legislation to end the seasonal return to standard time, but the bill remains stalled in the Senate. Nineteen states, including Washington, have approved measures supporting the change if federal legislation becomes law.

To estimate the effect on schoolchildren, the researchers examined 14 of those 19 states, combining enrollment data, each school’s start time and geographic location. They compared projected travel before sunrise under permanent daylight saving time with current clock changes. Washington state alone could see nearly 35 million additional dark school commutes, while Georgia could see about 71 million. In Mississippi and Alabama, where schools begin on average before 8 a.m., dark trips could increase from roughly 2 million to more than 20 million per state.

The nationwide estimate may be conservative. For the projection, researchers assumed students left home around 8 a.m., although some travel earlier for breakfast or other school activities. Their analysis of U.S. traffic collision records found fatal crashes involving school-aged pedestrians were roughly twice as likely on dark mornings as after sunrise.

The researchers also link morning light to circadian timing, which influences sleep, mood and appetite. Delaying school starts could reduce the darkness, they said, but might shift work and family schedules later as well. The findings were published October 29 in *Current Biology*.

What happened

Astronomers have found new evidence that Siwarha, a faint companion orbiting close to Betelgeuse, may be moving through the red supergiant’s outer atmosphere. The interaction could explain subtle changes in Betelgeuse’s shape and offer clues to how massive aging stars shed material.

Betelgeuse, the prominent red supergiant in Orion, was extensively studied after its dramatic “Great Dimming” in late 2019 and early 2020. Its brightness fell to the lowest recorded level in more than a century, apparently after a large release of material helped produce dust that obscured part of the star.

The latest observations, collected from 2021 to 2026 with the NSF Karl G. Jansky Very Large Array and the Atacama Large Millimeter/submillimeter Array, examined the atmosphere at seven radio frequencies. Combining those measurements allowed the research team, led by Lynn D. Matthews of MIT Haystack Observatory, to map layers ranging from just above the visible surface to several times the star’s radius.

The data indicate that Betelgeuse’s atmosphere has largely returned to typical conditions after the dimming. They also measured a much cooler molecular-gas layer near the surface, with temperatures around 2,200 kelvin, compared with roughly 3,500 kelvin farther out.

The strongest evidence concerns Siwarha. Researchers estimate that its orbit takes it through Betelgeuse’s extended atmosphere at a distance of about twice the giant star’s radius. The companion is too faint to detect directly in the radio data, but Betelgeuse’s atmosphere appears more elongated when Siwarha should be most visibly separated and rounder when it passes in front of or behind the star.

The relationship supports the proposed interaction, but further observations across more of Siwarha’s orbit are needed to confirm how strongly it distorts the atmosphere. The findings were published in *The Astronomical Journal*.
