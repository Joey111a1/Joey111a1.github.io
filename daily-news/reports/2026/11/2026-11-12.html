Water Law of the People's Republic of China
china_domesticpolitics_and_powerregional expansion
What happened

China has revised its national Water Law, with the changes adopted by the 25th meeting of the Standing Committee of the 14th National People’s Congress on November 6, 2026, and scheduled to take effect on March 22, 2027.

Promulgated as Order No. 88 of President Xi Jinping, the revised law governs the protection, development, use and conservation of water resources throughout the People’s Republic of China. It covers surface water and groundwater, while seawater remains subject to other applicable laws and regulations. The law states that water resources belong to the state, with ownership exercised through the State Council. Water held in ponds and reservoirs built and managed by rural collective economic organizations is reserved for use by those organizations.

The framework gives priority to conservation and requires water management to be coordinated across river basins and administrative regions. It establishes responsibilities for the State Council’s water administration department, basin agencies and local governments, while retaining a four-level river-and-lake chief system at provincial, city, county and township levels. The government is also directed to strengthen water infrastructure, monitoring, forecasting, early warning and emergency response for water-related disasters.

The revised law requires national and basin-wide planning for water allocation, protection, conservation and disaster prevention. Plans must be based on scientific water-resource assessments, coordinated with economic, social and land-use planning, and supported by public participation and expert consultation. In water-scarce areas, urban expansion and water-intensive industrial, agricultural and service projects are to be restricted in line with available resources.

It also provides for water-intake permits and paid use, with an exception for rural collective organizations and their members drawing water from collectively managed ponds and reservoirs. Projects that cause groundwater depletion, subsidence, declining river or lake functions, seawater intrusion or worsening water quality must undertake remediation and bear corresponding governance responsibilities.
