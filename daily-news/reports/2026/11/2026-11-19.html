What happened

The International Court of Justice is due to rule on whether Myanmar committed genocide against the Rohingya, a case with direct stakes for refugees displaced to Bangladesh and potential consequences for state accountability under the Genocide Convention.

The court is scheduled to deliver its judgment on November 19 in The Hague, after seven years of proceedings brought by The Gambia. About 1.2 million Rohingya in Bangladesh’s Cox’s Bazar refugee settlement, where they have been since fleeing violence in 2017, are expected to follow the ruling.

The Gambia argues that Myanmar bears state responsibility for genocide arising from a military offensive that drove at least 730,000 Rohingya from the country. Survivors have described killings, mass rape and villages being burned; a UN fact-finding mission concluded that the campaign included genocidal acts.

Myanmar denies genocide and says its forces responded legitimately to attacks by armed groups. The country’s authorities have described the operations as a response to Rohingya militants, while a UN report in 2018 said the army’s tactics were grossly disproportionate to the security threats.

The judges will decide whether genocide occurred and whether alleged conduct by Myanmar’s military or other state bodies breached the country’s obligations under the convention. The case concerns state responsibility: the ICJ resolves disputes between states and does not try individuals or impose prison sentences.

The Gambia has asked the court to order reparations and ensure Rohingya refugees can return safely. The ICJ’s rulings are final, but the court has limited means to enforce them. No state has previously been found guilty of genocide by an international court, making the judgment potentially historic if The Gambia succeeds.

Deadly drug-resistant infections are surging in European hospitals
global_front_pagematerial_harmmajor supplement
What happened

Drug-resistant *Klebsiella pneumoniae* bloodstream infections rose sharply in European hospitals between 2019 and 2025, raising the risk of severe illness as some strains resist nearly all antibiotics. The European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control says the trend is far from the EU’s target of reducing these infections by 5% by 2030.

The increase was 72%, according to ECDC’s latest findings. The agency warns that the bacterium can spread between patients and is harder to contain than some other hospital infections. Several strains are resistant to almost all antibiotics, including newer drugs; infections linked to the bacterium typically have a mortality rate of 40% to 70%. Christian Giske, who leads the ECDC work, identified the findings as the agency’s greatest concern among its data on superbugs collected over the past year.

Hospital prevention measures, including hand hygiene, correct use of protective equipment and thorough cleaning, are central to stopping transmission. Giske said *Klebsiella* can persist in the gut, making it possible that measures effective against MRSA will not be enough. EU countries reduced MRSA bloodstream infections by 19% between 2019 and 2025, exceeding the 2030 reduction target of 15%. By contrast, drug-resistant *E. coli* infections increased 16.4% over the same period, against a target to cut them by 10%.

Other indicators show mixed progress. Antibiotic consumption fell 5% between 2019 and 2025, short of the EU’s 20% reduction target for 2030. The share of antibiotics used that are considered the right types reached 62.5%, approaching a 65% goal. ECDC Director Pamela Rendi-Wagner said the bloc remained behind on several targets. The agency also points to genomic surveillance as a way to track spread sooner and guide treatment and decisions about where new antibiotics are most needed.

Antimicrobial resistance is estimated to kill 35,000 people each year in the EU, Iceland and Norway; that estimate covers resistance broadly, not *Klebsiella* alone. Europe’s pharmaceutical legislation, expected to be signed into law in December, includes proposed incentives to encourage antibiotic development and payment arrangements for countries to buy new drugs.

What happened

In South Africa, high prices and fragmented funding leave many patients unable to obtain newer cancer treatments: public patients are often excluded, while people with medical aid may face refusals or co-payments that make prescribed therapies unaffordable.

The obstacles differ across the two systems. Medical scheme cover depends on a member’s plan, and Cancer Alliance director Salomé Meyer says the country has 71 registered schemes, each with its own rules and oncology benefits. The law requires schemes to cover prescribed minimum benefits, but regulations define which cancer diagnoses and treatments qualify. Meyer argues that the framework is outdated in light of newer therapies. While regulations call for reviews of the benefits every two years or more frequently, the last full review was completed in 2003.

In public healthcare, the National Essential Medicines List guides which cancer drugs are considered for state-sector use, but provincial health departments decide whether they can afford listed medicines. Meyer says this creates differences between provinces. She cites trastuzumab, listed since 2017 for early HER2-positive breast cancer, as still not routinely available to all qualifying public patients in the Western Cape. The provincial department says cost limits access to some targeted therapies, including trastuzumab. A June review by the National Department of Health put the price of a six-month course at R26,766, compared with R65,316 in 2018.

Pembrolizumab, also known as Keytruda, is unavailable in the public sector and limited in private care to top scheme tiers, often with co-payments. Meyer says some cases brought to Cancer Alliance involved charges of nearly R90,000 for each administration. Patients might need six to eight treatments or more; some were asked to contribute as much as half the cost, while others were refused cover. Testing models against sensor drift

An electronic nose turns chemical signals in breath into sensor readings. Those readings can change over time as sensors age, become contaminated or encounter environmental changes. If training and test samples are randomly mixed, nearby measurements may appear in both sets, making a model seem more reliable than it is when applied to later samples.

To address that problem, the authors divided samples by collection week, keeping each patient’s data wholly in either training or testing and separating the test period from the training period. They evaluated 10 approaches, including conventional models using extracted signal features, convolutional and graph-based models, and time-series methods such as LSTM, InceptionTime and TS2Vec. The authors release the dataset, temporal splits, evaluation protocols and code. The benchmark is intended to let researchers compare methods under common conditions and study performance across diseases, rather than rely on results from different datasets and protocols.
