What happened

A Senate inquiry in Australia has found that older people are being left without adequate home support under the federal Support at Home program, with some dying while waiting, facing steep service costs or entering hospital and residential care before they are ready. Its interim report calls for human assessors to be able to correct flawed algorithmic decisions.

The program, launched in November 2025, is intended to help people remain at home. The inquiry has focused on its assessment algorithm, which allocates support and funding. The interim report says it gives greater weight to physical mobility than cognitive decline, potentially classifying people with severe dementia as independent and leaving them without sufficient funding. Applicants can face an annual funding difference of almost $40,000 from just one point on the decision tree, the inquiry heard.

Assessors are barred from overriding algorithm results, and the inquiry heard that appeals can be lengthy and difficult. It recommended restoring human override powers. The government has declined to make immediate corrections, while in July Aged Care Minister Sam Rae said assessors would be able to refer cases to a system governor. An independent examination of the aged care classification and prioritisation assessment has also been announced by the government.

The consequences described to the inquiry include people being pushed toward residential care or ending up in hospital as their health worsens. A woman discharged with heart and kidney failure died four months later, before receiving funding for help with eating and administering essential medication. The official June waiting list for a place in Support at Home included over 106,000 Australians. The health department said in October that 6,436 people died before their approved funding was available; evidence to the inquiry also indicated that 4,812 program-approved people died before any service reached them.

Costs and delays are also putting support out of reach. According to evidence heard by the inquiry, some services such as meals and cleaning had become three times as expensive, and people can be required to cover as much as 80% of the costs. One example cited was crutches that typically cost about $50 but rose to $1,800, with a three-month wait. After nearly 300 submissions, the inquiry has extended its work by a year.

What happened

A team of researchers has introduced Real2SimLab, a platform intended to make methods that turn real-world recordings into simulations easier to compare across benchmarks. It gives methods and benchmarks a shared interface, while preserving each benchmark’s scoring rules where they apply.

The platform addresses a mismatch in real-to-simulation research: benchmarks differ in the inputs they provide, the outputs they request and the software they use to score results. Without a shared interface, evaluating *N* methods on *M* benchmarks can require *N × M* separate integrations. Real2SimLab instead treats a runnable program as the link between a method and a benchmark, reducing the integrations to *N + M*, the authors say.

One program interface, with benchmark-specific scoring

In the platform, a method receives the visible data for a task and produces a program or another specified deliverable. Real2SimLab runs the program and scores its output using the benchmark’s original scorer when one applies. A method and a benchmark each need their own adapter, rather than a separate connection for every pairing.

The paper describes six existing benchmarks: Physics-IQ, Video2World, 4DCodeBench, PhysCodeGym, PhySimCode and IRIS. Five use their official scoring systems. For IRIS, the platform instead compares simulated object trajectories with trajectories extracted from videos. That measures how well a program reproduces motion; it is not comparable with IRIS’s original scoring of recovered physical parameters. The platform also includes a synthetic suite with exact ground truth.

To make results reflect the program rather than the session in which it was written, Real2SimLab freezes a deliverable and runs it again offline before scoring. It checks whether the new run matches the original output and whether repeated runs are reproducible. Some benchmark protocols score outputs produced during the method’s session, so the platform reports those results separately from scores based on frozen reruns. The paper says the benchmark adapters were checked against original releases, including matching Physics-IQ scores and reproducing reference-package scores on Video2World’s Push-T task.

Training data, with a stated boundary

Real2SimLab also provides a pipeline for creating training tasks and reward annotations. It compares video frames in candidate training recordings with evaluation recordings, filtering out matches including re-encoded, resized or clipped copies. For remaining recordings, the pipeline runs a method’s program and calculates its reward with the same scoring code used for evaluation.

The authors say these data could support offline fine-tuning or online reinforcement learning, but the platform does not train models itself. They also state that they have not yet trained a model using the pipeline. The distinction matters: the paper presents infrastructure for training and evaluation, not a demonstrated improvement in a method’s performance.

A platform rather than a comparison of methods

At the time described in the paper, Real2SimLab supported 47 coding agents and five non-agent methods across the six benchmarks. The authors also released Awesome-Real2Sim, an open-source research map covering nearly 300 papers, and invited researchers to extend the platform and map through the Real2SimHub community. They say a systematic comparison of the connected methods is left for future work.

The work was posted as an arXiv preprint on 25 November 2026 and had not been peer reviewed. Its central contribution is a shared evaluation and data-generation framework; the supplied results do not establish which connected method performs best.
