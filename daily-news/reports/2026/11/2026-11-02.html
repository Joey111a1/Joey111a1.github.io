What happened

The upgraded China–Peru free trade agreement protocol took effect on Sunday, according to China’s Ministry of Commerce (MOC). The countries began negotiations in November 2018 and signed the protocol on Nov. 14, 2024. It updates seven existing areas, including customs, rules of origin, services, investment, intellectual property and sanitary measures, and adds five chapters covering standards cooperation, competition, e-commerce, supply chains, and environmental trade.

The new supply-chain provisions encourage multimodal transport and logistics corridors, while a standards and conformity-assessment mechanism is intended to support mutual recognition and lower companies’ trade costs. China and Peru also agreed to begin negotiations on negative lists for services and investment within two years, a planned step toward greater openness rather than an outcome already in effect. The MOC described China as Peru’s largest trading partner, export market and import source, while identifying Peru as one of China’s leading Latin American trading partners and an important investment destination. Official data cited by the ministry show bilateral trade rising from $6.4 billion in 2009 to $50.4 billion in 2025.

Chile: Atacama, World’s Driest Desert Experiences a Historic Bloom Thanks to El Niño
global_south_and_exploitedmaterial_harmregional expansion
What happened

Northern Chile’s Atacama Desert has entered an unusually extensive flowering season after exceptional winter rainfall associated with El Niño. More than 200 native species now cover parts of the normally arid landscape with brightly colored blooms along roughly 1,600 kilometers of the coast. Rainfall from July through September reached 183.5 millimeters in Vallenar, compared with an annual average of about 2 millimeters in the area without El Niño.

The National Forest Corporation (CONAF) says the bloom extends across an area equivalent to 23% of the Atacama region, or about 17,000 square kilometers, making it one of the largest episodes in recent decades and among the most intense since 1997. The same storms also caused floods and landslides in northern Chile, affecting dozens of homes. Most flowers are expected to disappear by November, although drought-resistant species may persist until January. Authorities anticipate unusually high tourism, with thousands of people traveling to view the phenomenon.
