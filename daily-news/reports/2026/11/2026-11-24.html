Oxfam and the Conflict and Environment Observatory say environmental contamination indicators now cover more than 99% of Gaza, with potential risks to water, soil and air that could persist for decades. The findings point to immediate hazards for residents and a difficult cleanup, while the scale of contamination remains hard to test directly.

In releasing the assessment on November 23, the groups said they had based it on satellite observations, remote-sensing techniques, spatial examination and other publicly available data. Gaza’s destroyed laboratories and restrictions on specialist equipment and scientists limit direct contamination testing.

Oxfam’s report author Lama Abdul Samad warned that persistent contaminants could contribute to chronic health effects. Doctors in Gaza have reported respiratory infections associated with dust and pollution. Dermatologist Alaa Abu Hajar said to the BBC that chronic skin diseases had become five times more common in his experience since the war began, citing pollution and crowded, unsanitary living conditions.

Israel’s embassy in the UK challenged the report’s professional rigour and methods, arguing that its conclusions rested on satellite observations and assumptions it said had not been verified, while overlooking Hamas’s excavation of tunnels. Oxfam is calling for an independent environmental assessment before cleanup. The report says testing access is needed to identify risks and protect cleanup workers.

Russia’s war dead: the hidden toll of the Ukraine conflict

What happened

Russian death certificates in a cache suggest that the war in Ukraine was associated with over 460,000 deaths between February 2022 and September 2026; the toll surged in 2025. The records also show losses concentrated in poorer regions and around Pokrovsk, while Russia’s official military death count remains years out of date.

The figures come from more than 11 million certificates compiled by Russia’s state statistics agency, Rosstat, and obtained by the Belarusian opposition-linked hacking group Cyber Partisans and Ukrainian hacker collective Silent Crow. The Guardian’s analysis identified deaths recorded as war-related or listing conflict as a cause, as well as deaths of people whose employment was marked as military. That means the count may include some civilians; it excludes certificates for people outside combat age. Deaths may also be registered months or years late, or never recorded, so the real toll may be higher.

The recorded toll climbed from nearly 20,000 in the invasion’s first year to 50,000 in 2023 and more than 134,000 in 2024. In 2025 it rose a further 52%, to close to 200,000—about 548 deaths a day. During one week in May 2025, more than 5,000 Russian troops died, according to the analysis. Since September 2022, Russia’s defence ministry has left unchanged its official tally of 5,937 servicemen killed.

The deadliest concentration was around Pokrovsk. Nearly 116,000 recorded deaths with known locations occurred in Pokrovsk district, which includes Avdiivka and Pokrovsk. After Avdiivka fell in February 2024, Russian forces took 21 months to advance 39 kilometres to the edge of Pokrovsk; about 88,700 deaths were recorded in the district over that period. Data from Ukrainian monitoring group DeepState show that Russia has gained about 8,000 square kilometres since the start of 2023, amounting to under 1.5% of Ukraine’s territory.

The losses were also unevenly distributed within Russia. The eight regions with the highest poverty rates recorded four times the military death rate of the least-poor regions. In 2025, war was the leading recorded cause of death for Russian men under 65. The analysis also found the average age at death rising, while Russia relied on financial bonuses to recruit volunteers rather than order a second mobilisation.

What happened

The authors of a Nov. 20, 2026 arXiv preprint propose a way to control information exchange among the parallel feature streams used in some neural-network architectures. Their method aims to avoid both streams that barely interact and streams that become too similar, while preserving the mathematical constraint used to stabilize signal propagation.

Hyper-Connections extend ordinary residual connections by allowing multiple feature streams to mix. Manifold-constrained Hyper-Connections (mHC) restrict the mixing matrices to the Birkhoff polytope: the set of matrices whose rows and columns each sum to one. The constraint helps stabilize propagation through deep networks, but does not ensure useful mixing. The researchers say matrices can remain near a permutation matrix—effectively reordering streams rather than blending their information—or move toward uniform mixing, making streams less distinct.

The proposed method, Contraction for Manifold-Constrained Hyper-Connections (c-cmHC), geometrically contracts the polytope inward. This keeps the matrices doubly stochastic while ensuring that every input stream contributes to every output stream. The authors say the method requires no additional parameterization and gives a provable lower bound on stream mixing. Their analysis also identifies a trade-off: stronger or repeated contraction can suppress differences between streams, and its effects accumulate across layers. They therefore apply moderate contraction in shallow layers, encouraging early exchange while avoiding excessive mixing deeper in the network.

The preprint reports experiments on GPT-2 and mixture-of-experts models at 6-billion and 300-billion parameter scales. The authors say c-cmHC achieved lower training loss while stabilizing gradient propagation, and describe the results as evidence of scalability. They also report developing fused operators for Ascend neural processing units, which reduced hyper-connection execution latency by up to 44.8%. The work targets a gap in interactive video generation: models respond to movement commands but do not necessarily check whether the requested path is physically possible. On a test route into a wall, five of six generators evaluated by the researchers passed through it; the sixth moved against the command. They argue that existing benchmarks often emphasize open paths, making this kind of failure hard to measure.

The researchers call their benchmark NoPeBench and their method NoPeWorld. The study is a preprint, not peer-reviewed research. Its central finding is that a model’s intermediate signals can help identify an unsafe action before the next video segment is generated—and that this information can be used to change the action rather than merely describe the obstacle in a prompt.

Using a model’s hidden signals to change its action

NoPeWorld adds a small prediction component to a frozen diffusion transformer (DiT), the model that generates video. The researchers train this component to read the DiT’s internal states and estimate whether candidate movements are safe, how close they come to obstacles, and how well they preserve the intended motion. Training labels come from obstacle geometry in Unreal Engine simulations.

At run time, the system evaluates possible movements. If the requested action would cause a collision, it substitutes a safer option—such as stopping or sliding along a wall—that best retains the original direction of travel. The researchers describe two ways to make that decision: one checks the current video segment’s features; another uses features from the previous segment to anticipate a collision in the next one.

This is intended to avoid two shortcomings the authors identify in simpler approaches. Their tests found that asking a model in a prompt to stop or slide had little effect on penetration. An external depth-estimation system could help stop unsafe motion, they say, but requires additional processing and may halt rather than preserve the user’s intended movement. NoPeWorld instead reads information from inside the generator.

Benchmarking collisions, not just open paths

NoPeBench has three tasks. The first isolates the movement decision by replaying real histories and checking whether a method changes unsafe commands while leaving safe ones alone. The second tests generated video against known scene geometry. The third assesses generalization from open-domain images, including third-person views and scenes with moving obstacles, where the benchmark does not have engine-generated 3D ground truth.

For the first two tasks, the researchers use 100 collision routes across 32 held-out UnrealZoo scenes, as well as 50 unobstructed control routes. The routes include walls and other objects, and test movement on foot, by drone and by car. The prediction heads were trained on 729 videos from 44 separate scenes; the benchmark test scenes did not share asset packs with the training scenes.

Across six video world models, the paper reports that NoPeWorld reduced the average physical-violation rate from 74% to 16%. On LingBot-World 2.0, it reports a decrease from 89% to 4%. The authors also report transfer without retraining to open-domain imagery, third-person viewpoints and moving obstacles. These are results of the study’s tests, not evidence that all world models or real-world systems will behave safely.

The different tasks rely on different forms of assessment. For generated videos in Unreal scenes, the researchers reconstruct camera paths and compare them with known geometry. For open-domain cases, four vision-language models judge visible violations; three non-author annotators also assessed a sample. The authors report that the open models agreed less with people and geometric evaluation than the two closed models, so their main open-domain results use the latter pair’s mean judgments. The preprint makes the code, benchmark and model weights available.
