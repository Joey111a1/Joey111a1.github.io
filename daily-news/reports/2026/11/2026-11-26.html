South Africa: CHILD GAUGE 2026: Austerity and falling grant uptake threaten 30 years of progress for SA’s children
global_front_pagematerial_harmpolitics_and_powereconomy_and_businessregional expansion
What happened

South Africa’s Child Gauge 2026 warns that fiscal restraint and barriers to accessing support are putting children’s services and earlier gains at risk. Its findings point to rising food poverty and a sharp fall in infants receiving the Child Support Grant, with consequences for children’s access to early development, schooling and assistance.

The University of Cape Town’s Children’s Institute unveiled the Gauge on 24 November; it measures how far the country has advanced in realising children’s constitutional rights. It records improvements over three decades, including falling child food poverty and under-five mortality, alongside expanded access to housing, water, electricity and sanitation. But it finds that progress stalled in the third decade and that some services have begun to regress.

Falling grant uptake compounds food poverty

The Gauge says food poverty among children worsened over the period, affecting 33% in 2019 and 37% in 2024, and the post-Covid dip has not been followed by a rebound. In 2026, the food poverty threshold was R868 per person a month, while the Child Support Grant was R580. The Gauge says annual grant increases below food inflation have widened that gap.

Grant coverage among infants younger than one contracted by 44% over the six years to March 2026: recipients numbered 658,000 in March 2020 and 366,108 in March 2026, a level of uptake last seen in 2008. The Gauge warns that early and continuous receipt matters because the grant helps protect children. It also says 10% to 20% of babies—about 160,000 each year—are not registered in their first year of life. Delays can leave children waiting years for birth certificates and exclude them from early childhood development services, social assistance and schools.

The report links service decline to austerity and an administrative culture that prioritises compliance and control over supporting frontline workers. Regional disparities remain pronounced, the Gauge finds: in 2026, 44% of children in former homelands lived in households with piped water either inside the dwelling or on its plot, versus 93% of children in urban areas.

The Gauge recommends strengthening community-based services and cooperation between local government and NGOs, supporting frontline staff, protecting children’s services during crises and investing early. At the launch, planning minister Maropene Ramokgopa said the findings should inform government planning as it develops a National Development Plan for 2027.

‘The only free thing is a bed’: hospital patients forced to pay as Malawi runs out of medicines
global_front_pagematerial_harmeconomy_and_businesspolitics_and_poweranchor
What happened

A months-long shortage of medicines and basic supplies at Salima district hospital in Malawi is forcing patients’ families to buy items privately, while staff say shortages are complicating emergency and maternity care for a largely rural population of more than 500,000.

Maureen Msukwa, a pregnant 25-year-old from Kasache village, had spent two days waiting for pain relief when hospital staff gave her a note to buy paracetamol from a vendor across the road. The hospital is Salima district’s main public referral centre. According to frontline staff, the hospital has been without intravenous fluids for over a week, and stocks of sterile gloves, painkillers, catheter bags and bandages have run out for up to three months.

The shortages are shifting costs to families despite public healthcare being intended to be free at the point of use. Nurse-midwife Evelyn Jossam, who requested anonymity, says relatives are sometimes told to obtain supplies before staff can safely assist women in labour. She warns that families may return with loose, unsterile gloves, creating infection risks. In one case she described, a pregnant woman with pre-eclampsia convulsed twice after staff lacked the blood-pressure medicine normally used to treat the condition.

A state-run Central Medical Stores Trust (CMST) internal report puts nationwide availability of essential medicines at 40%, down from 77% in early 2025; major referral hospitals were as low as 18%. Health analysts say public facilities have not faced a breakdown on this scale since 2013.

The immediate procurement problem is a shortage of foreign exchange. Nearly four-fifths of Malawi’s pharmaceutical and surgical supplies come from abroad, and CMST owes overseas suppliers about $18m; they have stopped sending new shipments until the bills are paid. Although the 2026–27 budget allocates 108.3bn kwacha for medicines and supplies, the agency has struggled to convert local currency into dollars. A directive to buy from five domestic manufacturers has not resolved the shortage because they also need foreign currency for imported ingredients and packaging.

Herbert Chandilanga, a CMST spokesperson, said currency shortages and late funding had interrupted the supply of medical goods. He said the trust had recently started clearing outstanding bills with suppliers and distributors in an effort to resume deliveries. Meanwhile, families continue to face out-of-pocket costs: Selina Chombe said she borrowed money after being sent to buy antibiotics, gloves and painkillers for her pregnant daughter-in-law.

What happened

Poundland entered administration in the UK after talks with potential buyers failed, putting 11,000 jobs at risk while its shops and distribution centres remain open. The dispute is over how a retailer whose sales had improved ran short of the cash needed to keep trading normally.

The chain, which operates 600 stores in the UK and Ireland, including Dealz-branded shops in Ireland, was put up for sale by its owner, US investment firm Gordon Brothers, in September 2026. The failed sale talks also centred on a £22.5m shareholder loan owed to Gordon Brothers, which it inherited when it bought the company from Pepco for less than £1. Both offers were rejected, as was a late offer from Zachert Private Equity.

Poundland’s cash constraints had also slowed stock replenishment ahead of the festive period. Gordon Brothers has denied accusations that it deliberately drove the retailer into insolvency to profit from selling its shops and stock.

Physicists trap antimatter in a breakthrough that could explain why the universe exists
science_generalpolitics_and_powerscience anchorinstitutional_press_release
What happened

Northwestern University physicists have trapped and cooled a single positron, then controlled its quantum energy states without letting it touch ordinary matter. The achievement creates a basis for a precise comparison with the electron—a test that could probe particle physics theories, but has not yet explained why matter dominates the universe.

The positron, the antimatter counterpart of an electron, was produced by radioactive decay of sodium-22. Researchers slowed the fast-moving particle and brought it to about 300 millikelvins, or 0.3 degrees above absolute zero. They held it in a near-perfect vacuum using electric and magnetic fields, which suspended it away from the trap’s walls. Contact with ordinary matter could have caused it to annihilate.

Led by Gerald Gabrielse, the Northwestern team calls the setup a “one-positron quantum cyclotron.” In it, the particle can occupy distinct quantum energy states. The researchers used microwaves to move it between those states and detect the transitions, demonstrating control needed for a later measurement of its magnetic properties. They say the positron has been kept confined in the laboratory for months.

A comparison still to come

The next goal is to measure the positron’s magnetic moment—the quantity describing its magnetic properties—and compare it with the electron’s. Gabrielse’s group previously measured the electron’s magnetic moment to a precision of about one part in 10 trillion. The team now intends to make an equivalent measurement for the positron; the comparison has not yet been reported.

The Standard Model predicts that electrons and positrons should behave as near mirror images. A measured difference could point to a limitation in the theory and offer a clue to the imbalance between matter and antimatter. That imbalance is central to the question of why the observable universe contains matter, but the trapping result itself does not resolve it. The research was published in *Physical Review Letters*.

Quark-gluon plasma—a state of matter that filled the universe shortly after the Big Bang—can be recreated briefly in particle accelerators, where colliding ions produce tiny, short-lived droplets. Researchers can use heavy quarks passing through this plasma to investigate its microscopic properties. Einstein’s 1905 framework describes how drag and random impacts from a fluid affect a particle, but the researchers say its familiar relationship between those forces breaks down when particles move close to light speed.

Jean Du Plessis of MIT and Bruno Scheihing-Hitschfeld of UCSB calculated how a heavy quark would respond to forces in a gas of quarks and gluons that interact relatively weakly. For small changes in the quark’s momentum, the calculated probabilities resembled the familiar Brownian-motion pattern. For larger changes, however, the distribution was tilted: unusually strong slowdowns were more likely than equally large increases in speed, and powerful jolts occurred more often than the traditional model predicts.

The researchers found a similar pattern in calculations involving different plasma compositions and recognized it in an earlier calculation of strongly interacting plasma. They suggest this may point to a feature shared across different types of fluid, though the work is theoretical. Comparing its predictions with accelerator experiments could help physicists learn about quark-gluon plasma’s internal structure and the conditions of the early universe. The results were published in *Physical Review Letters*.

The idea emerged from work on a contactless weighbridge, intended to measure vehicles’ mass through their gravitational pull. The team recognized that controlled changes in a heavy object’s position could also encode information.

The researchers’ modeling suggests quantum sensors might eventually enable communication over distances of up to about 500 metres at roughly 15 bits per minute. Those estimates rely on ambitious assumptions and have not been demonstrated in an experiment; even at that rate, transmission would remain far slower than modern wireless communication. The research was published in *Science Advances*.

What happened

Images taken in September 2026 revealed C/2026 S1 (Euclid), the first comet not previously known to be identified by ESA’s Euclid space telescope. The find shows that a mission designed to map distant galaxies can also identify a rare solar-system object, though this comet is not a target for ESA’s future Comet Interceptor.

While collaborating with the Euclid Consortium’s Solar System Objects Science Working Group, University of Helsinki doctoral student Mikko Pöntinen identified the object. Software he developed flagged a moving streak in Euclid images. The object appeared in five fields of view, across 16 exposures; closer inspection showed a fuzzy cloud around it, consistent with an active comet shedding dust and gas. Known-object catalogues yielded no match, so Pöntinen sent his observations to the International Astronomical Union’s Minor Planet Center; the center confirmed the discovery on October 8.

An unusual find for Euclid

The comet is about 1.3 billion kilometres from Earth and travels well outside the relatively flat plane of the planets’ orbits. Its path takes thousands of years to complete, though further observations are needed to calculate its orbital period more precisely.

Euclid rarely finds new solar-system objects: it avoids the plane where many comets and asteroids lie, and usually images each sky region only once. Researchers must nevertheless identify objects in its images so they can distinguish them from features relevant to measurements of distant galaxies. Euclid’s capture of this comet in multiple images also provided a practical test of how its team could alert other observers. The discovery team says that process may help identify future Comet Interceptor targets, but C/2026 S1 itself is unsuitable.
