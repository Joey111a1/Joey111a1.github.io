Anti-homosexuality laws in Trinidad and Tobago overturned
global_south_and_exploitedpolitics_and_poweranchormajor supplement
What happened

A London ruling has overturned Trinidad and Tobago’s laws criminalising consensual same-sex intimacy between men, ending legal penalties that had exposed LGBTQ+ people to imprisonment and advancing an 11-year constitutional challenge.

The Judicial Committee of the Privy Council, Trinidad and Tobago’s final court of appeal, unanimously ruled on 10 November 2026 that the provisions were unconstitutional and incompatible with the right to respect for private and family life. The case concerned sections of the Sexual Offences Act covering “buggery” and “gross indecency”; the laws had allowed prison sentences of up to five years and two years respectively.

Jason Jones, a dual citizen and LGBTQ+ rights campaigner, brought the challenge. He first succeeded in Trinidad and Tobago’s High Court in 2018, but the government appealed and overturned that ruling. Jones then took the case to the Privy Council in London, which agreed with the lower court’s conclusion and restored the legal challenge’s success.

The ruling removes the criminalisation of private, consensual same-sex intimacy under the contested provisions. Privy Council Judge Philip Sales said the laws could not be justified in a society that respects individual rights and freedoms. Jones said the decision would help accelerate the wider fight for equality, while his lawyer described it as a major victory for equal dignity and privacy in Trinidad and Tobago.

The legislation dated from the colonial period and remained in force after independence. Trinidad and Tobago had also strengthened its punishments in 1986, a change Jones’s legal team argued meant the provisions could not be protected by constitutional “savings law” clauses preserving some pre-independence legislation.

Rights groups in Trinidad and Tobago and internationally welcomed the judgment. Campaigners say it could encourage similar legal challenges in other former British colonies where consensual same-sex relations between men remain criminalised, although that broader effect is a projected consequence rather than an immediate ruling in those countries.

Slight fall in carbon emissions from fossil fuels as Iran war drives renewables push
global_south_and_exploitedmaterial_harmeconomy_and_businessanchor
What happened

Global carbon dioxide emissions from oil, gas and coal are forecast to be broadly flat in 2026, with fossil-fuel emissions down about 0.4% from last year, according to a scientific assessment. The assessment, presented Tuesday at Cop31 in Antalya, Turkey, links the change to higher oil and gas prices after the Iran war and to expanding solar, wind and electric-vehicle use. Oil-related emissions are expected to fall 2% as drivers use less fuel and shift toward electric vehicles.

That fossil-fuel easing does not mean total carbon emissions will decline: deforestation and land-use change are projected to lift the total from 42.8bn tonnes last year to 43.3bn tonnes in 2026, after fires in south-east Asia and further conversion of wild land to agriculture. China’s emissions are likely to fall close to 1%, while India’s have grown strongly, mostly because of coal use; US emissions are likely to fall about 1.3%. Authors and analysts cautioned that a one-year dip is not a durable peak: much more renewable and low-carbon investment is needed, and coal expansion, lower oil prices and fossil-fuel policies could reverse the trend.

What happened

Thailand plans to raise excise taxes on fully imported electric vehicles from 10% to 35% on January 1, 2027, affecting consumers and manufacturers as the country tries to shift from EV imports toward local production. The policy could increase prices and narrow choice while strengthening incentives for companies building in Thailand.

For drivers such as Bangkok ride-hailing worker Pichai Phungmai, affordability is central. Experts expect a possible rush to buy before the deadline, followed by higher prices or a 2027 sales slump. Some brands could abandon imported models rather than build locally, although manufacturers may first bring in inventory under the existing tax structure.

China: Answering reporters' questions on the revision of the Foundation Administration Regulations
china_domesticpolitics_and_powerregional expansion
What happened

China’s revised Foundation Administration Regulations will take effect on January 1, 2027, after Premier Li Qiang signed a State Council order. The changes are intended to address governance weaknesses, regulate charitable activity and increase transparency and oversight of foundations.

Officials from the Ministry of Justice and the Ministry of Civil Affairs said the revision updates rules first issued in 2004. They cited incomplete internal controls, insufficiently standardized activities and the need for stronger supervision, alongside requirements in the Charity Law and the 15th Five-Year Plan.

The regulations would make openness and transparency central to foundation operations, restrict benefits to donors and related parties, regulate related-party transactions and require information disclosure. They would also increase penalties for violations.

Registration rules would define the responsibilities of civil-affairs authorities at prefecture level and above and link registered-capital requirements to registration level. Foundations working in areas including aid for vulnerable people, medical assistance, education and disaster relief could qualify for direct registration under procedures set by provincial authorities. The rules would also clarify termination grounds, including two consecutive years without public-welfare activities, and require liquidation before dissolution.

Other provisions would set duties for founders, boards and supervisors; prohibit conflicts of interest; regulate fundraising, investments and outsourced projects; and strengthen joint checks of foundation finances. The Ministry of Civil Affairs said it would prepare supporting documents, training and administrative systems before implementation.

What happened

The preprint ReproBridge proposes turning the repairs, configuration decisions and execution procedures recovered during machine-learning reproduction into reusable artifacts, reporting higher reproduction success and lower downstream API costs across 1,029 tasks from 36 papers.

Reproducing an ML experiment often requires more than running released code. Dependencies may conflict, data loaders may fail, configurations may be unclear, and the path from raw outputs to a paper’s reported statistic may be unspecified. ReproBridge, presented in arXiv version 1 on 5 November 2026 by researchers affiliated with the University of Waterloo and Google, studies whether preserving the solutions to those problems helps a later reproducer work independently.

The handoff combines four elements: a repaired codebase, environment instructions, experiment launchers, and guidance for reproducing the results. The guide records the reasoning behind settings, fixes, paper–code discrepancies, measurement rules and known sensitivities, rather than leaving those decisions inside an earlier agent’s conversation or shell history.

A benchmark built around verified executions

The paper introduces ReproBridge-Bench from 36 papers and 387 experiments, with 1,029 eligible tasks. Those tasks were selected from 1,463 curated measurements covering the papers’ main experimental results. Five human reproducers independently executed each retained task from pinned raw code, with separate environments and sessions. They checked the scripts, configurations, outputs and extraction procedures used for every reference measurement.

The benchmark distinguishes numerical agreement from evidence that the intended experiment was actually performed. A separate Auditor traces reported values to output files, execution records and generating scripts, looking for copied measurements, substituted instruments, altered computations or incorrect statistics. A result can therefore fail even when its number is close to the target if the execution record does not support it.

The evaluation compares fresh coding-agent sessions given either the original codebase or a refined ReproBridge artifact. All 1,029 tasks were attempted in both settings by four models: Sonnet 4.6, Sonnet 5, Opus 5 and Gemini 3.8 Flash. The sessions had a 24-hour limit per paper, and GPU experiments used eight H100-80GB GPUs.

Higher success and lower reproduction cost

The preprint reports that refined artifacts increased mean success across the four models from 76.4% with raw code to 92.6%. The improvement varied by model: Sonnet 4.6 gained 19.3 percentage points, Sonnet 5 gained 16.8, Opus 5 gained 5.5, and Gemini 3.8 Flash gained 23.2.

The refined handoffs also reduced downstream API spending by between 16.8% and 23.7% for individual models, or 19.7% on average. Using the refined artifacts reduced the success-rate difference between the best- and worst-performing models to 3.9 percentage points from 21.6, while all four surpassed 90% mean success. The authors interpret this as evidence that explicit execution knowledge reduces the need for agents to infer missing details from raw repositories.

The gains were not universal for every paper or task. In the first evaluation, 813 raw-code failures became successes with refined artifacts, while 136 previously successful outcomes became failures. The recoveries included numerical mismatches, tasks that had not run because of execution or resource barriers, and results invalidated by execution audits. The reversals included numerical mismatches, resource failures and a smaller number of audit-invalid results.

Examples in the paper describe artifacts resolving an ambiguous aggregation rule, exposing a configuration that matched the paper rather than the repository’s default, preserving a difficult dependency installation, and correcting a data-loader problem. These examples illustrate the paper’s central distinction: reproducibility depends not only on releasing code, but also on making the recovered procedure portable and understandable.

An ablation study on 287 tasks found that removing any component reduced success. Removing refined code caused the largest decline, 5.2 percentage points, followed by environment setup at 4.5 points; removing the reproduction guide reduced success by 3.8 points, and removing launchers by 3.1 points. A handoff retaining ordinary outputs but without the standardized structure performed better than raw code but worse than the complete artifact.

What the evidence does and does not establish

The results are from a preprint and have not been peer reviewed. The main sample targets accessible official implementations and began from 742 ICML 2026 Spotlight papers; exclusions included unavailable artifacts, high compute requirements and closed-model access. The authors also report results on 45 CORE-Bench Hard capsules, where refined artifacts had higher success and lower reported time and spending than raw code and another patched-code handoff.

Cross-model comparisons are described by the paper as descriptive because the evaluation harnesses differ among vendors. The construction cost for the 36 artifacts was reported as $5,026 in API usage. Under the paper’s assumptions, repeated reuse would need eight to 13 complete downstream rounds to offset that cost, depending on the model; GPU, auditing and maintenance costs were excluded from that calculation.

The contribution is therefore a workflow and artifact design, supported by the preprint’s benchmark results, rather than a claim that all ML research can be reproduced automatically. Its reported gains depend on the selected papers, task definitions, reference protocol, audit rules and available computing resources.

It covers Clojure, Dart, Elixir, Erlang, F#, Haskell, Julia, Kotlin, Lua, OCaml, R, Scala and Swift. The authors say these ecosystems have relatively limited representation in repository-level issue-resolution evaluations, reducing evidence about how agents handle their libraries, toolchains and development practices.

Each task is based on a GitHub issue and an associated merged pull request. The researchers use the pre-fix repository, its execution environment, the reference change and evaluation tests. Their central contribution is a solver–judge–repairer process: an agent attempts the task, judges inspect the attempt, tests and task description, and a repair agent can revise tests or append clarifications. The aim is to avoid rejecting valid alternative implementations while also preventing incorrect solutions from passing incomplete tests.

The process also checks installation, builds and test execution in clean containers. Tasks are repeatedly re-evaluated after repairs; the reference patch remains unchanged, and candidates with faulty reference patches or defects that cannot be repaired without changing the original intent are removed. Of 237 tasks selected for initial difficulty calibration, 35 were ultimately excluded, leaving 202 tasks, with 12 to 27 tasks per language.

The authors evaluated nine model configurations, examining success rates, repeated-attempt consistency and reported resource use. In their table, the highest Pass@1 result was 81.52% for Opus 5.5, while GPT 6.1 Sol reached 78.38% and GPT 5.6 Sol 75.74%. Performance varied substantially: Qwen 3.6 27B recorded 16.67% Pass@1. These are benchmark results reported by the authors, not independent validation of the models or of the benchmark’s generality.

The release includes the benchmark instances, executable environments, construction and evaluation tools, agent trajectories and an easier companion set for smaller models. The work is a preprint and has not been peer reviewed.

What happened

Researchers at Kioxia propose training self-supervised Vision Transformers to direct different attention heads toward distinct image regions, aiming to make their internal representations more spatially selective and interpretable without labeled data.

A preprint posted to arXiv on 9 November 2026 addresses a limitation the researchers identify in models such as DINO: several attention heads can learn ambiguous or similar visual patterns, making it difficult to determine what regions they represent. Their approach adds two visual-focusing loss functions to self-supervised training, encouraging correspondence between a model’s attention and particular parts of an image.

The first, the inside-focus loss, pairs a cropped region sent to the teacher model with the full image sent to the student model. The student is prompted to emphasize attention heads that focus on the corresponding region. The second, the outside-focus loss, masks a region for the teacher while prompting the student to focus on areas outside that region. Together, the losses are intended to make attention heads specialize in different spatial information rather than repeatedly concentrating on the same discriminative features.

The method uses randomly selected image boxes during training, so it does not require object locations, segmentation masks or other annotations. The researchers combine the losses with the DINOv2 training framework and train Vision Transformers with register tokens on the unlabeled training split of ImageNet-1k. Their main experiments use a ViT-L/16-Reg model, with evaluation conducted using the teacher model.

The reported qualitative and quantitative evaluations found more diverse and spatially focused attention maps than conventional training, including clearer differences among the 16 heads in the model’s final layer. The researchers also report improvements in zero-shot image segmentation, unsupervised object discovery and multi-object image retrieval, alongside greater interpretability of the attention maps.

The results are not presented as an across-the-board improvement. The researchers observed a slight reduction in classification performance and caution that the method should not be understood as producing universally stronger global or dense representations. Instead, the preprint presents spatial focusing as a way to trade some classification performance for more differentiated visual features and clearer attention behavior. The work is a research preprint and has not been peer reviewed.

What happened

A computational method has uncovered an exoplanet hidden in existing James Webb Space Telescope observations, improving astronomers’ ability to distinguish faint planets from the intense light of their host stars and offering a way to search archival data for worlds missed by earlier analyses.

Developed by researchers including scientists at Caltech and Northwestern University, the technique uses a detailed computer model—a “digital twin”—of Webb’s Near Infrared Camera. The model simulates how the instrument operates and how starlight spreads through its optical system, including tiny imperfections and changes that can obscure a nearby planet.

Called Physics-based Learning for Imaging Planets (FLIP), the method uses machine-learning optimization to adjust millions of variables until the simulated pattern of starlight matches the actual observation. Astronomers can then subtract the modeled glare from the image, making a planet’s much fainter signal easier to detect.

Tests involving known planets produced detection sensitivity two to 15 times higher than conventional approaches, according to the researchers. FLIP has also revealed an exoplanet that was concealed in existing Webb data. The available account does not identify the planet or establish that it is habitable.

Direct imaging is difficult because exoplanets generally orbit stars whose light overwhelms them. Even highly stable space telescopes can leave optical distortions that make planets indistinguishable from residual glare. FLIP addresses those effects computationally after observations are collected, rather than requiring the telescope hardware to eliminate every imperfection.

A tool for existing and future telescopes

The technique could allow astronomers to revisit Webb’s archive for planets overlooked in earlier analyses, including objects previously considered too difficult to image directly. The researchers also said FLIP may be adaptable to other instruments, including NASA’s Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope and telescopes operating from Earth.

The method was described in a Nature paper published online on November 4; the institutional science news item was dated November 10, 2026. The researchers intend to refine FLIP and study its use with additional instruments. They also see possible applications to NASA’s proposed Habitable Worlds Observatory, which is intended to image Earth-like planets and investigate possible signs of life. Those longer-term uses remain prospective: the current result demonstrates improved detection in existing telescope data, not the discovery of life or a confirmed habitable world.

These results indicate that Alzheimer’s-associated mutations can affect the peripheral nervous system directly, even when the central nervous system is absent from the model.

What happened

CERN scientists have reported evidence for an exceptionally rare decay of the Bd beauty particle, a result that could let physicists test for indirect effects from particles too massive to produce directly and refine the search for physics beyond the Standard Model.

Researchers working with the LHCb experiment found signs that Bd, made of a beauty quark and a down antiquark, can decay into two muons—heavier counterparts of electrons. The process is expected to occur only about once in every 10 billion beauty-particle decays. The result was announced at a CERN seminar after a search lasting more than four decades.

The evidence reached a statistical significance of 3.0 standard deviations using data collected in 2024 and 2025. Combining those measurements with data from 2011–18 raised the significance to 3.2 standard deviations. That exceeds the commonly used threshold for “evidence,” but not the five-standard-deviation level required for a confirmed observation. Earlier evidence for the same Bd decay had not been confirmed by analyses using data through 2018.

Why the decay matters

Rare decays can be influenced by quantum effects from particles that cannot be created at the Large Hadron Collider. Measuring the Bd process therefore offers an indirect way to search for hidden particles and forces. The reported result is consistent with Standard Model predictions while narrowing the possible properties of physics beyond that theory.

The advance depended on an LHCb upgrade that began collecting data in 2024 at five times the earlier rate, improved particle identification and used machine-learning methods to separate the faint signal from background events. Planned upgrades could increase data collection by another factor of 10: the High-Luminosity LHC is scheduled to start in 2030, while a further LHCb upgrade is planned for the middle of the following decade. These larger datasets could test the decay more precisely and determine whether it reaches discovery-level significance.

Comets may have destroyed life's ingredients instead of delivering them to Earth

What happened

A University of Cambridge-led study suggests comets were far less effective than previously thought at supplying Earth with the chemical ingredients for life, because most impacts would have destroyed or dispersed fragile molecules before useful chemistry could begin.

For decades, scientists have considered whether comets delivered prebiotic molecules—compounds that can participate in reactions leading to more complex organic ingredients—to the early Earth. The new research challenges that explanation. Using computer simulations of comet impacts, atmospheric interactions and the formation and evolution of comet populations, the researchers assessed whether these molecules could survive an arrival at Earth.

The simulations indicate that comets usually approached at speeds and angles that made successful delivery extraordinarily unlikely. The researchers focused in particular on hydrogen cyanide, a carbon- and nitrogen-bearing compound commonly detected in comets and considered relevant to origin-of-life chemistry. Even when some hydrogen cyanide survived impact, it would also have needed to accumulate in a sufficiently high concentration and remain in place long enough for further reactions to occur.

Timing made the scenario still less likely. Comet impacts were more frequent during Earth’s earliest history but declined sharply during the planet’s first 500 million years. The researchers concluded that only a small number of opportunities may have existed for a comet to deliver hydrogen cyanide under favorable conditions. The source describes the combined requirements—appropriate speed and angle, molecular survival, local concentration and rapid chemical reactions—as potentially a one-off event rather than a reliable supply route.

Mars offered better conditions, but no confirmed answer

The simulations suggest Mars may have been a somewhat better destination for comet-borne molecules. Its smaller size and thinner atmosphere could have allowed incoming objects to reach the surface with less destruction of their chemical cargo. However, the research does not show that successful delivery occurred there; the odds also remained low.

The findings instead point to asteroid impacts or chemical processes on the young Earth as more plausible sources of life’s building blocks, without establishing exactly how life began. They may also influence the search for life beyond the Solar System. The researchers suggest that planetary systems with closely spaced worlds, where comets could lose speed through repeated gravitational encounters, may be useful targets for future observations, including possible work involving the proposed Habitable Worlds Observatory.
