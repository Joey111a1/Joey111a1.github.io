Growing up displaced in Cameroon after ten years of conflict
global_front_pagematerial_harmpolitics_and_powereconomy_and_businessregional expansion
What happened

In Buea, Cameroon, children displaced by the Anglophone conflict are returning to school while selling goods with their families, a difficult compromise that brings income to households but can leave young people with less time for learning, rest and play.

Some children have found greater safety in Buea after fleeing violence in the country’s Northwest and Southwest regions. But displacement has also meant losing homes, friends and years of education. At the OIC Market, children help sell items such as fruit and bleach, often on weekends or before school. Fourteen-year-old Joy travels from Mutengene to sell sachets of bleach; she says the earnings help support her household and siblings. Joy began helping to sell goods after her family left their village while she was still in primary school.

For Sidoine, also 14, displacement from Ekona followed the burning of her family’s home. She lost four years of schooling before a well-wisher helped her enroll in Buea. Her family sometimes visits its farm to collect food, but has not been able to return permanently. Her experience reflects a wider education crisis: figures from regional education delegations cited by UNICEF in September 2026 put the number of children completely out of school in the two regions at 223,749, with 2,463 schools non-operational.

Teachers and lawyers protested in 2016, arguing that English-language education and common-law systems were being sidelined; by 2017, the unrest had escalated into armed fighting between government forces and separatist groups. The UN refugee agency estimates that violence and insecurity have displaced 2.1 million to 2.2 million people.

Godiva Ndang epse Limene, who works on education and partnerships for Street Child UK-Cameroon, says that a child’s help with a household enterprise does not by itself amount to child labor. She says the key considerations include the work’s risks and duration and whether it interferes with schooling, health, rest or development. Children also need safe places to learn and opportunities for psychosocial support and play.

Local partners support nearly 100 Mobile Neighborhood and Satellite Learning Corners across the two regions, alongside enrollment assistance, learning materials and tutoring. Street Child also provides displaced girls with life-skills and vocational training, start-up kits and mental-health support. Limene says rebuilding caregivers’ livelihoods is important too, because easing pressure on families may reduce the economic burden placed on children.

What happened

Saudi Arabia is now confronting Houthi attacks that reach its capital while Saudi-backed forces fight in southern Yemen, putting public safety, economic activity and the kingdom’s military role in the conflict under pressure. The struggle also threatens a major Red Sea shipping route and Yemen’s government-held south.

In a late-October encounter in Riyadh, a Yemeni officer was discussing fighting in the south when phones around him warned of an incoming missile; loud blasts followed and shoppers froze. The missiles were intercepted, according to the writer’s account. A separate strike on Riyadh’s main airport in mid-October killed at least 14 people and injured hundreds, and few flights had resumed more than a month later.

Saudi Arabia, which backs Yemen’s internationally recognized government, organized a counteroffensive and embedded commandos with Yemeni forces. After gains in October, the Saudi-backed forces announced on November 15 that they had pulled back from the area around the strait.

A Saudi official told the writer that the immediate aim was to retake lost territory and then secure a truce; the Saudi-backed Yemeni forces had not achieved that, and whether they could do so remained unclear. They were also trying to break a month-long Houthi siege of Taiz. If the Houthis took the city, the source said, they would have an open route toward Aden, the government’s base and military headquarters.

The Yemeni officer described his side’s forces as fractured by political and regional loyalties, including hostility between his more secular faction and soldiers aligned with the Islah party. He said many southern troops were more focused on establishing a separate state than fighting the Houthis, who exploit these divisions. Saudi rulers have ruled out deploying their own troops beyond elite special forces already in Yemen, even as the kingdom has brought in foreign soldiers to help defend its border.

Denis Simon said it could open dialogue on international security, while Paul Frimpong described the gathering as an important opening. Other experts pointed to communication as a way to reduce the risk of miscalculation amid international crises.

The declaration also says the countries plan to increase production and supply of refined petroleum products and reaffirm the importance of stable energy flows. Frimpong said more reliable energy supplies could help businesses and families facing higher fuel and freight costs, and support businesses reopening in regions recovering from conflict. Rohingya survivors described how attacks in Myanmar drove them through a deadly flight into Bangladesh, where most of those who arrived in 2017 still live in refugee camps. The survivors’ journeys carried accounts of killings, sexual violence and destroyed villages.

Two sisters, Momtaz and Dildar Begum, each said Myanmar forces killed her husband and children and raped her; for months they believed the other had died. Anwara Begum, who had previously fled to Bangladesh twice, left again after seeing bodies in a river near her village. Her husband retrieved bodies for burial before they headed for the Naf River border; she gave a boatman gold worth about US$200 to cross. Elderly Nur Alam was carried by his children to Bangladesh, where he had sought refuge several times before.

Most Rohingya who arrived in 2017 remain in the camps, alongside people displaced in earlier decades. Refugees live in fragile shelters and, the account says, are barred from leaving the camps, working or pursuing education. The ruling comes as hundreds have died at sea trying to escape the camps, while Malaysia and India have spoken of returning Rohingya to Myanmar. Campaigners hope the judgment will prevent such returns.

global_front_pagematerial_harmpolitics_and_poweranchor
What happened

Former Irish president Mary Robinson has warned that Cop31 has given too little attention to El Niño as forecasts point to a potentially record-strength event nearing its peak, with about 50 million people at risk of severe hunger.

El Niño is a recurring Pacific weather pattern associated with marine heatwaves, disruption to the Indian monsoon, flooding in some regions and drought in others. The current event is forecast to become the strongest on record and soon reach its peak. The estimate that around 50 million people could be driven into severe hunger is a projected consequence, not a reported count of people already affected. The Guardian’s account says the climate crisis has intensified the event.

Robinson said the conference lacked urgency and questioned whether it had sufficient backing from senior government leaders. She contrasted the current leadership with the momentum she recalled at the Paris climate talks, and said the co-presidents of Cop31 had not provided comparable direction. At Cop30 in Belém, she noted, the final text did not pledge a fossil-fuel phase-out, although Brazil produced roadmaps for phasing out fossil fuels globally and halting deforestation.

Her concern is grounded partly in an earlier encounter: during a previous El Niño, in Honduras in 2016, she met women facing a lack of water. Robinson warned that the current event could worsen hunger and destroy homes, amid heat, drought, flooding and other climate shocks. She urged Cop31 to respond with a stronger sense of shared responsibility.

The debate is over whether electrification should require clean power, and how much freedom countries retain to choose their own transition paths.

At COP31, Wang Yi, a professor at the Chinese Academy of Sciences and senior adviser to China’s government, said China would work toward clean electrification of end uses and help other countries improve. China’s endorsement of the goal is therefore distinct from a binding global commitment. The issue matters for China, which produces more than a quarter of global greenhouse-gas emissions. Electricity accounted for nearly 30% of China’s energy use last year, and the country is on track to reach 35% by 2030, according to the supplied account.

The power source remains contested

Wang said China supported the 2035 target, while warning that requiring all the electricity to be low-carbon would make it harder to meet, particularly for other countries. China still has many coal-fired power stations and is planning some new ones; Wang said coal would provide backup during the transition to clean energy.

Civil society groups want any electrification commitment to specify that power must come from clean sources. Campaigners warn that leaving the choice to individual countries could allow more gas-fired plants, while countries including China and India resist rules they see as interference in national plans. The latest draft also removed language on transitioning away from fossil fuels that had appeared in its first version.

What happened

A November 17 preprint proposes that self-improving AI agents often stall not because they cannot generate better prompts, skills or memories, but because evaluation scores are too noisy to identify which changes help. The authors say they tested this explanation across seven agent-evolution methods and six models, using more than 10 million language-model calls. The work is a preprint and has not been peer reviewed.

The authors report problems at two stages. They argue this makes method rankings vulnerable to luck and that progress depends on making real improvements easier to detect and retain.

An arXiv preprint posted on Nov. 19, 2026, introduces Posterior Matching Distillation (PMD), a method for compressing diffusion image and video generators so they produce outputs with fewer network evaluations. A preprint dated November 19, 2026, introduces Coda-DaVinci, an agent-based system for generating video from code. The Mask Is Not the Object: Volumetric Supervision for 3D Gaussian Segmentation

What happened

Researchers propose VODA, a supervision method for 3D Gaussian segmentation, arguing that methods trained on rendered views can produce convincing masks while missing occluded or weakly visible 3D components. VODA uses local depth structure to group pixel contributors and supervise Gaussians in selected depth clusters. The preprint also introduces Removal-3D, a synthetic object-removal benchmark, and Removal Depth Error, which compares predicted and ground-truth depth changes. Across four rendering-based baselines, the authors report improved volumetric segmentation on Removal-3D and maintained or better performance on LERF and ScanNet.

Time may be far stranger than we realize. Scientists have a way to test it
science_generalscience anchorinstitutional_press_release
What happened

An international team led by Kyushu University has outlined a theoretical way to test whether a clock can experience multiple rates of time simultaneously in a quantum superposition. The study, published in *Physical Review Letters*, proposes using advanced trapped-ion atomic clocks to detect this effect, which researchers have not directly observed. The work connects quantum mechanics, where motion can have multiple possibilities, with relativity, under which motion affects how quickly time passes.

The proposed method links a clock’s quantum motion with its internal energy through entanglement, producing changes in its quantum behavior that could serve as a measurable signal. The team says a technique for controlling the clock’s motion could increase sensitivity to the effect by 100 to 1,000 times. The approach has not yet been tested in a working experiment: researchers say they must develop one that accounts for real laboratory disturbances and uncertainties. They also suggest that atomic clocks could eventually help investigate the quantum nature of gravity.

Optimal 10-mm window: Integrating peri-ablation radiomics with preoperative features to predict early hepatocellular carcinoma recurrence after thermal ablation
science_generalpublic_healthscience anchorscholarly_metadata_record
What happened

A retrospective study examined whether ultrasound-based radiomic features around the ablation area could help predict early recurrence of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) after thermal ablation. It included 301 patients, 109 of whom developed early recurrence, and divided them into training, internal-validation and external-validation groups. Researchers combined clinical information and preoperative imaging features with postoperative contrast-enhanced ultrasound measurements from tissue margins extending 5 to 20 millimeters around the treated area.

The preoperative-only baseline model had a test-set area under the curve (AUC) of 0.849. The support vector machine model using a 10-mm peri-ablation region performed best, with an AUC of 0.876 (95% confidence interval, 0.784–0.969), significantly higher than the baseline. Models using 5-mm and 15-mm margins each reached 0.863, while the 20-mm model scored 0.795. The study’s findings suggest that the predictive value of postoperative imaging features varied with the size of the analyzed margin.

Scientists say the human family tree may need a radical rewrite
science_generalscience anchorinstitutional_press_release
What happened

A Monash University study proposes bringing Australopithecus and Paranthropus into the genus Homo, a change that would reshape how scientists name and interpret human relatives across the past four to five million years. The proposal challenges longstanding divisions in the human family tree, but it has not been formally adopted.

In an article in the *American Journal of Biological Anthropology*, the researchers call for classifying every human species and closely related fossil form from the past four to five million years in the genus Homo, which also encompasses modern humans, Homo sapiens. The proposal would keep each species distinct while placing them in a more inclusive genus; it does not suggest that ancient relatives shared all the characteristics of people today.

The proposal reflects a view of human evolution as a branching, overlapping history rather than a neat progression in which one species replaced another. The study’s lead researcher, Dr. Ian Towle, a research fellow at Monash’s Biomedicine Discovery Institute, says new fossils and improved methods for examining evolution and genetics have made it more difficult to maintain conventional boundaries. Traits once used to distinguish groups, the researchers say, appear across more than one group, complicating efforts to define where one genus ends and another begins.

Brain size, tool use and anatomical features have helped distinguish Homo from Australopithecus and Paranthropus. But the researchers point to members of Homo with relatively small brains, alongside evidence that species assigned to the other groups may have had more complex behavior than once assumed. These findings, they argue, weaken the case for treating those characteristics as clear dividing lines.

Towle’s proposal is to expand Homo, not to erase differences among its species. He argues that a broader genus could better reflect the relationships suggested by the fossil record and reduce repeated changes to classifications as new fossils are found. The suggestion remains a scientific argument, not an officially adopted revision; it raises the broader question of whether the categories used to describe human evolution still fit the evidence.

A giant star swallowed another star. James Webb reveals the aftermath
science_generalscience anchorattributed source material (republished)
What happened

Astronomers have reconstructed a merger in Andromeda in which a small star spiraled into a supergiant, producing a sudden eruption and a single remnant called Bao. Webb’s early observations, combined with years of telescope data, offer a rare view of how such a merger unfolds.

The system had shown warning signs for years before its eruption was detected on October 17, 2025. Archived measurements revealed unusual changes beginning in 2023, and observations in 2024 and 2025 recorded increasing brightness and dust. The supergiant’s light rose sharply and reached its peak within about a week. Webb observed Bao on December 24, 47 days after the peak, helping researchers examine the aftermath.

The team compared Webb’s mid-infrared measurements with observations from ground-based and other space telescopes, and refined computer simulations of the two stars. The data indicated that the larger star had about seven times the Sun’s mass, a radius roughly 350 times greater, and 18,000 times its brightness. Its companion had an estimated mass of about 0.7 solar masses. Researchers concluded that the smaller star sank into the giant’s core and merged with it.

The interaction is an example of common-envelope evolution: a companion moves through the expanded outer gas of a larger star, loses orbital energy and spirals inward. In this case, the process took only seven further orbits—about seven years—after the smaller star began interacting with the giant’s atmosphere. Other systems can instead lose material and survive as two stars in a tighter orbit.

Researchers describe Bao as potentially rejuvenated, but its final internal structure and distribution of mass remain uncertain. Further observations could clarify how the remnant evolves and help improve models of binary stars, including systems that may later produce neutron stars, black holes or gravitational waves.

A 50-year-old theory about evolution may have been wrong all along
science_generalscience anchorinstitutional_press_release
What happened

A University of Bath study of spontaneous mutations in *E. coli* and baker’s yeast found no evidence that genes essential for survival mutate less often than other genes. The result challenges a theory proposed in 1972 and bears on a basic distinction in evolution: how often mutations arise versus which ones survive.

Dr. Sofia Radrizzani and Professor Laurence D. Hurst analyzed mutation data from the two microorganisms, which the researchers described as the largest collection of spontaneous genetic mutations assembled to date. Their study, published in *PLOS Biology*, tested whether genes more important to survival are less likely to mutate—a proposed form of protection that would mean mutation rates are not simply independent of a gene’s importance.

The team used mutation-accumulation experiments, designed to record genetic changes while reducing natural selection’s usual filtering effect. They also conducted three tests for possible remaining bias; none found substantial distortion. To assess gene importance, the researchers drew on experiments deleting individual genes: some are indispensable to cells, while others can be removed with little apparent effect. The essential genes did not show consistently lower mutation rates in either species.

The same pattern held when the team considered other measures: how much protein a gene produces and how strongly natural selection has shaped it over thousands of years did not predict mutation frequency. The findings challenge the idea that organisms have evolved to shield their most critical genes from mutations.

Mutations can disappear before they are counted

A possible explanation for earlier findings is that scientists were measuring mutations left in populations, not all the mutations that had occurred. As Hurst explained, harmful changes in important genes can be removed quickly by natural selection. Such “ghost mutations” may therefore go undetected, making essential genes appear less prone to mutation even if they were not protected from it in the first place.

The results do not mean every gene has the same mutation rate. The researchers identified variation linked to genome structure and DNA replication: genes copied later during replication tended to accumulate more mutations than those copied earlier. But those differences did not show that essential genes receive special protection.

The study therefore challenges the proposed mechanism, rather than claiming mutation rates are uniform across the genome. Its evidence comes from two microorganisms, and the researchers say it offers no support for revising the conventional distinction between mutation introducing genetic variation and natural selection affecting which variations persist.

After 8 years in space, a groundbreaking X-ray telescope finally opens its eyes
science_generalscience anchorinstitutional_press_release
What happened

After eight years and 10 billion kilometres in space, the Mercury Imaging X-ray Spectrometer has detected the Crab Nebula, giving its team a first test of the instrument in flight. The results indicate it is working well ahead of BepiColombo’s planned arrival at Mercury.

Built in the UK and carried by the European Space Agency and Japan Aerospace Exploration Agency’s BepiColombo mission, MIXS could not view space directly until the Mercury Transfer Module separated from the science orbiters. The University of Leicester team began its calibration observations in September, before the spacecraft assembly is scheduled to enter orbit around Mercury on November 21.

The team used the Crab Nebula, about 6,500 light-years from Earth, because its X-ray signal is strong and predictable. During a 39-hour campaign on September 11 and 12, the spacecraft made 55 controlled orientation changes. Comparing the resulting images with laboratory measurements from 2015, researchers found that MIXS’s angular resolution—the ability to distinguish detail—was slightly sharper than before launch.

The team also reports no radiation damage to the detector after eight years in space. It has identified effects from cosmic rays that can be filtered from the data, and a small amount of emission produced within the instrument when sunlight hits its optics; this is not expected to hinder measurements of Mercury’s sunlit surface. Some other instrument effects remain under study, and further adjustments are expected during commissioning.

From calibration to Mercury’s surface

MIXS will detect fluorescent X-rays released when solar X-rays strike rocks on Mercury, allowing researchers to identify elements in the surface without collecting samples. It will work with the Solar Intensity X-ray and particle Spectrometer, which measures incoming solar radiation and particles. The team’s successful Crab Nebula observations are an early performance check, not yet a map of Mercury’s chemistry.
