Improving Copyright Adjudication Rules to Promote Cultural Prosperity
politics_and_powereconomy_and_businessregional expansion
What happened

China’s Supreme People’s Court has put into effect revised copyright adjudication rules intended to update civil dispute decisions for digital publishing, social media and online reuse, affecting creators, news organizations, internet platforms and the public’s access to cultural works.

On August 20, 2026, the court issued the *Explanation on Several Issues Concerning the Application of Law in Civil Disputes Involving Copyright*. The revision responds to the 2020 Copyright Law amendments and to disputes created by changing methods of creating, sharing and using works online. It addresses when a work is considered public, how fair use applies to artworks displayed in public places, and which reprints qualify for statutory licensing.

For publication disputes, the revised interpretation adopts an objective standard: a work is considered made public when disclosed to unspecified people, without requiring proof that the author personally disclosed it or authorized the disclosure. The explanation also says that unauthorized leakage can constitute public disclosure while leaving reproduction and online-dissemination rights protected. Rights holders may still seek an end to infringement and damages, and may pursue contractual remedies when confidentiality obligations were breached.

The rules broaden the category of public-place artworks to include works displayed indoors, such as in museums, galleries and exhibition halls. People may reasonably reuse photographs, videos, paintings or other representations of those works, but may not, without permission, install, display or publicly disseminate the original work in the same manner. The stated aim is to permit legitimate cultural and creative use while preventing fair use from becoming a cover for copying.

The explanation also narrows newspaper-reprint licensing in the internet environment. The statutory scheme applies to approved print newspapers and journals, as well as digital editions that fully match their print versions in content and layout. Reprinting between newspapers and internet information providers, or among online providers, is excluded; permission from the copyright holder and remuneration are required.

The court’s interpretation is presented as guidance for judges and market participants, not as a mechanical substitute for case-specific assessment. Courts are to consider the work, purpose and extent of use, and effects on the original work’s market when weighing infringement and fair use.
