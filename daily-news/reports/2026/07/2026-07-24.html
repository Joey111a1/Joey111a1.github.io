Climate crisis-driven heat ‘sucking soils dry’ as extreme drought hits Europe
global_front_pageclimate_disastereconomy_and_businessmaterial_harmanchor
What happened

Extreme heat is now drying European soils faster than rainfall alone would explain, and scientists say the result is a more severe drought that is already hitting water use, farming and rivers across western and eastern Europe.

A scientific analysis found that record-breaking heatwaves strongly evaporated water from rivers, lakes, soils and plants. It said those conditions were made 80 times more likely by global heating in western Europe and 40 times more likely in eastern Europe, while the level of soil dryness was made five times more likely in the west and 11 times more likely in the east by fossil-fuel pollution.

Heat is redefining drought

The researchers said drought in Europe is no longer just a story of low rainfall. Western Europe’s most recent winter was wet, yet the scientists said the climate crisis was redefining drought this summer, with high temperatures now the key factor. They classified today’s drought in western Europe as “extreme”, but said it would have been “moderate” in a world before the climate crisis.

The World Weather Attribution consortium used peer-reviewed methods to assess the drought in western Europe, including England, France, Spain, Portugal and Italy, from April to June. For eastern Europe, where lower rainfall has been affecting the region for a year, the analysis covered 15 nations from Belgium to Estonia over the past 12 months. The researchers compared today’s super-heated world with one without 1.4C of global heating.

Dr Dominik Schumacher of ETH Zurich said there is now “an enhanced tendency for unusual soil drying already in spring, before summer has even begun.” He said that as temperatures climb, “the atmosphere’s thirst increases quickly, sucking moisture out of rivers, lakes, reservoirs and the soil.”

The damage is already visible

The drought was assessed only through the end of June, early in the summer, but many countries have already imposed emergency restrictions on water use, with most of southern England under a hosepipe ban. Deadly wildfires have raged through Spain and France, and farmers are facing some of the driest soils on record.

About 9 million tonnes of grain are expected to be lost across Europe. The worst maize harvest in 50 years is expected in France, and Romanian farmers are losing over a million hectares of crops. Rivers are running low across Europe, cutting clean hydropower generation and restricting cargo ships. The Rhine at Lobith in the Netherlands is lower than the minimum level seen in the hot summer of 1976.

The scientists warned that this disruption could push up food and energy prices, with low-income households hit hardest.

What the researchers warn next

Dr Mariam Zachariah of Imperial College London said the conditions are already occurring at 1.4C of warming, and that if global emissions push temperatures to 2.8C as predicted, these intense evaporative conditions could double in likelihood. Without rapid, aggressive emissions cuts, she said, Europe is heading for scorching, dry summers again and again.

The UN climate chief, Simon Stiell, said worsening droughts are a “red alert” and that until humanity stops burning huge amounts of coal, oil and gas, these climate-driven droughts will become more severe and more costly. He said the solution is to move much faster to renewable energy.

What happened

Yu Deng, John Pardon, Jacob Tsimerman and Hong Wang were named winners of the 2026 Fields Medal in Philadelphia, bringing together four mathematicians whose work ranges across number theory, geometry and mathematical physics, and whose selection had been widely expected.

The names were revealed at the International Congress of Mathematicians in Philadelphia, Pennsylvania. The Fields Medal is awarded every four years to up to four mathematicians under the age of 40, and all four winners had been rumoured as favourites. They all work in North America, but Deng and Wang were born and raised in China. The award also marks a milestone for Wang, who is only the third woman to win in the medal’s 90-year history, after Maryam Mirzakhani in 2014 and Maryna Viazovska in 2022.

Four mathematicians, four different paths

Deng, who is 37 and grew up in Shenzhen, did his PhD at Princeton University and is now at the University of Chicago, where he specializes in differential equations. He said hearing that he had won made him “really happy, not just for myself, but for the field I’m representing”. His best-known achievement was a breakthrough on a problem posed by David Hilbert in 1900: together with two collaborators, he found a rigorous proof that the microscopic jostling of many particles produces, as a necessary consequence, a differential equation used to describe fluids. That result helped reconcile the apparently irreversible behaviour of fluids with the reversible behaviour of microscopic physics.

Pardon, who is also 37 and now at Stony Brook University on Long Island, was born in Chapel Hill, North Carolina. He made his first original contribution to mathematics in secondary school, went on to publish research as an undergraduate at Princeton, and later completed a PhD at Stanford University. His work has moved through symplectic and contact geometry, and his thesis developed techniques for telling two contact spaces apart. He later used those tools on major problems in geometry, including some connected to string-theory physics.

Tsimerman, 38, is a Russian-born Canadian at the University of Toronto. His work focuses on number theory, especially Diophantine equations, and their links to algebraic geometry and Shimura varieties. In 2021, he and collaborators proved one of the central statements in the theory of Shimura varieties, the André–Oort conjecture, as well as another major conjecture stated by Phillip Griffiths.

Wang’s medal places her alongside Deng as one of only the second and third Chinese nationals to receive the prize.

International PhD students to face strict limits on time in the US

What happened

The US government will soon cap international students at four years in the country unless they secure an extension, according to a new Department of Homeland Security rule that takes effect on 15 September. The current system allows students to stay for the length of their academic programme, but the new rule sets a fixed limit for F-1 visas and also prohibits graduate students on those visas from switching programmes or institutions. The DHS says the change “combats rampant visa abuse” and “restores integrity to the nation’s immigration system”.

The rule lands as many PhD programmes already take longer than four years: on average, students in the United States take 5.6 years to complete a PhD in science or engineering. There were some 488,000 international graduate students in the United States in the 2024–25 academic year. Researchers warned that the limit could make US PhD programmes less rigorous and reduce the flow of international students. Bennett Goldberg, a physicist at Northwestern University in Evanston, Illinois, said the policy would deprive US academic institutions of talented students who could contribute to the national scientific enterprise. Chris Glass, a higher-education researcher at Boston College in Massachusetts, said doctoral enrolment would continue to contract.

Breathalyser can tell if you are burning fat from a single exhalation

What happened

A handheld breathalyser designed for home use can tell whether the body is burning fat from a single exhalation by measuring acetone, a volatile compound released in breath during fat breakdown. The device is meant to help people track fat burning in real time, and the researchers say it could be useful for weight-loss monitoring without waiting weeks for changes on the scales.

The team says one route to burning stored fat is ketosis, which can be reached through low-carbohydrate diets or high-intensity exercise, but is difficult to measure in real time. The device was tested on 12 healthy volunteers during exercise and dietary interventions and compared with proton transfer reaction time-of-flight mass spectrometry, a gold-standard lab method for breath analysis. The authors report strong agreement. According to the inventors, a smartphone app guides exhalation to improve accuracy, and the device separates acetone from the breath before measuring it.
