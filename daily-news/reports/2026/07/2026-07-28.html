Devastating European wildfires in maps - and how they're being tackled

What happened

Wildfires across France and Spain have forced more than 300,000 people to leave home, with the biggest damage around Gironde near Bordeaux and west of Madrid, as firefighters race to slow fast-moving blazes before another heatwave makes the job harder.

The fires have cut through dry forests and fields in both countries over the week of July 20-26. In France, the worst-hit area is Gironde, where more than 200,000 people have been evacuated and about 162 square miles (420 sq km) have burned in less than four days. The blaze was said to be nine miles (15km) from the south of Bordeaux and has threatened sensitive military and industrial sites, including facilities working on French ballistic missiles, rocket motors and hazardous chemicals.

West of Madrid, separate wildfires have burned about 297 square miles (770 sq km), according to authorities, while officials said the region was facing the worst fires in its history. A fire in the hilly terrain of Ávila was described as Spain’s largest on record, burning 50,000 hectares, with the scorched land near the capital reaching 70,000 hectares. Smoke has also blanketed Madrid in recent days.

Firebreaks, aircraft and rapid evacuations

French crews have been using planes loaded with fire retardant, along with Canadair aircraft that can scoop 6,000 litres of water from lakes or the sea in 12 seconds and drop it directly on the flames. On dangerous days, aircraft patrol high-risk areas before a fire has even been reported so they can respond within minutes. Ground crews have also used bulldozers, diggers and mechanical shovels to cut firebreaks, including a 100-metre-wide strip near Lacanau.

In Gironde, about 2,500 firefighters, 1,500 military personnel and 1,200 police officers and gendarmes have been involved. Gironde official Jean Luc Gleyze said authorities do not have enough people or trucks to fight the fires. Similar methods have been used in Spain, where an Air Tractor AT-802F Fire Boss dumped water near San Martín de Valdeiglesias, a helicopter scooped water from a swimming pool there, and firefighters battled flames in El Tiemblo and La Adrada. Evacuees have been sheltered in Bordeaux and around Madrid, while horses and dogs have also been moved out of danger.

Another heatwave may keep the crisis going

The immediate concern is that weather may worsen before the fires are under control. Forecasts in France point to temperatures of up to 40C in some areas from Tuesday, while Spain’s interior minister said Monday and Tuesday were “absolutely crucial days” before hotter weather returns from Wednesday. Officials also warned that winds and dry conditions could make the blazes even harder to contain.

The European Union has already sent extra firefighting help: seven planes and four helicopters to France, and six planes plus three forest firefighting teams to Spain, after both countries triggered the bloc’s emergency assistance mechanism. EU officials said the wildfire season began in April and could last until early November, leaving both countries vulnerable to more flare-ups if the heat holds.

TV presenter and comedian Bill Oddie dies at 85

What happened

Bill Oddie has died aged 85, his agent said on Monday, closing a career that moved from The Goodies to Springwatch and leaving tributes that focused as much on his conservation work as on his comedy.

David Foster said Oddie was “the country’s best-loved birder” and a “multitalented celebrity” who encouraged people to respect and protect the environment. Foster added that Oddie “protested, lobbied and campaigned” for nature, and said his contribution to conservation would “never be forgotten”.

From The Goodies to wildlife television

Oddie became a household name in the 1970s as part of the comedy trio The Goodies, alongside Tim Brooke-Taylor and Graeme Garden. Their slapstick comedy and songs ran for 12 years and, according to the BFI, Oddie was known as the group’s “hairy, cynical, anarchist member”. His contribution also included novelty songs such as *The Funky Gibbon* and *The Inbetweenies*, both of which reached the UK top 10.

He had already been writing and appearing in television from the early 1960s, including *That Was The Week That Was*, *I’m Sorry I’ll Read That Again*, *At Last the 1948 Show*, *Twice a Fortnight* and *Doctor in the House*. Later, The Goodies worked together again on *Astronauts* and *Bananaman*, while Oddie also appeared in children’s programmes including *Tickle on the Tum*, *From the Top* and *The Bubblegum Brigade*.

His later public life was tied to wildlife and ornithology. He presented programmes including *Birding with Bill Oddie*, *Britain Goes Wild with Bill Oddie* and *Bill Oddie’s How to Watch Wildlife*, and co-presented the first four series of *Springwatch* from 2005, as well as the spin-off *Autumnwatch*. He was made an OBE in 2003 for services to wildlife conservation.

A public struggle with depression

BBC News said Oddie’s life was marked by bouts of acute depression and many visits to psychiatric hospitals. He also spoke openly about his own struggles, saying that after being dropped in 2009 he suffered “probably the worst 12 months of my life” because of depression and bipolar disorder.

Former *Springwatch* co-host Kate Humble said he was one of the earliest and bravest public figures to say that mental health was something he had to deal with. She remembered him as someone who could tell a story, make people laugh and encourage anyone to go outside and get excited about a robin.

Other tributes described the same mix of wit, expertise and activism. Chris Packham, Oddie’s successor on *Springwatch*, called him “a renegade polymath with binoculars and a beard” who loved birds and disliked those who harmed them. Samuel West said Oddie had introduced a generation to the fascination of birds, while naturalist Stephen Moss said he “did more than anyone to popularise birding”.

Jo Rowland of the RSPCA said many wildlife rescuers had been inspired by Oddie’s broadcasting and knowledge of British birds, and that “nature has lost one of its best friends, fiercest champions and most iconic defenders.”

South Sudan: Restoring life-saving nutrition services in Akobo

What happened

In Akobo Town, South Sudan, emergency nutrition services have been restored for children and mothers after clashes forced a temporary closure, giving returning families somewhere to treat severe malnutrition even as hunger, displacement and illness continue to strain the county.

A child’s recovery shows why the service matters

When Nyemiem Biel came back home to Akobo Town in Jonglei State after weeks of displacement, she had hoped the worst was over. The family had been in Tiergol, where food was scarce, and soon after their return her 15-month-old son, Chuol Gatbel, developed a fever and diarrhoea.

By the time she reached Akobo Hospital, his condition had worsened. He spent a week in the hospital’s stabilization centre before being referred to an outpatient therapeutic programme in Kony, Akobo Town. A month after treatment began, health workers recorded an encouraging sign: his weight had risen from 9.3 kilogrammes to 10.5 kilogrammes.

Nyemiem now returns to the programme every week to collect 14 sachets of ready-to-use therapeutic food so Chuol can keep receiving treatment at home. “I feel very happy when I have food for my child,” she said.

Services reopened after clashes in Akobo

Chuol’s treatment is part of a wider response in Akobo, where many children are struggling with acute malnutrition after weeks of prolonged hunger, displacement and limited access to healthcare. A rapid emergency response supported by the OCHA-managed UN Global Emergency Fund (CERF) has helped UN agencies and partners restore food assistance and nutrition services for children and mothers at risk.

UNICEF, the World Food Programme, the World Health Organization, Oxfam and Medair have supported the delivery of lifesaving nutrition supplies and services across Akobo. For families returning from displacement, the outpatient therapeutic programme is also a place to turn when children fall sick.

The site had been temporarily closed in mid-March when clashes broke out in Akobo. It reopened on 16 May after Medair’s Emergency Response Team relaunched it under the Emergency Rapid Response Mechanism, with funding from the European Union Civil Protection and Humanitarian Aid Operations and the United Kingdom Foreign, Commonwealth and Development Office.

Since reopening, the site has admitted 377 children. It is one of 12 outpatient therapeutic programmes supported by Medair in Akobo County, where 6,000 children and pregnant and breastfeeding mothers are receiving nutrition support.

“We are thankful for this place,” Nyemiem said. “After we returned from Tiergol, we had nowhere to take our children for treatment.”

Hunger, disease and the return from displacement

The response is reaching families whose displacement left them without enough food for months. In another case in Dengjok Payam, Nyibol Lia arrived at the Thokliel Primary Healthcare Stabilization Centre on 18 June carrying her 10-month-old son, Balat Tut. He had a high fever, diarrhoea and vomiting, and she said he could not even breastfeed.

Nyibol had walked for two hours from neighbouring Galdong Payam to reach the facility. Balat was diagnosed with severe acute malnutrition with medical complications and was started immediately on antibiotics and therapeutic milk. Within a week, the diarrhoea and vomiting had stopped and his weight had increased from 5 kilogrammes to 5.7 kilogrammes.

Like Nyemiem’s family, Nyibol’s family fled to Tiergol after violence erupted in Akobo. They returned home in early May when calm returned, but months without enough food had already taken a toll. “We had no food in Tiergol, and the children were getting sick,” she said. “Now I am happy because we have been given food, water and soap.”

Her family, including her four other children, also received food assistance from WFP. The Thokliel stabilization centre began operating on 15 June and had admitted 10 children with severe acute malnutrition and complications by the end of June. Eight had recovered and were referred to a nearby outpatient therapeutic programme to continue treatment.

Mothers attending the programme also receive counselling on infant and young child feeding, as well as hygiene practices. “We teach mothers how to prepare balanced meals at home and also how to maintain good hygiene to prevent infections,” said Makhor Mai, an infant and young child feeding counsellor at the site.

What happened

Yemen’s Houthis have raised the stakes against Saudi Arabia by threatening a maritime embargo on Saudi ports, while attacks on Sanaa International Airport and Saudi shipping have turned Bab al-Mandeb into a new pressure point alongside the Strait of Hormuz.

Pressure on Bab al-Mandeb

The escalation began after Saudi Arabia attacked Yemen’s Sanaa International Airport more than two weeks ago, after the Houthis had decided to reopen the airport to direct flights from Iran. Those would have been the first such flights since 2015, and Riyadh would have seen them as a major red line and a sign of growing Iranian influence, including the possible transfer of weapons. Hamish Kinnear, principal analyst for the Middle East and North Africa at Maplecroft, told Middle East Eye that the airport strike was also a red line for the Houthis.

The Houthis, officially known as Ansar Allah, have since said they launched missiles at the Saudi cities of Jazan and Yanbu. They also reported targeting two Saudi oil ships last week, while Riyadh confirmed one attack in the Red Sea. Houthi military spokesperson Yahya Saree said the group would “not hesitate to expand our movements and escalate our steps” depending on developments in the coming hours and days.

Saudi Arabia has already diverted more than 70 percent of its crude exports from the Strait of Hormuz to the Red Sea port of Yanbu after the US-Israeli attack on Iran and Iran’s blockade of Hormuz. That shift has made Red Sea routes more important, and the renewed fighting now threatens the Bab al-Mandeb Strait, the narrow passage between the Red Sea and the Gulf of Aden. Before shipping companies began diverting vessels around the Red Sea, it carried about 12 percent of global seaborne oil trade and 8 percent of global liquefied natural gas trade.

Shipping through Bab al-Mandeb has not stopped completely, with some tankers still carrying crude to Asia through the waterway. Kinnear said this showed the Houthis were open to negotiations with the Saudis and the Saudi-backed Yemeni government, which has also said it does not want to widen the confrontation. Khaled Almaeena, a Saudi political analyst, said the escalation was “totally unexpected” and warned that quiet could be taken as weakness, even as he said Saudi Arabia would want to avoid unnecessary bloodshed.
