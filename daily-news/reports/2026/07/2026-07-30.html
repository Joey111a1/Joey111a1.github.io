How the UK's extreme heat is shifting the conversation about air-con
global_front_pageglobal_south_and_exploitedpolitics_and_powerpublic_healthclimate_disastermaterial_harmanchor
What happened

Britain's hot spells are pushing air conditioning from a niche fix into a live policy argument, as residents like Mandy Bourne struggle in overheated flats and landlords, councils and policymakers weigh cooling against the country's long focus on cutting carbon.

Mandy Bourne lives with her 80-year-old mother in a top-floor social housing flat in the West Midlands. Both have health conditions worsened by the recent heatwaves, and Mandy says changes in temperature can trigger flare-ups of her chronic lung condition. "I've got COPD and sometimes I can't breathe," she says. As the flat overheats, she says she is constantly reaching for her inhaler: "It's red hot in the flat. The sun is just beaming in."

Homes that hold on to heat

Across the UK, solid brick homes absorb heat and then radiate it back into rooms that never fully cool down. That retained warmth can push night-time temperatures in urban centres up to 7C higher than the surrounding countryside, part of the urban heat island effect that is at its most dangerous in the early hours, when bodies need cooler air and cannot find it.

Researchers from the University of Birmingham modelled a previous West Midlands heatwave and concluded that the heat-island impact alone accounted for roughly half the 130 heat-related deaths. Jo Shields, head of sustainability for Mandy's housing association Walsall Housing Group, says she keeps hearing the same complaint: "The heat gain stays within the concrete or the brick, so the night-time cooling becomes harder."

Last summer, as the region baked, residents at Ellum Pointe, a whg scheme for over-55s, told their landlords they simply could not cool their flats. The response was improvised. The housing association took the community room, closed all the windows and doors, and put a mobile air-conditioning unit in there to create an "internal cool room."

Why air-con is back in the argument

That kind of stopgap is part of a larger shift. For decades, richer countries have focused on mitigation, while adaptation such as cooling homes and hospitals has had less attention and far less money. In 2023, $1.8trn went to mitigation, while adaptation got $65bn. Last year, Bill Gates argued in a memo that the climate movement's "doomsday" fixation on temperature targets had diverted money from measures that improve lives in a warming world, including cooling. Climate scientists accused him of false dichotomies, and the row exposed the fault line now visible this summer: how much should go to cutting carbon, and how much to adapting to the heat?

Baroness Brown, who chairs the adaptation committee of the Climate Change Committee, says the current system is lopsided. Under the Climate Change Act, the mitigation side recommends legally binding carbon budgets, but adaptation has no such teeth; the government writes its own risk assessment and its own plans. This year, the committee published its report just as the first heatwave struck Britain in May, urging hard adaptation targets and saying support with cooling for the 30% of most vulnerable homes, plus cooling in hospitals and preventive care visits, could reduce 40% of the predicted increase in heat-related deaths by 2050.

The political fight over cooling

Air conditioning is the most contested part of that shift. Only about 5% of English homes have a cooling unit, but the committee recommends fitting them in hospitals and care homes within 10 years, and schools within 25 years. That goes against years of official advice that passive cooling — shade, ventilation and design changes — should come first.

The politics are already visible. Some London councils, following a planning "cooling hierarchy", prioritise low-energy ways of keeping homes cool over air conditioning and, in a small number of cases, have ordered property owners to remove units. The Conservative front bench calls that "dark ages" miserabilism, while other MPs and commentators want an "air-con revolution." Since April, England and Wales have offered £2,500 towards a reversible air-air heat pump through the Boiler Upgrade Scheme, but the support is badged as a heat pump and only worth a third of the £7,500 for a heating-only model; in its first month, only around 45 households claimed it.

The scheme is closed to social housing and newbuilds, so people like Mandy are left out. In Woolwich, a resident called Keisha says her top-floor flat reaches 43C in a heatwave, and she and around twenty neighbours are taking their landlord, PA Housing, to court over homes they say are unfit to live in. The landlord's chief executive accepts that a growing number of residents face "uncomfortably warm temperatures... due to the changing climate" in homes "designed to keep heat in rather than keep them cool."

For Mandy, the issue is immediate rather than theoretical. The cooled community room gives her and her mother some relief, and she says she wants the same in her flat: "We sit there all day with it on, it helps so much I want one in my flat."

How strain changes handedness

The authors describe the piezochiral effect as the induction of chirality by applying strain. Through symmetry analysis, they say uniaxial strain can induce chirality in achiral crystals that contain fragments of opposite chirality within each unit cell. In that framework, the handedness can be tuned by changing the strain direction or by switching between compressive and tensile strain, allowing both left- and right-handed states.

They also place the idea in a broader materials context. Earlier efforts to make chiral materials have relied mainly on bottom-up routes such as crystal growth, molecular synthesis and nanostructure fabrication. The new claim is that strain offers a more general coupling.

The experimental test was carried out in AgGaS₂.
