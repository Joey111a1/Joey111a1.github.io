One report said it also leaves unclear whether officials would search the social media histories of tourists and foreign residents.

What happened

A federal appeals court decision has blocked the Trump administration’s effort to expand mandatory detention for migrants inside the United States, intensifying a split among appellate courts over whether detained immigrants can still seek bond and pushing the dispute closer to the Supreme Court.

The 9th Circuit rejects the detention expansion

On Thursday, the San Francisco-based 9th US Circuit Court of Appeals ruled 2-1 that the Trump administration cannot deny immigrants detained inside the United States a chance for release on bond. The majority said the administration’s reading of the law was not the better one, and the decision left in place a route that many detained noncitizens have traditionally used while their immigration cases are pending.

The ruling matters because it cut against a policy change issued last July, when immigration officials expanded mandatory detention to immigrants in the US interior. That move, which was part of the Trump administration’s mass deportation campaign, triggered a wave of federal lawsuits from immigrants seeking release from custody. Under previous administrations, most noncitizens without a criminal record who were arrested inside the US were allowed to ask for a bond hearing while their cases were pending; mandatory detention was generally reserved for people arrested at the border.

Writing for the majority, 9th Circuit Judge Daniel Bress, a Trump appointee, rejected the administration’s argument that Congress changed immigration law in 1996 to permit mandatory detention beyond the border. “Although no reading of the complicated and interrelated textual provisions at issue here is without some shortcomings, the historical understanding of the statute is the better one,” Bress wrote. He was joined by Judge M Margaret McKeown. Judge Carlos Bea dissented, saying the text and purpose of the 1996 amendment support the administration’s reading.

A widening split among appeals courts

The decision deepened a split between federal appeals courts over the mandatory detention policy. Four other appeals courts have also rejected it, while the Fifth Circuit in New Orleans and the Eighth Circuit in St Louis have sided with the Trump administration. The Guardian also reported that a Chicago-based 7th Circuit panel joined the 9th Circuit in rejecting the policy, in a pair of 2-1 decisions.

That split is now large enough that the Supreme Court may eventually step in. The administration asked the high court last month to take up the issue, and the expanding divide among the circuits increases the chances that it will do so.

The legal fight has also been marked by sharper clashes in the lower courts. Some federal judges who ordered bond hearings have blasted the administration for repeatedly violating their orders, according to the evidence cited in the reports.

The administration’s response and the next court fight

In response to Thursday’s ruling, the US Department of Homeland Security said it “strongly disagrees with the Ninth Circuit panel and is confident in its legal position regarding mandatory detention.” The statement added: “President Donald Trump and Secretary [Markwayne] Mullin are now enforcing the law as it was actually written to keep America safe.”

The immediate question is whether the Supreme Court will take up the dispute after the administration’s request last month. For now, the appellate ruling keeps open the possibility of bond hearings for detained immigrants inside the United States, even as the federal courts remain split over whether the 1996 law requires mandatory detention for people who entered unlawfully.

Instead of asking for new telescope time, she examined observations gathered for unrelated astronomy work and searched them for narrowband radio signals. The findings are being presented this week at the Royal Astronomical Society’s National Astronomy Meeting in Birmingham. The search covered only four archived ALMA observations and did not find any candidate technosignatures above the survey’s detection thresholds.

The work also points to a different way of estimating how many stars have actually been included in SETI data. Astronomers have often relied on catalogues such as Gaia to count “stellar bycatch,” but Mason used the Besançon Galactic Model instead. Applied to an earlier SETI survey with 1,327 telescope pointings, Gaia data identified about 288,000 stars, while the model suggested that more than 6.1 million stars may have been observed. Mason said the result gives a more complete picture of how much of the Milky Way has already been searched and where future SETI work might go next.

Ultrafast X-rays capture chemistry unfolding atom by atom

What happened

Researchers used rapid X-ray flashes at the European XFEL to watch chemistry unfold in real time in 3-fluoropyridine, a small ring-shaped molecule containing nitrogen and fluorine. After a brief ultraviolet laser pulse excited the molecule, they followed how energy moved through it as it bent out of its normally flat structure and passed through a conical intersection. By recording delayed measurements over just a couple of picoseconds, they tracked how the absorbed energy was released and redistributed at specific atoms.

The results showed that different atomic sites carried different parts of the story. The fluorine atom gave a clearer signal for how vibrations relaxed, while the nitrogen atom reflected both electron redistribution and structural change because it was more directly involved in the excitation. The team used time-resolved X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy at the Small Quantum Systems instrument, along with computer simulations and theoretical models, to reconstruct the process. The technique could help scientists examine extremely fast chemical reactions at the atomic scale and in real time, including questions about DNA, light-harvesting materials, and other light-driven processes.

This 4,000-year-old city defied the rules of history

What happened

A University of York study of Mohenjo-daro, the largest city of the Indus civilization, suggests the 4,000-year-old urban center followed a very different path from many early cities. Researchers examined differences in house sizes across the city and found that wealth became more evenly distributed as Mohenjo-daro matured. Instead of inequality growing as the city became larger, more productive, and more successful, the gap between the largest and smallest homes appears to have narrowed over time.

The study also says Mohenjo-daro appears to have been more equal than comparable societies in Mesopotamia and Greece. Lead author Dr. Adam Green said legacy data show the wealth gap had dropped to levels typical of the first farming villages in the city’s later years. The researchers pointed to broad access to practical infrastructure, including brick-lined drains and organized street layouts, along with Indus seals that archaeologists usually find in ordinary homes rather than public buildings. They also noted a standardized system of weights and measures that may have supported fairer trade.

Scientists found evolution repeating itself in the Galápagos Islands

What happened

Scientists studying the Galápagos giant daisies have found that similar leaf shapes evolved more than once in separate branches of the same plant group, using different genes each time, raising questions about how many distinct lineages remain unrecognized.

The work focuses on *Scalesia*, a group of plants on the Galápagos Islands that evolved rapidly after arriving from mainland South America. Professor Michael D. Martin of the Norwegian University of Science and Technology’s University Museum said the islands still “reveal new biology” more than 150 years after Darwin’s visit. The team, which included researchers from the Royal Botanic Gardens, Kew; the University of California, Davis; the University of Copenhagen; the Charles Darwin Foundation; the University of Georgia, Athens; and the University of British Columbia, published its findings in *Nature Communications*.

Different genes, same leaf shape

The researchers analyzed the complete genomes of every known *Scalesia* species. They found that deeply lobed leaves evolved independently several times in separate branches of the family tree. Vanessa Bieker, the study’s first author at Kew, said each time the trait appeared, it did so through different genes within the same biological system controlling leaf development.

The study says *Scalesia* species range from low shrubs to tall trees and live in very different habitats, including humid highland forests and hot, dry lowlands. The leaf form may help plants survive dry conditions by limiting water loss and releasing heat more effectively.

The genetic evidence also suggests the story is not finished. Martin said populations within the same species show large genetic differences and have been isolated for long periods, so new species may be forming. The researchers said many populations may represent distinct evolutionary lineages that have not yet been formally described, and that they should be treated as individual conservation units.
