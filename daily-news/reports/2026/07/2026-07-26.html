What happened

The latest Ebola outbreak in the Democratic Republic of Congo has pushed deaths past 1,300 and confirmed cases to 2,973, while officials and health experts warn the virus is moving faster than any previous outbreak and is spreading in an area where access, security and treatment options are all constrained.

Deaths and cases climb at record pace

Government data released on Saturday showed that the death toll had risen above 1,309, an increase of more than 40 percent in just five days. The same update put the total number of confirmed cases, including deaths, at 2,973 as of Thursday, up 27 percent from the figure reported the previous Saturday.

That pace has led health officials to describe the DRC epidemic as the fastest-spreading Ebola outbreak ever documented. Abdulsalami Nasidi, a public health consultant who helped establish the Africa Centres for Disease Control and Prevention, said the lack of a proven vaccine meant the virus was “spreading like a wildfire.” He also said the epidemic was unfolding in an area of active hostilities, adding to the strain on public health resources.

The scale of the current outbreak has also drawn repeated comparisons with the deadliest Ebola epidemic on record. Between 2013 and 2016, more than 11,000 people died from at least 28,000 cases across Guinea, Liberia and Sierra Leone. That outbreak took about eight months to reach 1,000 deaths; the latest epidemic in the DRC did so in less than 10 weeks.

Why this strain is especially hard to control

The virus spreading in the DRC is Bundibugyo, described in the report as the rarest of the four Ebola variants known to affect humans. It currently has no approved vaccines or treatments, which makes the outbreak harder to contain even as the case count rises.

Nasidi linked that absence of a proven vaccine to the speed of transmission, saying it was one reason the virus was spreading so quickly. The concern is not only the number of people already infected or killed, but how quickly the outbreak is moving through communities that are already difficult to reach.

Africa CDC Director-General Jean Kaseya said last week on X: “We must act now. If we do not stop it today, this will become the worst outbreak the world has ever documented.” The warning reflected the same sense of urgency expressed by health specialists who say the outbreak could overtake earlier epidemics if it is not slowed quickly.

Health workers are under pressure

The response has also been strained by the situation on the ground. Reporting from Goma, Al Jazeera’s Alain Uaykani said the government’s response had lacked coordination and urgency, leaving medical personnel overstretched and working in poor conditions. He said the death toll among health workers continued to rise on Saturday, with a doctor and a health worker among the latest victims.

More than 100 health workers have been infected since the outbreak was declared in May, and around 35 have died. Shortages of protective equipment have worsened the danger, and in some communities residents have been hostile to health teams and question whether the disease is real.

That pressure has spilled into work stoppages at several facilities in northeastern Ituri province, the epicentre of the outbreak. Health workers at Rwampara General Hospital near Bunia went on strike last week, and parts of the hospital’s major Ebola treatment centre were set ablaze in May by angry residents who also chased after health workers fleeing in trucks.

The latest strike began on Saturday at the Elikya Ebola Treatment Centre in Bunia, where activities were largely paralysed as doctors, nurses and security staff stopped work. About 100 workers held a protest outside the centre, saying unpaid bonuses were undermining morale and disrupting patient care. “We need to be paid, because in the meantime the disease is spreading at the treatment centre,” Martin Bolombi, one of the striking workers, told The Associated Press. “I’ve already had five people die who are still inside there. A solution must be found for us,” he said.

A vaccine trial has begun, but the outbreak is still spreading

Scientists are racing to find a way to slow the epidemic, and the University of Oxford’s Oxford Vaccine Group said a volunteer received the first dose of a rapidly developed experimental vaccine on Friday. Katrina Pollock, the Oxford team’s chief investigator, called it “an important milestone for the trial” and said it marked “the next phase in our multinational collaborative journey to develop a Bundibugyo ebolavirus vaccine.”

For now, though, officials say access remains one of the biggest obstacles. Tedros Adhanom Ghebreyesus, the WHO director-general, has warned that the true number of cases could be more than double the official count because insecurity continues to restrict access to affected communities. He said contact tracing reaches roughly 80 percent of known contacts, but response teams still cannot reach every village or deploy where they are needed most.

Tedros has also called for a ceasefire in the conflict gripping eastern DRC, saying medical resources alone are not enough and political action is needed to open access for responders before the virus takes hold in provinces not yet affected. Outside the country, the United States and Canada have both restricted entry for travelers who recently have been in the DRC, even though WHO guidance issued in May had declared the outbreak a global health emergency.

What happened

A month after twin earthquakes killed more than 5,000 people in Venezuela, rescue work is fading into recovery planning, but thousands remain homeless or missing and the country still does not know how it will pay for rebuilding that could cost tens of billions of dollars.

The magnitude 7.2 and 7.5 quakes on June 24 also left 17,000 injured and more than 58,000 buildings destroyed or damaged, according to the figures cited in the reports. More than a month later, rescue workers are still searching for the missing, while reconstruction questions remain open.

Rescue work is turning into a search for the dead

Aid groups often describe the first 72 hours after a major earthquake as the critical window for finding survivors. The UN Office for the Coordination of Humanitarian Affairs said on July 8 that 6,462 people had been rescued, but by then the focus had already started shifting from survival to recovery and burial.

Families in places such as La Guaira are now searching for bodies so they can hold proper funerals. Laura Barrios, who is looking for missing relatives there, said crews had pulled out many dead and that her group had gone in with picks, shovels and machinery but still had not found remains. While the government has not released an official missing-person tally, the UN has estimated the figure could be as high as 51,000.

The rebuilding bill is still climbing

The World Bank said direct physical damage came to nearly $20bn, and that the full price of physical recovery could reach $50bn if rebuilding includes “building back better” and debris clearance. An earlier UN estimate put the cost at $37bn.

The scale of the damage has wider economic implications. The World Bank said GDP could remain below pre-quake levels until 2036 without greater public and private investment, while the country’s labour supply is expected to fall by 1 percent this year. The UN humanitarian relief agency has also said 1.3 million people will need assistance over the next six months.

Aid has started, but the funding gap remains

On July 24, Venezuela marked a month since the quakes with a prayer ceremony near the coastline in La Guaira, the coastal state that authorities described as the most affected. Acting President Delcy Rodríguez presented an updated reconstruction strategy and said more than 128,324 people had received food, water, medical care and temporary shelter. She also said 200 homes had been delivered, 4,000 more were planned, and the response involved 2,200 international rescuers and 31,475 local volunteers.

Help has also come from abroad. The United States said it would commit $300m in financial assistance, Rodríguez said Venezuela had secured $346m in emergency financing from the IMF, and the Development Bank of Latin America and the Caribbean said it was working to channel more funds, including from the private sector. The UN said the European Commission had donated $36.5m, along with $17m from its Central Emergency Response Fund and $9m from Germany.

Even with those pledges, the UN humanitarian response plan remains far short of the $931m needed for 2026, with only 39 percent covered and a shortfall of about $566m. Some experts have warned that remaining US sanctions could complicate the flow of aid, while civil society groups say poverty, information controls and hostility toward humanitarian organizations could slow the recovery. Beatriz Borges of Cepaz said the earthquakes did not create Venezuela’s crisis but exposed it.

In related background, the College of Engineers of Venezuela said more than 8,000 structures in Caracas had been inspected after the June 24 quakes, and that checks would continue in denser, older areas of the capital.

What happened

The English-speaking Colombian islands of San Andrés and Providence are being pulled into the fallout from US airstrikes on speedboats allegedly carrying drugs, as Raizal fishers describe missing men, shrinking livelihoods and a fear that the sea has become a place of sudden death.

An island world with its own language and history

Officially, San Andrés and Providence belong to Colombia, but most of the local Afro-descendant population does not speak Spanish as its first language. Among the brightly painted wooden houses and verandas, the most commonly heard language is Creole, a distinct variety of Caribbean English.

The islands lie north-west of mainland Colombia, between South and North America, and that location has long shaped them. Young men with seafaring skills have been recruited for years by groups moving cocaine produced in Colombia northwards, often as far as the US. They are members of the Raizal people, defined above all by African heritage and by a history shaped by different waves of settlement.

The islands’ sea is central to daily life. It provides fish for local communities and draws tourists, but residents also describe it as a place where fishers disappear. Edgar Jay Stephens, 58, the president of the main local fishers’ association, I-Fish, said the islands’ involvement in trafficking is not new, but that residents see it as growing and taking on a more dramatic form since the US began bombing speedboats allegedly carrying drugs in the Caribbean and eastern Pacific.

Missing men and fear at sea

The airstrikes have killed 215 people since September, in what the UN has condemned as “extrajudicial killings”. Against that backdrop, Stephens said at least 16 fishers from San Andrés and Providence have gone missing since January.

He said some of them might have been carrying drugs, but “certainly” not all. Disappearances have been a decades-long problem on the islands, he added, whether the men were killed, arrested by other countries or lost to harsh conditions at sea. But, he said, the current pace was unprecedented.

US Southern Command, which conducts the airstrikes, said it “does not comment on specific operational details” and added: “We have no information of any strikes, operations, seizures, or arrests involving residents of San Andrés or Providence.”

Stephens said he did not rule out US involvement, and asked: “How many people could have died without us knowing what happened to them?” Human rights activists have made a similar criticism, saying the US does not release information about the victims, which makes identification difficult.

One family member said her brother had been trafficking drugs with two other Raizal men. She said he took the job because it was easy money and he wanted to buy land and build a house. When she saw news of a US airstrike on a speedboat carrying three men near Costa Rica, she sent his documents to Colombia’s embassy in the neighbouring country, hoping he might be identified among the dead. He was not.

‘We’re living with fear’

Raizal fishers say the airstrikes are affecting more than missing men. Ling Jay Robinson, 38, said there are days when he chooses not to go out to sea because he fears US military crews could mistake fishers for traffickers and bomb them.

“We’ve changed our routines,” Robinson said. “We’re living with fear.” He said fishing is a family inheritance that runs through his father, grandfather, great-grandfather and great-great-grandfather.

The islands have also lived with disappearance for decades. Miguel Angel Castell Cano, San Andrés’s government secretary, said: “Everyone has a relative who has been killed or disappeared at sea.” He added that his own father has been missing for 10 years after a drug-trafficking trip near Honduras.

Writer and lawyer Maríamatilde Rodríguez said families are often reluctant to speak openly about missing relatives because the islands are deeply religious and no one wants a loved one subjected to moral judgment. In the 1990s and 2000s, she led an investigation that, using documents and interviews with dozens of relatives, arrived at a figure of nearly 700 disappeared people across the two islands over the previous 30 years. She said there is no official Colombian data and that updating the count would require a similarly extensive effort.

For Stephens and other islanders, the immediate concern is that the sea is becoming more dangerous while the number of trafficking trips appears to be rising. He said the US bombing campaign has not stopped the trade and that it has drawn in more fishers because there are so few other opportunities. “We need more jobs for young people,” he said. “Because they have so few opportunities here, some of them end up taking that path.”

What happened

Venezuela said it will formally leave the International Criminal Court this Friday, turning a long-running dispute over the court’s conduct into an immediate break over sovereignty, bias and the use of international justice.

Foreign Minister Félix Plasencia González said the withdrawal was being processed under Article 127 of the Rome Statute, the provision for states that want to leave the court’s jurisdiction, and said he acted on direct instructions from Acting President Delcy Rodríguez. Venezuela also said it had notified UN Secretary-General António Guterres of its “firm and irrevocable” decision to denounce the statute and begin its final withdrawal from the ICC.

Caracas’ criticism of the ICC

The move formalizes what Venezuela described as a longstanding critique of the ICC’s record. The Foreign Ministry said the court shows a “demonstrated geographical bias,” arguing that its investigations disproportionately concentrate on African and Latin American countries. Caracas said that pattern undermines the principle of equality before the law that the institution was meant to uphold.

The Bolivarian Government said the “imbalance reveals an international justice system that has lost equity as its compass.” Plasencia warned that the court’s mechanisms have been instrumentalized to deepen inequalities among nations and to disregard the right of peoples to self-determination and sovereignty. He also said Caracas rejects any suggestion that this is a matter of procedural coincidence.

In the government’s telling, the ICC has moved far from the purpose Venezuela says it was created to serve. Plasencia described it as an institution that placed its tools at the service of interests “far removed from justice and the very peoples it claims to protect.” Venezuela’s formal notice framed the decision as a challenge to a court it says has not applied justice evenly.

The legal step and the message behind it

Venezuela’s withdrawal was set out as a legal denunciation of the Rome Statute under Article 127, which the government presented as the route for ending its participation in the court system. The Foreign Ministry said the decision was communicated to the UN Secretary-General, and Plasencia repeated that the retreat from the ICC was “firm and irrevocable.”

The government tied the departure to a broader argument about lawfare, saying international legal tools have been used in ways that perpetuate political persecution and widen inequality. It said the court’s performance reflects a proven bias that has concentrated its work disproportionately in African and Latin American countries, to the detriment of the Global South.

Venezuela said the denunciation was also meant to reaffirm its support for a more equitable justice system that respects sovereignty and self-determination. In that sense, the withdrawal was presented not only as a departure from the ICC, but as an explicit rejection of what Caracas sees as the court’s unequal application of international justice.

Powerful Facts About the July 26 Moncada Assault on the Barracks That Changed Cuba
global_south_and_exploitedwar_and_conflictmaterial_harmpolitics_and_powerregional expansion
What happened

The July 26 Moncada assault turned a failed attack on a Santiago de Cuba barracks into the founding memory of Cuba’s revolutionary movement, and it still matters because the raid’s military defeat, repression, and political afterlife became inseparable.

The assault on the Guillermón Moncada barracks took place in Santiago de Cuba on July 26, 1953, after Fulgencio Batista had seized power in the March 10, 1952 coup and shut off legal routes for progressive political forces. In that setting of political repression, social inequality, and closed institutions, the attack emerged as a response to dictatorship and exclusion, and to the belief that only armed struggle could reopen the path to national change.

The city itself gave the episode its symbolic force. Carnival, popular tradition, and anti-colonial memory converged in Santiago de Cuba, and the operation was timed to take advantage of the confusion created by the festivities. The assault involved a small group of young revolutionaries led by Fidel Castro, many of them later identified by him as part of the “Generation of the Centennial,” in reference to José Martí’s birth. Their aim was not just to fire on a barracks, but to seize weapons, ignite a popular uprising, and challenge Batista from inside a military stronghold.

A failed raid with a larger political meaning

The attack began in the early hours of July 26 with around 150 rifles, short firearms, and only three weapons of war. The military objective failed, but the account says the historical meaning of the action quickly outgrew the battlefield result. Moncada became, in this telling, the opening chapter of a revolutionary process that would later overthrow the Batista dictatorship, and the phrase “July 26” became the name of the movement that followed.

The balance of casualties was severe. According to the account, Batista’s forces suffered 46 casualties, including 19 dead and 27 wounded between the Moncada barracks in Santiago de Cuba and the Carlos Manuel de Céspedes barracks in Bayamo. The revolutionary forces reported 8 dead and 10 wounded in combat, while later killings of detained rebels raised the number of martyrs to 61. Among the participants were two women, Haydée Santamaría and Melba Hernández, who later became central in preserving the memory of the attack and its political legacy.

Repression after the assault

What followed the assault mattered almost as much as the raid itself. The account describes the Moncada barracks as a site where the Batista regime carried out torture, execution, and a coordinated effort to hide the scale of the violence. Fidel Castro was captured by a soldier named Pedro Sarría, who reportedly advised him not to reveal his true identity. Sarría then refused to hand him over to other officers and took him to the Vivac instead of returning him to the barracks, a decision that likely saved Fidel’s life.

A famous photograph of Fidel seated beneath an image of José Martí was taken at the Vivac, where he was interrogated by Alberto del Río Chaviano, known as the “Jackal of the East.” According to the account, Chaviano ordered journalists and photographers to surrender their film and prevent any record of the killings from leaving the scene. The role of the press became crucial after that. Marta Rojas, then a journalism student, arrived in Santiago on the first flight from Havana and witnessed the aftermath firsthand. Her reporting and later recollections described signs of torture, bloodstains, beaten bodies, and a staged attempt by officials to reconstruct the crime scene before the press could fully inspect it. Bohemia preserved some of the evidence, and photographer Panchito Cano secured images of the victims.

From setback to revolutionary program

The Moncada defeat did not end the movement. The surviving rebels moved toward the Sierra Maestra and a deeper phase of political organization, and the setback was turned into the beginning of a broader insurrection. The account says the movement survived by transforming defeat into political strength, prison into school, and memory into program.

That program emerged in Fidel Castro’s self-defense speech at trial, later known as “History Will Absolve Me.” Written and reconstructed in prison, the speech became the ideological text that gave coherence to the revolutionary cause. The account says Fidel used lemon juice to write portions of it in secret, preserving the text line by line so it could circulate later. Thirty revolutionary combatants were tried, many were sentenced to prison on Pinos Island, and Fidel received the harshest sentence: 15 years.

Even imprisonment did not end the organization. The account says public pressure and protests by mothers and supporters helped secure the release of the Moncada prisoners, while Fidel kept writing to Melba Hernández and Haydée Santamaría to insist that the movement remained alive and needed discipline, structure, and continuity. In that sense, the assault failed as a conventional attack but succeeded in creating a revolutionary nucleus that would keep going.

Cyclospora outbreaks start with human contamination. Nobody has ever been able to figure out how

What happened

Cyclospora is driving a large U.S. outbreak season, but the parasite’s human origin has only sharpened the central mystery: health officials know contamination comes from people, yet they still have not definitively figured out how it gets into food and water.

As cases of explosive diarrhea rise across the country this summer, investigators are dealing with what CNN described as the biggest U.S. cyclospora caseload ever recorded, with more than 11,500 confirmed and suspected cases across 41 states, according to the CDC. The cases were acquired in the U.S., not abroad, which makes this year unusual. Dr. Keith Schneider of the University of Florida said the usual annual total is 2,000 to 4,000 cases, and generally about one in five is tied to international travel. Cases also tend to rise in summer.

Cyclospora has one clear source: humans. It has only ever been found in human feces, and Dr. Mark Moorman, who recently retired as director of the Office of Food Safety at the FDA, said “Humans are the source.” But the harder question has never been answered. Investigators have never definitively identified the cause of an outbreak, even when they find evidence that points near produce growing or processing. This year, the FDA has launched investigations into six outbreaks. The largest is a nine-state Midwest outbreak believed linked to iceberg lettuce grown in Mexico, and the agency is also looking into a cluster of 72 related cases with no known source yet.

Why the source is so hard to pin down

Cyclospora cases are difficult to trace because symptoms can take up to two weeks to appear, and people may not seek medical care until three weeks to a month after exposure. By then, stool testing, local reporting, interviews, and the process of reconstructing what people ate can all take time. Moorman said many investigations end without a clear “smoking gun.”

One of the closest clues came in 2020, when an FDA investigation tied Fresh Express bagged salad kits to more than 700 cases across 14 states. Testing found cyclospora in two samples from a canal used for irrigation at a Florida farm that grew red cabbage for the mix. But the FDA said it could not determine conclusively whether the canal contamination caused the outbreak, and Moorman said investigators still could not figure out how feces got into the canal in the first place.

Most experts think contaminated water is the main route onto produce. Jennifer McEntire, a microbiologist who advises food companies, said “Water is number one,” but the source of the human fecal contamination remains unclear. Moorman said there is no law requiring farms to test irrigation water or produce for cyclospora, though big buyers could require it and the government could pass testing rules or fund more research.
