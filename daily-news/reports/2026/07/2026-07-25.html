How Chinese tech and Russian tactics are aiding Iran’s bombing of US bases

What happened

Iran’s strikes on US bases and Gulf infrastructure have become more accurate, and the picture emerging from officials and analysts is that Chinese satellite imagery and Russian-style tactics may be helping — even as some experts dispute how direct that assistance is.

Chinese imagery and target selection

Last Friday’s attack on the Muwaffaq Salti airbase in Jordan killed three US soldiers, despite the base being protected by a Thaad air defence system. Iranian media later released satellite imagery showing several buildings had been destroyed. Why the air defence system failed is not clear, and the incident is under Pentagon investigation.

The strike has become a reference point in the broader debate over how Iran is improving its attacks. Andreas Krieg, an associate professor at the school of security studies at King’s College London, said the combination of better intelligence, missile navigation, manoeuvrability and coordinated attack planning amounted to “a real transformation”. He said that came after three years of technological development in Iran’s ballistic missile programme, which US intelligence estimates still judge to be roughly 70% intact despite earlier claims from Washington that Tehran’s military capabilities had been obliterated.

One part of that picture is Chinese satellite imagery. The first step, the evidence says, was to use it to help identify targets at US bases and other sites in the Gulf. The US imposed sanctions on three Chinese companies in May for providing such images. One of them, Mizar Vision, exposed the presence of US military aircraft and Patriot and Thaad air defence systems at bases in Saudi Arabia, Bahrain and the Jordanian base hit last week, and imagery released on social media was widely picked up.

Russia’s shadow in Iran’s tactics

Alongside the imagery question is a second one: whether Iran has learned from Russia’s war in Ukraine. The evidence says a string of accurate Iranian attacks on CIA facilities across the Gulf made US analysts wonder whether Russia had shared locations with Tehran, but that hypothesis is tentative.

Sidharth Kaushal of the Royal United Services Institute said Iran had focused on static targets at US bases, for which commercial imagery was enough. “I haven’t seen anything where Iran has struck a target so dynamic that I’ve looked at this and gone ‘this has got to be foreign aid,’” he said. Some experts, then, are less sure Beijing has been providing intelligence in the way the more sweeping claims suggest.

Even so, the tactical parallels with Russia are part of the story. Iran has evolved its missile attacks using methods similar to Russia in Ukraine, where smaller salvos can be more effective than larger ones and drones are used to distract air defences before a ballistic attack. Kaushal said that looked more like “a convergent evolution” between the two militaries than direct Russian tutoring.

Matthew Savill, a defence analyst from the Royal United Services Institute, said there is “a kind of collaboration” among Iran, Russia and China, and also North Korea, but he said the so-called “axis of upheaval” is far from a consistent military alliance. That leaves the degree of direct help open to interpretation, even as the attacks themselves appear more capable.

What the attacks have shown

The evidence also points to the hardware and planning behind the strikes. Iran’s ballistic programme relies heavily on Chinese electronics and ammonium perchlorate for rocket fuel, and the finished weapons are considered sophisticated enough to occasionally defeat stretched US and Gulf air defences. Iranian missiles use complex navigation, including GPS, China’s BeiDou and Russia’s Glonass simultaneously as they launch towards targets.

Their re-entry speeds, several times the speed of sound, make interception difficult, and Tehran has also developed steerable warheads that can adjust course during descent on some missiles, according to Krieg. Heat-seeking and optical navigation in the terminal phase also means they are not vulnerable to electronic jamming because they are not using radio communication.

Those capabilities matter because US air defences are already under strain. The evidence says stockpiles of Patriot and Thaad interceptors have been run down by about half after the 38-day US-Israel bombing campaign in the spring. Add in energy sites, oil refineries and desalination plants, and there is a large area to cover.

The official line remains mixed. US defence secretary Pete Hegseth told Congress this week that China and Russia were helping Iran to some extent. Donald Trump contradicted him on Friday, saying Xi Jinping and Vladimir Putin had personally assured him they would not sell weapons to Iran. The result is a picture of Iranian strikes that are clearly becoming more precise, while the extent of foreign assistance — especially from China and Russia — remains contested.

Gabriela Rodríguez*: Demographic impact of climate change
global_front_pageglobal_south_and_exploitedpolitics_and_powerclimate_disastermaterial_harmregional expansion
What happened

Climate-linked disasters are pushing internal displacement onto Mexico’s demographic agenda, with Gabriela Rodríguez and Conapo warning that floods, droughts, earthquakes and other events are straining cities, water systems and public finances even as the exact climate share of those movements remains hard to pin down.

Gabriela Rodríguez said disasters associated with extreme hydrometeorological phenomena and sudden geophysical events had displaced nearly 80 million people internally worldwide by 2023. She added that 7.7 million people were displaced by disasters in 2023 alone, and that such shocks can intensify global poverty and reverse development gains when they interact with socioeconomic, demographic or political vulnerabilities.

From floods to slow-onset losses

The study groups the relevant hazards into sudden hydrometeorological events linked to global warming and climate change, such as floods, hurricanes — also called tropical cyclones or typhoons — heat waves, cold waves and wildfires. It also includes geophysical events such as earthquakes, tsunamis, landslides, collapses and mudslides.

It also points to slow-onset phenomena associated with climate change, including droughts, thawing or permafrost melt, the loss of coastal zones, desertification and lack of access to essential resources such as water. On that point, the text says it is difficult to establish to what extent climate change will contribute to sudden disasters and slow-onset events, but the evidence indicates that some of them will increase and may be irreversible, such as droughts and loss of coastal zones.

Mexico’s population policy is taking note

In Mexico, Rodríguez said population policy is paying greater attention to the demographic impact of climate change alongside the decline in fertility and the aging of the population, which are unfolding at different speeds across the country’s regions. She said people displaced by these events tend to move elsewhere, adding pressure on urban infrastructure and the water crisis.

The study also says displaced people suffer irreparable human and material losses that worsen quality of life and raise marginalization. It adds that Mexico has made progress in civil protection, with regulatory frameworks, governmental structures and budgets for disasters associated with natural phenomena, but still faces major challenges in prevention and post-disaster response.

Those challenges are also fiscal. The text says the costs of restoring conditions after a disaster have become increasingly substantial and have required extraordinary action by local and national governments, as well as collaboration from international organizations, the private sector and civil society.

What the study looked at

To deepen knowledge about population displacement due to climate change and disasters in Mexico, the study was produced through a collaboration between the technical teams of the National Population Council (Conapo) and the Laura Gabriela Rubio Díaz Leal consultancy, on behalf of the United Nations High Commissioner for Refugees (UNHCR). Rodríguez said the aim is to guide policies that can anticipate and meet the needs of people affected by these situations.

The publication drew on the socioeconomic impact database of damages and losses caused by disasters in Mexico compiled by the National Center for Disaster Prevention for 2013 to 2023, the Flood Catalog compiled by the National Coordination of Civil Protection and the National Water Commission, and verified information in the media. For hydrometeorological and geophysical events, it identified 1,037,910 displacements over the 11 years analyzed.

The broader message of the study is that prevention, adaptation and resilience to climate change have not been a priority for economic elites, even though population settlements and human activity have expanded into risk areas. Rodríguez said that knowing and measuring these effects is strategic for anticipating them and for building a more complete demographic diagnosis of Mexico.

Referring first, then tracking

Embodied visual tracking asks a mobile agent to continuously follow a specific target described in natural language using only onboard vision. The ReferTrack paper argues that the hard part is not just planning a path, but first identifying which person in a crowded scene matches the instruction. It positions that identification step as a compact choice over indexed bounding boxes, rather than as abstract chain-of-thought reasoning in a latent space.

The model’s pipeline reflects that choice. It first selects a target from an indexed set of detected pedestrian boxes, then predicts tracking waypoints conditioned on that image-grounded decision. To carry motion cues forward, it keeps a sliding-window queue of previously selected boxes and injects their geometry into the visual history through temporal-viewpoint-bbox indicator, or TVBI, tokens. The paper says this gives the system a target-specific memory while keeping the reasoning tied to visible regions instead of abstract spatial codes.

To reinforce that referring ability, the authors also co-train on a custom Refer-QA dataset built around the same indexed-bbox selection interface. In the paper’s framing, this makes the identification step more directly supervised and more aligned with the kind of grounding a vision-language-action model needs for embodied tracking.

What the model was trained and tested on

ReferTrack is trained on two matched data sources: 1.3 million expert tracking trajectories from the EVT-Bench training split in Habitat 3.0, and 1.3 million custom referring samples synthesized from SYNTH-PEDES. The paper says the two are mixed 1:1 and trained in a two-stage supervised fine-tuning setup. Stage 1 trains the vision projector on general multimodal QA data, and Stage 2 jointly fine-tunes the navigation and Refer-QA tasks.

For evaluation, the authors use EVT-Bench under a single forward-view protocol, with success rate, tracking rate, and collision rate reported on Single-Target Tracking, Distracted Tracking, and Ambiguity Tracking splits. The implementation uses Qwen3-4B as the language backbone and an indexed bbox catalog built from YOLO11 plus ByteTrack detections.

On that benchmark, the paper says ReferTrack reaches 89.4/92.5/1.6 on STT, 73.3/81.8/7.6 on DT, and 74.1/85.7/7.7 on AT. ReferTrack was also tested in real-world qualitative deployment on two robotic platforms, each with a single forward-facing camera. On-policy self-distillation is attractive because it removes the external teacher used in on-policy distillation, but the paper says it still needs an asymmetry between teacher and student. The paper says the token-wise log-probability difference between those two teacher outputs highlights candidates whose likelihood is increased by the instance-level visual content. The method is presented as not needing an external teacher, privileged answers, visual evidence signals, reasoning traces, or extra inference-time cost. In the paper’s framing, the only changing ingredient is the visual condition, which is what supplies the self-distillation asymmetry.

Reported results.

The preprint’s larger claim is that visual contrast can stand in for the auxiliary signals that earlier self-distillation methods relied on, making the target both on-policy and visually informed without adding another teacher pipeline.

Shorouq Al-Ashqar, a researcher at the Mansoura University Vertebrate Paleontology Center and a first author of the study, said those features suggest a flexible, mainly fruit-based diet, with the ability to process harder foods such as nuts or seeds when needed.

What the fossil suggests about ape origins

The researchers said the anatomy and age of *Masripithecus* point to a more complicated early ape history than the fossil record had implied. They used Bayesian methods that combined anatomical traits from living and extinct apes, DNA from living species, and the geological ages of known fossils to estimate relationships and the timing of evolutionary splits. Their results indicate that *Masripithecus* was more closely related to living apes than any Early Miocene species currently known from East Africa.

They also ran biogeographic analyses to ask where the common ancestor of all living apes most likely lived. Those analyses point to northern Africa and the Middle East during the Early Miocene. The team said gaps in the fossil record may have distorted the history of crown Hominoidea, the group that includes all living apes and their last common ancestor. The researchers expect more fieldwork in North Africa and the Middle East will uncover additional fossils that help clarify how modern apes originated and diversified.
