'It feels like hell': Gaza families battle suffocating summer heat in tents
global_front_pageglobal_south_and_exploitedwar_and_conflictpublic_healthclimate_disastermaterial_harmregional expansion
What happened

Gaza families living in tents and school shelters are now enduring a summer they say traps heat, insects and thirst inside flimsy coverings, turning the same shelters that failed them in winter into another hazard as displacement, damaged services and scarce relief leave little escape.

From winter cold to a greenhouse in summer

In January, Rajaa Jendiya described Gaza’s winter as the harshest she had endured in nearly two years of displacement. Now the rain has stopped, and the same tent that did not protect the Palestinian widow and mother of three from the cold has become, she said, a trap for stifling heat under the summer sun.

“It’s exactly like living in a greenhouse,” Jendiya said. “The way the tent traps the sun’s rays and concentrates the heat creates the same conditions as a greenhouse.” She said the temperature and humidity become so intense that it feels “literally suffocating” and she has to go outside.

Jendiya lost her husband when Israeli forces killed him on 14 March 2024 in one of the “flour massacres” during Israel’s siege and starvation of northern Gaza. After her home in Shujaiya, in eastern Gaza, was destroyed in an Israeli bombing, she is among the hundreds of thousands of Palestinians in Gaza who remain displaced, living in makeshift shelters such as the school in the heart of Gaza City where she now stays.

Heat, mosquitoes and sleepless nights

With little access to basic services and few ways to escape the heat, summer has brought a new set of problems for displaced families. Jendiya said the insects are as much a burden as the temperature itself.

“The heat is something, and the insects that come with it are another thing,” she said. “All night long, we cannot sleep because of the mosquitoes, and once the sun rises, flies take over.” She said the problem has become so severe that “we cannot sleep either at night or during the day,” and that the heat has brought “new kinds of insects we have never seen before.”

The scale of displacement in Gaza gives those complaints wider weight. Around 1.9 million of Gaza’s 2.1 million residents remain displaced, according to Unicef, many of them living in tents and other makeshift shelters. Gaza’s summers are also marked by high humidity because of its Mediterranean location, with average temperatures around 30-32C and frequent heatwaves above 35C.

Jendiya says she regularly takes her children to hospital because of illnesses linked to the unhealthy conditions in the school-turned-displacement camp. “Inside and around the school, you often see sewage water overflowing, while rubbish accumulates, attracting flies and insects,” she said. “These conditions become even worse with the heat, and many times my children have developed skin diseases.”

Health workers have reported that skin diseases have become increasingly widespread among displaced Palestinians across the Gaza Strip, including scabies, fungal infections, rashes and lice, particularly among children.

No shade, no clean water, no way to cool down

For other displaced Palestinians, the problem is not only the heat inside a shelter but the absence of any place to escape it. Mona al-Mallahi, 47, has set up her tent on a street island opposite al-Azhar University of Gaza, and she says she spends much of her day outside because the tent becomes unbearable.

“There is no escape from the heat. During the day, we cannot stay inside the tent because it becomes unbearably hot, but outside there is no shade to protect us from the blazing sun,” she said. “We spend every day searching for a way to breathe.”

Al-Mallahi said the lack of water makes the heat harder to bear. “We are drenched in sweat from the moment we wake up until we go to sleep. I need to shower at least five times a day just to cope, but that’s impossible,” she said. “We barely have enough water for the most basic needs, let alone to cool ourselves down.” Families often collect water for washing and bathing by filling containers from nearby schools, homes or displacement sites, while drinking water is harder still to get and can require hours of queuing at distribution trucks.

The destruction of more than 81 percent of Gaza’s buildings has made conditions even harsher for people living in tents, while more than 80 percent of the Strip’s water, sanitation and hygiene infrastructure has been damaged or destroyed. That has left many displaced people without what they describe as the “luxury” of running water, even as the summer sun intensifies the hardship.

Even a roof does not bring relief

The burden is not limited to people sleeping in tents. Emad al-Sirsawi, who lives in a damaged apartment in Tal al-Hawa, southwest of Gaza City, said the only difference between his situation and that of people in tents is that he has a ceiling above his head.

“The only difference is that we have a ceiling above our heads to protect us from the sun. But we still suffer from the heat and insects with very little protection,” he said. “We don’t have air conditioners or fans. And even if some of us had them before the war, we cannot use them because there is no electricity, or because we cannot afford the electricity supplied by private generators.”

Since the beginning of Israel’s military offensive on Gaza in October 2023, the Strip has been plunged into darkness because of fuel shortages and the destruction of much of its electricity infrastructure. The electricity authority has not been able to restore a reliable supply, even partially, and private generator owners sell limited electricity at extremely high prices. In some areas, one kilowatt-hour costs around 35 Israeli shekels, or about $11.5, leaving most residents able to use it only for essentials such as lighting and charging devices.

That shortage also affects food and cooking. Al-Sirsawi said refrigerators often cannot be used, so food spoils by the end of the day. He said families are forced to cook every day, but without cooking gas they rely on firewood even in the extreme heat. “No one can find comfort in Gaza, neither in summer nor in winter,” he said.

What happened

A 6.8-magnitude earthquake in Kumamoto prefecture killed at least two people and injured at least 100, with the most urgent questions centered on the Aeon Mall Kumamoto, where rescuers were pulling people out after an explosion and searching for anyone still missing.

The quake struck southern Kyushu late on Tuesday afternoon, damaging buildings, roads and bridges and knocking out power to thousands of homes. CNN said the earthquake was recorded on Kyushu, about 550 miles from Tokyo, and noted that the area was still recovering from the 2016 Kumamoto quakes, which killed hundreds and caused widespread damage.

The mall became the hardest-hit and most uncertain site

The prefectural government said rescuers working overnight at the Aeon Mall Kumamoto shopping centre had taken eight people out of the building. Two women in their 20s were confirmed dead, one other person was reported to be in cardiopulmonary arrest, and the remaining five were injured but not thought to be in life-threatening condition.

Firefighters had said earlier that an explosion occurred in the mall shortly after the earthquake. A government official told reporters that a gas leak had been reported, but the cause was still not known. NHK reported that about 20 to 30 workers at the mall were unaccounted for, while fire services said “many people” were trapped inside.

Footage showed one side of the mall, the prefecture’s largest with roughly 200 shops, torn away, exposing steel beams and leaving debris across the neighbouring car park. Several ambulances and fire engines were parked outside as rescue teams searched for survivors.

Damage spread beyond the shopping centre

Kyodo News agency said a hospital in Yatsushiro city accepted about 40 people with injuries from the quake. The Guardian also cited NHK saying two hospitals reported more than 50 injuries each, including 10 in a serious condition.

Other damage was reported around the prefecture. A train derailed and fell on its side at Yatsushiro station, and stone walls were damaged at Kumamoto Castle, Kyodo said. In the nearby town of Mashiki, Shinto priest Yozo Kai said a few stone lanterns fell down. “I worried if the main hall and the gate would tilt again,” he told Kyodo.

Hiroki Shimoda, an official at Mifune town hall, said rooftiles from nearby homes crashed down to the ground. “The shaking reminded me of the Kumamoto quake (10 years ago) and I was frightened,” he said.

Aftershocks kept the prefecture on edge

CNN said at least six additional quakes were recorded in the region after the larger incident, with magnitudes ranging from 4.4 to 5.6. The Japan Meteorological Agency said the biggest tremors would remain at risk of further strong quakes and landslides for about a week, and that more than 60 aftershocks ranging in magnitude from 1 to 4 had already been recorded.

The disaster management agency said about 300,000 people had been instructed to go to evacuation centres. A tsunami warning was issued immediately after the quake, but was lifted within two hours.

Japan’s prime minister, Sanae Takaichi, said authorities were still assessing the extent of the damage. She said people had been injured and that “power outages and fires have occurred in some areas,” along with damage to roads, bridges and buildings.

‘The dream I carried is gone’: Miscarriages rise amid trauma of Gaza’s war

What happened

Pregnancy losses are rising in Gaza as doctors and women describe war, displacement, hunger and collapsing medical care shaping pregnancies that end in miscarriage, stillbirth or emergency birth, with one mother in Deir el-Balah saying repeated losses have left her long-held dream of motherhood in ruins.

A mother’s repeated losses

In Deir el-Balah, Gaza, Nuha Abu Lebda said she had dreamed for years of becoming a mother. Since the war began in October 2023, she has been forcibly displaced several times across the Gaza Strip and has lost three pregnancies.

She said the losses came after months of air strikes, fear, hunger and living without proper medical care. “I miscarried because of the air strikes, the displacement, the difficult conditions, the lack of clean water and malnutrition,” she said. After each miscarriage, she hoped she could still become a mother, but after the latest loss she said, “the dream I carried for 10 years was gone.”

Abu Lebda said she sold her gold and almost everything she owned to pay for heparin injections, a drug prescribed to some women to prevent blood clotting during pregnancy. She hoped the treatment would help her carry a pregnancy to term, but she lost the babies anyway. “I cried so much because I lost the baby I dreamed of every single day,” she said.

Even after the fighting slowed, she said, the hope remained the same. “I dream of bringing a baby into this world. I want to be a mother and have children,” she said.

Doctors say the losses are increasing

At al-Awda Hospital, doctors said they are seeing a sharp rise in pregnancy losses. Dr Yasser Saadeddine, the maternity director at the hospital in Deir el-Balah in central Gaza, said roughly 70% of the pregnancy losses his team treats are linked to the war and blockade.

He said the increase has worsened as Gaza’s health system has collapsed under bombardments and Israeli ground operations, with malnutrition and psychological strain among the main drivers. Doctors at the hospital said they have seen more miscarriages, more premature deliveries and more dangerous pregnancy complications.

Saadeddine said the causes include “the war and the blockade, including malnutrition, poor hygiene, environmental contamination and continuous displacement.” He also said many women have lost homes, medical records and access to regular check-ups during repeated displacements, while hospitals struggle with shortages of medicines, equipment and medical staff.

Other women describe the same pattern

Abu Lebda is one of several women in Gaza who told of losing pregnancies or babies while trying to survive the war. Mai Zaqout said she lost her daughter during childbirth because she could not access the medical care she needed. She said her daughter had died in her womb a month earlier, but she could not deliver because the medical resources and support she needed were unavailable. Her loss came soon after her husband was killed.

Sara al-Awad said powerful explosions in Tal al-Hawa, a neighbourhood in Gaza City, caused extreme fear and stress when she was eight months pregnant, leading to severe bleeding. She said no ambulance could reach her, so after waiting for hours she was taken to al-Quds Hospital in a wheelchair by her brother. Doctors decided she needed an emergency C-section immediately. Her baby, Muhammad, was born weighing 1.1kg and placed in an incubator. She said fighting around the hospital later separated her family from him, and they could only check on him through messages from medical staff until they were reunited after more than a month.

Other women described similar losses. Iman Badah said she lost her only son after giving birth prematurely while being displaced. Nuha Salameh said fear and trauma caused her to lose twins. Nidaa Seyam said she suffered a second miscarriage during the war and was told by doctors that psychological stress and malnutrition had affected the pregnancy.

For women in Gaza, the war has left wounds that persist in every lost pregnancy and every interrupted attempt to become a mother.

9 million still miss HIV treatment, WHO warns
global_front_pagepublic_healthmaterial_harmregional expansion
What happened

WHO says the global response to HIV has made major gains, but nearly nine million people are still without treatment and recent funding cuts, humanitarian shocks and inequalities could still upset the drive to end HIV, viral hepatitis and STIs as public health threats by 2030.

The warning comes from WHO’s latest assessment of the Global Health Sector Strategies on HIV, Viral Hepatitis and STIs 2022–2030, released at the 26th International AIDS Conference in Rio de Janeiro, ahead of World Hepatitis Day. The picture is mixed: progress over the past decade is real, but it remains fragile unless countries keep investing and strengthen the systems that deliver care.

Gains on treatment, but the gap remains large

WHO said about 32 million people were receiving HIV treatment by the end of 2025. The report also says new HIV infections have fallen by 42 per cent and AIDS-related deaths by 57 per cent since 2010.

Even so, WHO said nine million people still lack access to treatment. Additionally, new infections are still rising in several regions, especially among key populations including sex workers, men who have sex with men, people who inject drugs, transgender persons and people in prisons.

WHO Director-General Dr Tedros Adhanom Ghebreyesus said the report shows “two stories” at once: what can happen when countries invest in health, communities and science, and how quickly progress can be undone by funding disruptions, humanitarian emergencies and persistent inequalities. He said the next five years will require “integrated, resilient health systems” that reach people “furthest behind.”

The report also says progress is uneven because access to prevention has been affected in some places. It expressed concern that reductions in international donor funding in 2025 disrupted HIV prevention programmes in several countries, limiting access to pre-exposure prophylaxis, or PrEP. While some governments have stepped in to sustain services, preliminary data show PrEP use has declined in a number of countries and communities.

WHO warns the pressure is broader than HIV

The assessment does not limit its concerns to HIV. WHO said viral hepatitis remains one of the world’s deadliest infectious diseases despite advances in prevention and treatment.

Annual hepatitis B infections have fallen by 32 per cent since 2015 and hepatitis C-related deaths have dropped by 12 per cent. But it also says hepatitis still caused an estimated 1.3 million deaths in 2024, and deaths linked to hepatitis B rose by 17 per cent over the past decade because diagnosis and treatment coverage remain low.

On sexually transmitted infections, WHO said progress is mixed. More countries have strengthened syphilis screening during pregnancy and expanded HPV vaccination, but STI cases continue to rise in several countries. The report also flags antimicrobial resistance, particularly in gonorrhoea, as an increasing public health challenge.

That wider picture matters because the Global Health Sector Strategies are meant to guide work across HIV, viral hepatitis and STIs together through 2030. WHO said the central risk is that gains in one area can be weakened if health systems, financing and service delivery are not strong enough to hold them.

WHO wants integrated care and stronger community reach

WHO identified integrated healthcare as one of the biggest opportunities to speed up progress. It urged countries to deliver HIV, hepatitis and STI services through primary healthcare, alongside tuberculosis, maternal and child health, sexual and reproductive health, and non-communicable disease programmes.

The agency also called for wider use of digital health technologies, stronger disease surveillance systems, broader access to multi-disease testing platforms and equitable access to new prevention tools, including twice-yearly injectable lenacapavir for HIV prevention and doxycycline post-exposure prophylaxis for bacterial STIs.

WHO further said stronger community leadership and greater use of community-generated data would improve accountability and help services reach people at highest risk. Dr Tereza Kasaeva, who heads WHO’s Department for HIV, Tuberculosis, Hepatitis and STIs, described the report as “a practical guide for action for the second half of this strategy period.” She said it shows where countries are succeeding, where progress is stalling and where investments can have the greatest impact.

The assessment comes shortly after the adoption of the 2026 United Nations High-Level Meeting Political Declaration on HIV/AIDS. WHO said the report provides fresh evidence to support efforts to meet the 2030 goal of ending HIV, viral hepatitis and STIs as public health threats through universal health coverage and stronger primary healthcare systems.
