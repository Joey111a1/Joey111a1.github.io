Benjamin Netanyahu has hailed Karim Khan’s removal as ICC prosecutor and said US Secretary of State Marco Rubio told him Washington was determined to act forcefully against the court, sharpening the fight over arrest warrants, misconduct claims and the court’s future.

Netanyahu added that his call with Rubio had brought an assurance that America remained committed to confronting the ICC.

The remarks came after ICC member states voted on Friday to dismiss Khan over misconduct claims at the United Nations headquarters in New York. Eighty-two of the court’s 125 states parties backed removal in a secret ballot, more than the 63 needed, while 13 voted against and 15 abstained. It was the first time a sitting ICC prosecutor has been removed.

The vote that removed Khan

The removal followed an 18-month disciplinary process over sexual misconduct claims that Khan has denied. In March, a panel of judges appointed by the ICC’s governing body had unanimously cleared him of wrongdoing, concluding that the UN investigators’ findings “do not establish misconduct or breach of duty under the relevant framework.” But the court’s Assembly of States Parties bureau later set that conclusion aside and moved ahead with the dismissal vote.

Khan’s case has been tied throughout to the court’s work on Palestine. In May 2024, he sought arrest warrants for Netanyahu and Gallant over alleged war crimes in Gaza. The warrants were later issued in November and remain in force; they can only be withdrawn by judges. Since then, the evidence says, Khan, his deputies and several judges have faced US sanctions, while Israel has repeatedly called for the warrants to be dropped.

Pushback over Gaza and the ICC

Francesca Albanese, the UN special rapporteur on the occupied Palestinian territories, said on Saturday that Khan’s removal could not be separated from the push to overturn Netanyahu’s warrant. Writing on X, she said the removal “lines up neatly with the fight to neutralise Netanyahu’s arrest warrant” and added that “coincidence is a luxury intl law can no longer afford.” She also wrote that if the ICC is paralysed, “lawyers and judges worldwide must do more.”

Albanese’s statement came in response to an Israeli media report that said foreign minister Gideon Saar “played an active role” in efforts to remove Khan. The report cited a senior Israeli official as saying Saar oversaw a dedicated task force and “employed intensive diplomatic efforts aimed at securing Khan’s removal from office.”

Israel’s public line has remained that the warrants against its officials should go. Saar called Khan’s removal “long-delayed” and urged the court to drop the warrants immediately. Netanyahu also argued on Sunday that Khan’s removal exposed “the truth” and what he described as corruption inside both the prosecutor’s office and the ICC itself.

What happens next

The ICC’s office of the prosecutor said after the vote that its work would continue without interruption. Elections for a new prosecutor are expected to follow, but the court’s governing body has not announced a timeline.

Netanyahu’s remarks also landed against a broader American campaign against the court. In separate comments this month, Rubio said Washington would “dismantle the ICC - brick by brick, if necessary,” and the US later welcomed Venezuela’s decision to withdraw from the court, calling it part of “American-led efforts to dismantle the corrupt and worthless ICC.”

The Problem With Shutting Down ICE in Blue States
global_south_and_exploitedpolitics_and_powereconomy_and_businesspublic_healthclimate_disastermaterial_harmmajor supplementregional expansion
What happened

The battle over Delaney Hall in Newark has become a test of whether shutting ICE jails in blue states actually slows deportations, or whether it simply pushes detainees farther away, into cheaper southern facilities and conditions opponents say are worse.

Delaney Hall reopened last year as the only sizable ICE detention center in the New York City metro area, with room for 1,000 detainees. Since May, protesters have rallied outside the privately run facility to denounce poor conditions inside the jail, which is operated by the GEO Group under a $1 billion, 15-year contract. New Jersey Governor Mikie Sherrill and other prominent Democrats have joined calls to shut it down, and they are suing the Trump administration to block ICE plans for a larger jail elsewhere in the state.

Trump officials, meanwhile, say the complaints about Delaney Hall are exaggerated and that the real aim of the protests is to drive ICE out. Their argument is that closing one facility in a blue state does not end enforcement; it just changes where people are held.

Delaney Hall sits at the center of a broader ICE fight

Delaney Hall matters because ICE has been threatening for months to intensify enforcement in New York, and the Newark jail has become central to that plan. The facility sits in a region where Democratic state and local governments are dominant and where constituents are furious at ICE, which has helped make it a symbol for the broader confrontation over President Trump’s mass-deportation campaign.

The local picture is already tight. Last January, an ICE official in New York City said he had fewer than 100 detention beds available, leased from a jail about 60 miles north of the city, and the shortage had contributed to overcrowding at the agency’s processing center at 26 Federal Plaza in Lower Manhattan. Detainees picked up in the New York City area and held at Delaney Hall remain close to their families, and one demand from recent protests was increased visitation hours.

Why blocking ICE jails used to work

In recent years, immigration activists have often found that blocking or limiting ICE jails in Democratic-run states and cities was effective because it constrained the government’s ability to arrest and hold large numbers of immigrants. It also raised costs by forcing ICE to relocate detainees. That strategy worked alongside so-called sanctuary policies adopted by state and local governments that try to limit or prohibit cooperation with federal immigration enforcement.

Those efforts also helped create a geographic imbalance in ICE infrastructure. There are relatively few detention centers near northern and coastal cities with large immigrant populations, while a disproportionate number of ICE jails are concentrated in Texas and Louisiana. John Gihon, a former ICE attorney in Florida and a board member of the American Immigration Lawyers Association, said that when facilities shut down in Democratic-run states, it can be “a small-time win” for activists, but it leaves detainees worse off. “ICE is going to find the bed space,” he said. “A lot of time, it’s going to be farther away from a blue state, and farther away from friendly federal courts, in a place with terrible conditions.”

Advocates do not dispute that there are reasons to push for better conditions. At least 52 detainees have died while detained by ICE since Trump returned to office, two and a half times the rate of fatalities from his first term, according to a Human Rights Watch report published last month. But some attorneys and immigrant advocates say shutting down a nearby jail can mean that families lose access and detainees are moved farther from legal help.

Why the tactic may be weaker now

The biggest change is money. Since Trump returned to office, Republicans have used the budget-reconciliation process to direct more than $100 billion in additional funding to ICE, about 10 times the agency’s annual budget. Trump officials say they need detention capacity to grow to 100,000 to reach their goal of 1 million deportations a year, and ICE currently has about 65,000 detainees in custody nationwide, still close to record levels.

That means opponents may be able to block new jails in blue cities and states, but the administration now has the resources to move detainees elsewhere. Tom Homan, Trump’s “border czar,” said in New Jersey last month that if protesters shut down Delaney Hall, ICE would simply move people out of state. He said: “We’ll fly to Arizona, detain them there, because we’re going to keep arresting people.” For him, the threat is not a deterrent but a transfer of burden — from a blue state to a southern one.

That is why some ICE critics say the old playbook may now be counterproductive. Claire Trickler-McNulty, a former ICE policy and planning official under Joe Biden who now advises advocacy organizations, said southern detention sites are lower cost and more convenient for deportation flights or bus transfers to Mexico. She argued that activists should focus on safe, humane detention and on reducing the number of people held at all. Even so, advocates remain committed to trying to stop new jails and limit ICE’s reach in their communities.

At the same time, Trump officials are still pursuing detention capacity in the Northeast. In Maryland, a federal judge has blocked ICE’s attempt to open the state’s first large immigration detention facility. In New Jersey, state officials and a Republican-led town council sued to stop ICE from converting a warehouse in Roxbury into a detention center, and the administration has said it is scaling back or reevaluating parts of its warehouse plan.

A new category appears on the casualty database

Over the weekend, the Defense Casualty Analysis System was updated to add more than 140 wounded service members and to introduce a new casualty category, “Overseas Operations,” for personnel killed and wounded “starting July 7.” In the updated database, the official toll for the earlier phase of the US-Iran war, known as Operation Epic Fury, stood at 14 service members killed and more than 400 wounded in action.

That change followed a period of confusion over the database figures. The Pentagon had previously reduced the totals from 18 deaths and 482 wounded that had been recorded just days earlier. One account of the updated system said the overall tally since the US launched its war against Iran on 28 February came to 18 US troops killed and 624 wounded once the revised Operation Epic Fury figures were combined with the new separate count.

The shift became visible when the Pentagon’s official online list of service members killed in the ongoing Iran war no longer included the four soldiers who died during renewed fighting over the weekend. Their names did appear in a Pentagon press release announcing the formal transfer of their remains back to the US on Wednesday.

Why the counting is being fought over

The dispute comes after the collapse of the ceasefire with Iran and the resumption of near-daily airstrikes beginning on 6 July. The Trump administration is seeking to treat the renewed military campaign as separate from Operation Epic Fury, the US military’s official name for the Iran conflict, and the new casualty category beginning on 7 July follows that interpretation.

That distinction matters because analysts and officials have questioned whether casualties from the renewed fighting should be counted separately from Operation Epic Fury, saying such a move would be unprecedented. The administration’s position is also tied to the War Powers Act of 1973, which requires the president to end military operations within 60 days unless Congress authorizes an extension.

The administration has argued that the April ceasefire with Iran paused the clock on 1 May for the conflict’s initial period. Lawmakers from both parties have challenged that timeline. In one public criticism, Republican House member Thomas Massie said the Pentagon was “pretending there have been two Iran wars separated by a brief cease-fire,” and argued that going more than 90 days without congressional authorization meant the defense secretary was “breaking the law.”

A separate account of the database update said the Defense Casualty Analysis System had removed the four fallen soldiers as well as dozens of wounded troops from its Iran war tally last week, then added a new “Overseas Operations” category listing the four Army deaths by name and 207 wounded. That account also said it was unclear whether all of the wounded were injured in the Iran conflict.

For now, the result is a casualty count that no longer keeps all of the fighting under one heading. The Pentagon’s online system is treating the renewed campaign as a separate category, while critics say that split changes how the war’s toll is seen and whether the administration can continue it under the War Powers timeline.
