The Elderly Population Is Increasing Year by Year; Diverse Urban–Rural Models Are Solving Care Difficulties
china_domesticpolitics_and_powermaterial_harmregional expansion
What happened

China’s older population is growing while conventional institutional-care beds are declining, prompting cities and rural communities to shift toward home services, community support, upgraded facilities and technology to meet different care needs.

The population aged 60 and above reached 297 million in 2023, 310 million in 2024 and 323 million in 2025, according to the figures cited. At the same time, beds in various elderly-care institutions and facilities decreased. The shift reflects demand as well as supply: about 90% of older people in most Chinese cities reportedly prefer ageing at home, while others choose community or institutional care.

In Beijing’s Dongcheng District, 219,000 residents are aged 60 or older. Although applying a 1% institutional-care standard would imply more than 2,000 beds, the district has about 900, and existing beds are not fully occupied. Instead, family-based elderly-care beds bring weekly home visits, basic care, psychological support and caregiver training. A nearby community station combines meals, activities, health records and medical coordination; by the end of 2025, China had built 495,000 such home-care beds.

Rural areas are also reorganizing care. More than 60% of township elder homes have reportedly become regional service centers. In Xuyi, 20 homes were consolidated into seven larger centers, improving facilities, medical-nursing services and occupancy.

Community initiatives add another layer, including village mutual-help centers, affordable meals, classes and digital safety monitoring. In mountainous Hubei, smart devices and village-level responders provide emergency assistance for vulnerable older residents.

Four years ago countries made a historic deal to save nature. Is it dead in the water?
politics_and_powermaterial_harmanchor
What happened

Four years after governments agreed to halt biodiversity loss, countries are meeting in Yerevan, Armenia, amid evidence that most targets are slipping, while pollution, funding cuts and weak domestic action continue to damage ecosystems and threaten livelihoods.

The immediate warning is visible along England’s River Wensum, where more than 3,000 pike, roach, carp and rudd were found dead in August. Investigators believe heavy rain flushed concentrated pollution into the river after heatwave conditions had already reduced oxygen levels. The 45-mile chalk river, near Norwich, is legally protected but remains in poor ecological health.

Chalk rivers emerge from underground aquifers and support freshwater shrimp, otters and stoneflies while supplying drinking water for millions of people. They are rare globally, yet only 11 of roughly 250 such rivers in Britain have formal protection. Eliot Lyne of Norfolk Wildlife Trust said the Wensum is far from the clear, free-flowing condition it should have.

The wider concern follows the biodiversity agreement reached at a United Nations summit in Montreal, Canada, at the end of 2022. Governments committed to protect 30% of land and seas, restore nearly a third of degraded ecosystems and reform at least $500bn in environmentally harmful subsidies. The evidence presented ahead of the Yerevan summit indicates that countries are likely to miss nearly all of the deal’s targets.

The United Kingdom is reportedly on course to meet only three of the agreement’s 23 targets. Conservation groups point to weakened biodiversity rules in England, reduced rainforest-protection funding, opposition to a complete bottom-trawling ban in marine protected areas and the absence of new sites of special scientific interest since 2023. The government says it has committed £7bn to nature recovery and climate resilience, strengthened powers against water pollution and restored international climate-aid commitments, including about £1.5bn expected for nature.

Funding is also under pressure internationally. Cuts to US government agencies have halted support for conservation projects, several European countries have reduced spending despite a biodiversity-finance pledge, and a private-sector fund intended to raise hundreds of millions annually has so far secured only $6,000.

Progress is not absent: some countries may approach the land-protection target, Indigenous territories have gained formal recognition, and new marine parks and rewilding projects have been established. But UN Secretary-General António Guterres has warned that commitments without delivery will not protect nature. The Yerevan meeting is intended to determine how governments respond to that widening gap.

Menopause: why doctors say fear of hormone therapy is greater than the real risk
politics_and_powermaterial_harmregional expansion
What happened

Fear of menopausal hormone therapy remains widespread among Brazilian women despite evidence cited by specialists that its risks are generally lower than once believed, potentially leaving symptomatic patients without an effective treatment for hot flashes, sleep problems and other consequences of menopause.

In February, the US Food and Drug Administration approved new labeling for six medicines used in menopausal hormone therapy, removing the agency’s most severe warning about breast cancer, cardiovascular disease and dementia. The change applies in the United States; Brazilian medicines do not carry an equivalent FDA warning, but doctors say the decision could influence perceptions in Brazil.

Menopausal hormone therapy replaces hormones—mainly estrogen, alone or combined with progesterone—that fall sharply during menopause. It can ease hot flashes, night sweats, insomnia, vaginal dryness and mood changes. A Brazilian study of 1,500 women published in 2022 found that only 22% of those who had sought medical help for menopausal symptoms were offered the therapy.

The concerns are not baseless, but the risks depend on the patient and the type of cancer involved. For women who still have a uterus, estrogen without progesterone can stimulate the uterine lining and increase cancer risk, which is why progesterone may be added. Hormone therapy is also contraindicated for people who have had certain hormone-sensitive breast cancers.

The article links persistent fear to the Women’s Health Initiative study, published in 2002 after involving 16,608 women. That study was widely interpreted as showing a substantial increase in breast-cancer risk. Later investigations identified problems in the study population, and subsequent research described the risk for most women as smaller than initially believed.

Specialists interviewed for the report stress that hormone therapy is not appropriate for everyone and is not risk-free. Where it is indicated, they say it should be prescribed with medical supervision and regular examinations, including mammograms when appropriate. Clinicians describe patients who delayed treatment because of cancer fears and later reported major relief, particularly from hot flashes, after starting therapy.

The treated mice slept more than two additional hours per day and spent longer in restorative NREM sleep. Their amyloid plaque levels did not fall, suggesting in these experiments that the inflammatory response to plaques, rather than plaque accumulation alone, contributed to the sleep disruption.

The researchers also observed that sleep problems appeared early and did not substantially worsen between six and 18 months, even though plaque burden more than doubled. This pattern led them to propose that microglial activation may produce an early ceiling in sleep disruption.

The findings remain based on mice, and the study does not establish that the same mechanism or sleep benefit occurs in people. Removing most microglia is not presented as a practical long-term treatment, since these cells have protective functions. The team is instead investigating whether microglial activity can be moderated without eliminating the cells, including through experimental work involving Metformin and Stiripentol.

The researchers also identified electrical activity patterns they believe could eventually help EEG distinguish Alzheimer’s-related changes from normal aging. Both that potential diagnostic use and any treatment based on calming microglia require further research in humans.

Why some people escape Alzheimer’s despite carrying a high-risk gene

What happened

Some people who carry the Alzheimer’s-associated gene variant APOE ε4 remain cognitively healthy into their 80s. A Columbia University study identifies a possible explanation: a rare mutation in the FN1 gene may protect blood vessels in the brain from damage linked to excess fibronectin, pointing to potential new prevention strategies.

APOE ε4 is the strongest common genetic risk factor for Alzheimer’s, but it does not make the disease inevitable. Researchers had previously found that some APOE ε4 carriers also have a protective FN1 mutation. The new study, published in *Nature Aging*, examined how that mutation might work.

Fibronectin helps support tissue structure, but the researchers found that APOE ε4 can promote its production and accumulation around brain blood vessels. In mice carrying human APOE ε4, fibronectin levels were almost twice those seen in mice with APOE ε3, and the increase coincided with a leakier blood-brain barrier.

That barrier normally regulates what passes from the bloodstream into brain tissue. Using human brain tissue and cerebrospinal fluid, genetic and epigenetic analyses, single-cell sequencing, spatial imaging, human stem-cell models, three-dimensional blood-vessel cultures, zebrafish and mice, the team investigated how excess fibronectin might undermine it.

The experiments indicated that fibronectin was not merely a byproduct of vascular damage. When researchers increased it in astrocytes—support cells involved in maintaining the blood-brain barrier—the barrier became more permeable. Reducing fibronectin improved its function. The study linked the damage to abnormal signaling involving integrins and focal adhesion kinase, which disrupted growth-factor communication among astrocytes and blood-vessel cells.

Human samples showed related disturbances: higher fibronectin was associated with inflammation in astrocytes, while analyses connected FN1 activity with blood-vessel disease and changes involving VEGFA. These findings, the researchers said, connect APOE ε4 to an early vascular process that may be modifiable.

Potential treatments could try to prevent fibronectin from accumulating, block the damaging signals it triggers or restore growth-factor signaling. But these approaches remain experimental. Because fibronectin also supports tissue structure and healing throughout the body, any therapy would need to target its harmful buildup or effects without disrupting its normal functions.

Scientists win 2026 Nobel Prize for controlling brain cells with light
science_generalpublic_healthscience anchorattributed source material (republished)
What happened

Karl Deisseroth, Peter Hegemann and Georg Nagel have received the 2026 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for work that enables researchers to manipulate selected nerve cells using light. The technique has reshaped brain research and is being explored for vision loss and neurological disease.

The prize recognizes the development of optogenetics, which links a discovery in the microscopic alga *Chlamydomonas* to a method for studying the living brain. Hegemann, at Humboldt University of Berlin, and Nagel, at the University of Würzburg, investigated how the organism detects illumination. Their work identified channelrhodopsin, a protein located in the alga’s cell membrane.

Channelrhodopsin contains a light-responsive molecule. Exposure to light changes the protein’s configuration, opening a route through which electrically charged particles enter the cell. That movement generates a signal involved in the alga’s response to its surroundings. The researchers recognized that nerve cells likewise depend on ion movement across their membranes to regulate activity.

Deisseroth, a psychiatrist and bioengineer at Stanford University in California, helped translate the finding into neuroscience. Scientists can place the channelrhodopsin gene in chosen neurons, typically with a modified virus designed not to cause disease. The resulting protein makes those cells react to light, while neighboring neurons lacking it do not respond in the same way.

In animal studies, an optical fibre can deliver light to a precise brain region, allowing researchers to activate or silence defined cell populations rapidly. This lets them test the roles of neurons in processes including memory, sleep, sensation and movement. A 2012 experiment examined fear memory in mice: cells active during an electric-shock conditioning procedure were marked, then stimulated later in a different cage. The mice displayed freezing behavior, whereas animals that had not undergone the conditioning did not.

The method has also reached early medical testing. Clinical trials for retinal degeneration are introducing light-sensitive proteins into retinal cells that remain after rods and cones have been lost, with the aim of recovering some visual function. Separate investigations are considering possible applications in Parkinson’s disease and epilepsy, but those uses remain under study. The award thus links fundamental research on how an alga responds to light with a tool for controlled investigation—and possible treatment—of disorders affecting the nervous system.
