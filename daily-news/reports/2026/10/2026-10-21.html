Singapore: Primary 1 registration changes could cut Phase 2A places at some popular schools by a fifth or more

What happened

Singapore’s revised Primary 1 registration rules are expected to intensify competition for alumni-linked places at popular schools, while expanding access for families without school connections. Based on Ministry of Education figures from the 2026 exercise, Phase 2A places could fall by at least 20 per cent at some schools.

Under the new framework, primary schools with at least 180 vacancies will reserve 60 places for Phase 2C, which is open to all children, instead of 40. Because the total number of places is limited, the increase could reduce allocations in Phase 2A, which gives priority to children with existing links to a school, including those whose parents attended it.

The effect could be sharpest at schools already facing heavy demand. Red Swastika School received 88 Phase 2A applications for 24 places in 2026. It had 40 Phase 2C places; raising that allocation to 60 would leave only four Phase 2A places for the same number of applicants, based on the ministry’s figures. Princess Elizabeth Primary School would see its Phase 2A places fall from 47 to 27, while Princess Elizabeth, Shuqun Primary and Pei Hwa Presbyterian Primary would each face reductions of more than 25 per cent.

Thirty-five schools were oversubscribed in Phase 2A this year. The projected squeeze could also push 12 schools that still had enough Phase 2A places in 2026 into oversubscription. Methodist Girls’ School (Primary), for example, had 93 applicants for 95 places; the revised allocations could leave 77 places. Fairfield Methodist School (Primary) could fall from 97 places for 91 applicants to 87 places.

The changes continue a broader effort to widen Phase 2C access. Twelve schools will divide their Phase 2C places into separate tracks for applicants living within 2km and those living farther away, without distance ranking within either track. Sociologists said the reforms could improve socioeconomic diversity, but also raised questions about whether alumni status and volunteer schemes should determine entry to primary schools. Other education experts said alumni participation can help preserve school culture, leaving policymakers to balance continuity against broader access.

What happened

Parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) enabled a research team to adjust large multimodal models for predicting human brain activity without updating most of their weights. In an October 2026 arXiv preprint, the authors report gains across nine backbones, improved computational efficiency, and a 261-model ensemble that led the public Algonauts 2025 leaderboard when the paper was prepared.

Adapting multimodal models to neural data

Brain-encoding models estimate neural responses to stimuli such as films, speech and text. A common design freezes a pretrained model and trains a separate readout to convert its representations into brain measurements. The researchers say this preserves knowledge learned during task pretraining but limits the model’s ability to reshape its internal representations for neural prediction.

Their alternative keeps the pretrained backbone largely intact while introducing a small trainable component. The main implementation uses low-rank adaptation, or LoRA, together with a readout. This approach is intended for settings in which neural data are expensive and limited, making both training a large model from the beginning and updating all of its parameters unattractive.

The evaluation uses the CNeuroMod/Algonauts 2025 Challenge, which contains whole-brain fMRI measurements collected while subjects watched audiovisual narrative material. The experiments involve four subjects and seven seasons of *Friends*, accompanied by aligned video, audio and transcripts. Training uses four subjects; Season 6 is used for validation, while Season 7 and films provide the in-distribution and out-of-distribution tests.

The authors also redesign feature extraction. Instead of repeatedly running overlapping input windows for successive fMRI measurements, their system uses the backbone’s causal structure to obtain representations for many timepoints in one pass. They report reducing extraction requirements from roughly 700 to two GPU-hours on A800 hardware, an approximately 350-fold decrease. A 60-second lead-in supplies additional context at the beginning of each window and largely restores the performance lost by the more economical processing scheme.

The method was tested on nine multimodal backbones ranging from 4.7 billion to 104 billion parameters. According to the preprint, LoRA improved results over frozen-backbone versions in every case. The authors report that the full-tuning setup used approximately 330 times more trainable parameters.

Other components also affected the results. In an ablation, closed-form re-estimation of the linear readout contributed 0.0061 to the score, while the LoRA adapter supplied the largest improvement over the frozen baseline. The adapter’s benefit disappeared in a control where the fMRI targets were shifted out of alignment with the stimulus, according to the paper.

The study examines whether adapting one multimodal backbone obscures the relationship between stimulus types and brain regions. The authors identify combining multiple datasets, modalities and subjects as future work.
