Besieged Yemeni city on verge of ‘unmanageable’ crisis, aid workers warn

What happened

The humanitarian crisis in Taiz, Yemen, is worsening as fighting and a blockade restrict water, fuel, food and medical supplies for more than 900,000 people. Aid workers warn that continued inaccessibility could bring deaths from hunger, illness and missile injuries across the city.

Iran-allied Houthi forces have largely encircled Taiz, cutting most supply routes. Heavy fighting this week around the old airport has damaged a densely populated residential area. The conflict intensified after forces loyal to Yemen’s internationally recognised government and the Saudi-led coalition launched airstrikes on the Houthi-held airport in Sana’a in July, ending a ceasefire that had largely halted fighting since 2022.

Fatma Jaffar, an Oxfam advocacy manager based in Yemen, said the city’s entrances were blocked, fuel had not entered for two or three weeks, and even residents with money could not reliably buy food. Hospitals were struggling to obtain medicines, while attempts to evacuate wounded people were blocked. Oxfam staff also reported sharply higher prices, including petrol rising from about $20 to $80 for a 20-litre container.

The World Food Programme said wheat-flour reserves were nearly exhausted by mid-October. Waste was accumulating because it could not be removed, and Oxfam warned that disease could follow if the situation continued; this was a forecast, not a reported outbreak.

The UN has called for humanitarian access. Donors have funded only 23% of this year’s Yemen relief programme, while Oxfam urged the UK to provide more aid.

‘I never saw my son, until they handed him over in a bag’: inside El Salvador’s brutal crime crackdown
global_south_and_exploitedpolitics_and_powermaterial_harmanchor
What happened

El Salvador’s long-running crime crackdown has left tens of thousands detained, including people whose families say they had no gang links. As authorities move toward mass trials, relatives and former prisoners describe deaths in custody, alleged torture, prolonged detention and a justice system unable to distinguish innocent people from the guilty.

In December 2021, soldiers and police entered Sandra Carolina Velasco Mena’s home at midnight searching for her 22-year-old son, Jefferson Alexánder Hernández Velasco. She says officers indicated they were trying to fill an arrest quota. Hernández was taken away and later transferred to Cecot, the maximum-security prison, despite a judge ordering his release, according to his mother. She has not seen or communicated with him since.

The arrest was part of President Nayib Bukele’s campaign against criminal groups. After an alleged government-gang truce appeared to collapse in March 2022, when gangs reportedly killed 87 people in three days, Bukele declared a nationwide state of emergency. An estimated 90,000 people have been arrested, while about 120,000 remain imprisoned—roughly 2% of the population. Human rights groups say tens of thousands had no proven gang connection.

The policy has brought sharply lower homicide rates and greater security for many Salvadorans after years of gang control. But it has also produced severe reported costs. Cristosal says at least 513 people died in state custody during the emergency; Amnesty International says most were not convicted of crimes. Former detainees describe beatings, inadequate food and water, denied medicine and degrading conditions. Víctor Barahona, a journalist detained for nearly a year, says he lost 80 pounds and developed health problems in prison.

Almost four years into the crackdown, authorities are trying hundreds of defendants simultaneously by video link. Human Rights Watch says defendants cannot properly consult lawyers, defence teams face obstacles to calling witnesses, and some witnesses and lawyers have been threatened. It also found police pressure to meet arrest targets, which allegedly encouraged accusations based on weak or fabricated evidence, particularly against people in poor neighbourhoods.

Bukele acknowledged in 2024 that 8,000 detainees had been innocent and released, while rejecting human rights groups’ higher estimates of wrongful arrests. The emergency powers, initially presented as temporary, remain in force.

What happened

What happened

China’s National Medical Products Administration approved a domestically developed active Class III Auditory Brainstem Implant for market on the 20th. Developed by Professor Wu Hao and colleagues at the Ninth People’s Hospital affiliated with Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine, the device is intended for children with severe inner-ear malformations or an absent cochlear nerve, conditions in which cochlear implants cannot transmit signals to the brain. The development could provide an alternative for children who otherwise attend schools for deaf students.

The implant places an electrode array on the brainstem’s cochlear nucleus, bypassing the cochlea and auditory nerve to stimulate central auditory neurons. The team began work in 2019, developing a 16-electrode array, a high-rate stimulation chip and a Chinese four-tone coding strategy; it also obtained five invention patents. In 2024, the team completed the implantation surgeries and follow-up for its clinical trial. It reported a 96.67% effective rate 12 months after activation, describing the result as a successful clinical trial of domestically produced auditory-brainstem technology. The market approval marks the transition from development and clinical testing to an approved intervention.

The new research combines improved seismic imaging, microscopic analysis of rock cuttings from an oil well near the crater, and computer simulations. The researchers report finding shocked quartz and feldspar at the same depth as the crater floor. They say these minerals contain pressure-related structures that ordinary geological processes generally cannot produce, providing the physical evidence they regard as decisive.

Their reconstruction indicates that an asteroid about 160 meters across approached from the west at a shallow angle and struck the seabed 43–46 million years ago. The impact is modeled as having thrown rock and seawater about 1.5 kilometers upward before the material fell back into the North Sea, generating a tsunami more than 100 meters high.

The wave height is a simulation-based estimate, not a direct observation. The findings, reported as published in *Nature Communications*, place Silverpit among the relatively small number of confirmed impact craters on Earth. Its buried and comparatively well-preserved structure offers researchers an opportunity to examine how a high-speed ocean impact fractured and reshaped the seabed—evidence that is usually erased by erosion, sedimentation or tectonic activity.

A train was delayed by a solar storm. But something didn't add up.
science_generalclimate_disasterscience anchorinstitutional_press_release
What happened

A historical investigation has corrected the date of a train delay in Exeter, England, once described as an 1841 disruption caused by solar activity. The event occurred in 1848, researchers say, refining the early record of space weather’s effects on electrical technology and its continuing risks to infrastructure.

The incident took place during the 1840s, as telegraph networks and railway systems were expanding. A 1871 account in *Nature* described a magnetic disturbance on October 18, 1841, that interfered with railway signals and delayed a scheduled 10:05 p.m. train by 16 minutes. If accurate, it would have been the earliest known example of space weather disrupting technology.

Researchers led by Lancaster University found that the account’s date could not be right: the Exeter-to-Starcross railway line mentioned in it did not open until 1846. The international team, which included scientists from RMIT University, the British Geological Survey, Natural Resources Canada, Baylor University and RAL Space, re-examined the historical record.

They compared railway timetables and newspaper coverage with records of solar activity, auroras and Earth’s magnetic field. Those sources pointed to October 18, 1848—seven years later—as the date of the Exeter disruption. The finding resolves the mismatch between the reported event and the railway’s construction timeline.

An early warning about technological vulnerability

The corrected incident remains one of the earliest credible cases of solar activity affecting technology, but it is no longer considered the first. The researchers identify an earlier documented episode of telegraph interference on Britain’s Midland Railway in March 1847.

Space weather occurs when activity on the Sun disturbs Earth’s magnetic field. Such disturbances can generate unwanted electrical currents and interfere with systems connected to electrical or satellite networks. The study links the Exeter delay to a period when emerging communications technology first began exposing everyday infrastructure to these effects.

The researchers say the historical evidence also provides perspective on present-day risks. Modern systems are more advanced, but power grids, railway signalling, satellite navigation and communications remain susceptible to strong solar disturbances. The investigation’s authors argue that combining archival records with scientific observations can help reconstruct past events and improve understanding of hazards that may affect increasingly interconnected infrastructure.

Astronauts’ hearts just passed a surprising test for Mars
science_generalpublic_healthscience anchorinstitutional_press_release
What happened

A study of 13 astronauts who spent about six months aboard the International Space Station found that their hearts adapted to prolonged weightlessness and showed no major strain during a ground-based simulation of Martian gravity, offering evidence relevant to future crewed Mars missions.

Researchers from Northwestern Medicine, UT Southwestern and UT Houston tracked the astronauts’ hearts before, during and after their missions using echocardiograms—ultrasound scans that measure cardiac structure and performance. During the first two weeks in space, cardiac filling and stroke volume declined, meaning the hearts temporarily filled with and pumped less blood per beat. Those measures later improved toward preflight levels.

The astronauts exercised for up to two hours daily under NASA’s Exercise Countermeasures program, including endurance and resistance training. The researchers say this demanding routine helped preserve cardiovascular function as the body adapted to microgravity, where the heart and muscles no longer work against Earth’s full gravitational pull.

After the missions, the team tilted each astronaut upward by 22 degrees from a lying position to shift blood toward the lower body and approximate some circulatory effects of Martian gravity. Mars has about 38% of Earth’s surface gravity. In the test, the astronauts’ hearts performed as well as they had while standing on Earth before launch, with no significant cardiovascular strain reported.

The findings, published in *Circulation*, address a key concern for a Mars journey: whether astronauts can transition from months of weightlessness to a weaker gravitational environment. The study is an exploratory observational investigation involving a small group, and its gravity test was a simulation rather than an actual Mars landing. Still, senior author James Thomas said the results provide reason to think astronauts could reach Mars and work on its surface.

NASA’s Perseverance Mars rover just shattered the all-time off-world driving record
science_generalscience_cs_aiscience anchorattributed source material (republished)
What happened

NASA’s Perseverance rover has traveled farther on another world than any vehicle before it, reaching 28.10 miles on Mars while heading toward ancient terrain that could preserve evidence about the planet’s past environments and potential habitability.

On October 11, Perseverance’s odometer reached 28.10 miles (45.22 kilometers), exceeding the 28.06-mile (45.16-kilometer) record set by NASA’s Opportunity rover. Opportunity operated on Mars from 2004 to 2018 and took nearly 15 years to reach its total; Perseverance covered the distance in five and a half years, on its 2,006th Martian day, or sol.

The rover set the record while traveling toward Idubi through Lac de Charmes, an area beyond the western rim of Jezero Crater. NASA says the region may contain some of the oldest terrain examined in the solar system. At Idubi, scientists are looking for signs of ancient hydrothermal systems—places where heated water circulated through rock and may have created conditions suitable for life.

Perseverance’s pace has been aided by AutoNav, its autonomous driving system. The rover photographs its surroundings, builds three-dimensional maps of obstacles and selects routes between destinations chosen by mission controllers. NASA says AutoNav has evaluated more than 90% of Perseverance’s driving distance, compared with about 5% for the autonomous systems used by Opportunity and Spirit. The rover can travel as far as 1,351 feet (412 meters) in one Martian day.

The mileage milestone supports a broader scientific goal: reaching more geological formations and sampling different environments. Perseverance has collected 29 rock and regolith samples from the crater floor, an ancient river deposit and the crater rim. NASA has approved an extension running approximately through the end of 2028, during which the rover is expected to continue along the rim and later head toward the 100-meter-wide Rainbow Falls formation.
