No easy gig: China's push to better protect platform workers faces hurdles
china_domesticpolitics_and_powermaterial_harmregional expansion
What happened

China has proposed new labor rules for platform workers after evidence from Shenzhen highlighted the trade-off between flexible work and basic security. The draft targets pay, rest periods and automated management for a workforce Beijing estimates at 84 million, but its final effect will depend on enforcement and worker income needs.

Chinese authorities issued the draft on Oct 8 for ride-hailing drivers, delivery riders and others in “new forms of employment.” It would require platforms to provide a break after four hours of work, pay workers at least monthly, increase compensation for statutory-holiday shifts and include costs such as vehicle use when determining pay. Platforms would also need a person to review decisions to suspend accounts or withhold orders.

For workers, however, rest can mean lost earnings. Chen, a 43-year-old Didi driver in Shenzhen, generally works 12 hours or more daily and takes home about 10,000 yuan (US$1,400) a month after expenses, while paying for her own car, charging and insurance. She took time off after damaging the vehicle, but doing so reduced her income.

Delivery riders face different arrangements. Yang Yuhao, working through a Meituan contractor, said the station determines when he can take leave and that delivery delays or customer complaints can reduce his earnings. Wang, a crowdsourced Meituan rider, chooses his own hours but earns about 5,000 to 6,000 yuan monthly and receives no pension or medical contributions through the work. The company’s rider report said this category represented 71.5 per cent of its monthly active riders in 2024.

The draft seeks to cover people without conventional employment contracts, including through written agreements. Employment-law lecturer Ou Lin said that would not automatically provide the social-insurance rights available to regular employees.

Public consultation runs until Nov 8, and no start date has been announced. Analysts cited unclear penalties, incomplete work records and uncertainty over whether platforms or contractors should bear responsibility. The draft may change before adoption, leaving the central question unresolved: how to protect workers without making time away from work financially impossible.

The preprint presents this as evidence that matching or recognizing images is not enough: the harder requirement is to maintain a useful three-dimensional representation while selecting actions and incorporating visual feedback.

The benchmark also separates image-space progress from pose-space progress. A model may improve the visible silhouette while moving farther from the object’s underlying target orientation. At matched states, GPT-5.6-Sol improved silhouette overlap while worsening pose alignment in 19.4% of cases, compared with 10.9% for humans. The authors use pose error as a diagnostic rather than the task objective, because symmetries and projection overlap can make multiple 3D poses acceptable.

Additional trajectory statistics reported in the paper show that humans ended with a mean symmetry-adjusted pose error of 4.44 degrees, compared with 85.25 degrees for GPT-5.6-Sol. Human actions reduced pose error in 77.88% of executed steps, while the corresponding figure for GPT-5.6-Sol was 52.16%. These measurements come from the benchmark’s hidden ground-truth geometry and are not provided to the evaluated systems.

Interaction history affects fine control

RoTA-Bench is designed to test whether an agent can use the sequence of earlier observations and actions, not merely the current image. The reported ablation removes that interaction history. Under this condition, 96.8% of the model’s actions use the coarsest available 30-degree rotation, and observed task success falls sharply.

The authors argue that this result points to a specific control problem. A solver needs to know not only which direction appears promising, but also how far it has already rotated and whether a small correction is preferable to a large move. The benchmark therefore treats visual feedback and action history as part of the reasoning task rather than as incidental interface details.

The construction also includes automated reference-path searches. A pose-guided beam search and a breadth-first search restricted to 30-degree moves produced shorter verified paths than the original reversible initialization sequence for 585 of the 674 tasks, and paths of equal length for the remaining 89. These paths are used to organize the dataset and test selection, not supplied to the evaluated models or participants.

What the benchmark isolates

The task deliberately restricts the environment to rotation. It does not include translation, grasping, contact friction or other physical dynamics, allowing the authors to focus on whether a model can infer orientation from contours, silhouettes and depth cues. The use of abstract icon-derived objects is intended to reduce reliance on familiar object categories or memorized 3D shapes.

That design makes RoTA-Bench a targeted evaluation rather than a complete test of embodied intelligence. Its reported conclusions concern interactive projection alignment with synthetic, rotationally controlled objects. The work is presented as an arXiv preprint and has not been peer reviewed. Its central claim is that current vision-language models remain substantially weaker than people at converting visual evidence into precise three-dimensional action, even when the action space and success condition are explicitly defined.

The researchers describe the state as an exotic critical phase of quantum matter, not merely a random collection of energetic particles.
