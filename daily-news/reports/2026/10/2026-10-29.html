What happened

Multiscale Policy Optimization (MPO), proposed for long-horizon language-model agents, reorganizes reinforcement learning around complete interactions such as searches and tool calls while retaining token-level learning. The preprint reports higher benchmark scores than several established baselines on 4B and 30B-A3B models.

The method addresses a mismatch in agent training. An agent acts through whole assistant turns—such as a search query, tool call or final response—but generates each turn one token at a time. A terminal reward must therefore assign credit both across interactions and within each generated response. The researchers report that critic values usually change more between turns than within them: across 2,048 trajectories and 16,323 turns from one search-agent run, differences between turn averages represented a median 89.5% of token-weighted value variance, and between-turn variation was larger in 93.8% of trajectories.

MPO combines two components. Multiscale Advantage Estimation first propagates reward information across turns, then allocates more detailed credit among tokens while keeping each turn’s average signal unchanged. Its policy update uses the probability ratio for the complete turn, while preserving token-specific gradients. The approach is intended to match the way agents interact with environments without discarding information about individual tokens.

Experiments used Qwen3-4B-Thinking and Qwen3-30B-A3B-Instruct in search, Claw-Eval and Automation Bench settings. Against the initial models and GRPO, GSPO, PPO, VAPO and SAO, MPO achieved the highest reported result on all nine listed metrics at both model sizes. For example, on the 4B model it reached 48.08% BrowseComp-Plus pass@1, 39.47% GAIA pass@1 and 13.33% Automation Bench pass@1; the corresponding 30B-A3B figures were 50.32%, 43.42% and 15.00%. The reported evaluation selected the best checkpoint from each run and averaged five offline evaluations.

An ablation replacing complete-turn importance ratios with token-level ratios reduced every reported score, while retaining multiscale credit still outperformed PPO on the six search metrics. Tests of propagation and within-turn settings also favored intermediate values rather than the longest or shortest tested credit traces.

The work is an arXiv preprint dated 26 October 2026, so its findings have not yet undergone peer review. The evidence also describes single training runs for each model size in the training-dynamics comparisons and one run for each ablation setting, limiting how broadly those comparisons can establish robustness.

A streaming model may learn that an object is on the left or right without retaining the metric displacement behind that judgment. The authors call this specialization “interface lock-in”: emphasizing one readout can improve that answer type while transfer to others remains weak or declines.

ChronoReplay targets the problem through two training choices. The training combines 33,180 single-turn preservation examples with 6,381 ordered trajectory episodes containing 45,006 supervised turns. The two families are balanced so that their different sizes do not determine how strongly they influence the shared model. The approach does not add a map, memory component or auxiliary state loss.

Capacity, not distortion

The study compares several ways of assigning discrete codes to visual features, including residual vector quantization (RVQ) and product quantization (PQ). Increasing the number of codebooks generally improved multimodal performance and helped with tasks requiring recognition of small visual details, including text in images.

That improvement did not continue indefinitely with existing methods. In RVQ, later codebooks encode progressively weaker residual signals. PQ instead divides the feature into smaller channel groups as more codebooks are added. Consequently, the formal bit count can rise while the amount of new task-relevant information contributed by each codebook falls. The researchers distinguish this nominal capacity from the effective capacity available for semantic understanding.

Their experiments found that larger budgets could bring discrete representations closer to continuous ones, including on optical character recognition and other fine-grained visual tasks. However, RVQ and PQ eventually showed diminishing returns, creating a scaling bottleneck for systems that rely on discrete visual tokens.

A different quantization structure

The researchers propose Factorized Vector Quantization (FVQ) as one response to that bottleneck. FVQ maps each visual feature into several complementary views of the same fixed dimension. Separate codebooks quantize those views in parallel, rather than assigning them progressively smaller residuals or splitting the original feature into narrower channel sections.

The projections are organized as a tight frame. Before quantization, combining the projected views reconstructs the original feature exactly; the quantization step, rather than the factorization itself, introduces the remaining distortion. This arrangement gives each codebook access to information derived from the full feature and is intended to prevent newly added codebooks from becoming structurally less informative.

For its main model, the study uses a 16-codebook FVQ tokenizer with Qwen3 ViT features and pairs it with Qwen3-8B. The training pipeline includes visual-tokenizer reconstruction, alignment of visual token embeddings with the language model, pre-training and supervised fine-tuning. The reported main configuration uses 100 billion tokens for quantizer training, 10 billion for alignment, 60 billion for pre-training and 30 billion for supervised fine-tuning. The data mixtures cover STEM material, OCR, interleaved image-text sequences, image captioning and text-only content.

The authors report that the model led the compared discrete-representation systems, with relative gains of 22.8% over UniAR and 59.2% over Emu3-Chat on a nine-metric average.

The paper also reports competitive results against continuous-representation models. Its system matched Qwen3-VL-8B on MathVista, achieved a higher RealWorldQA score in the cited comparison and surpassed the continuous models on both CharXiv tasks. The preprint concludes that discrete visual tokens could provide a scalable interface for native multimodal foundation models, but its results tie that possibility to maintaining informative additions as the token budget expands.
