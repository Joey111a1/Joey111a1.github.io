Galloway hails SA’s ‘second age of greatness’

What happened

George Galloway has praised South Africa’s genocide case against Israel as a new chapter in the country’s history, saying its legal intervention for Palestinians has served humanity and projected South Africa’s anti-apartheid legacy onto the international response to the destruction in Gaza.

Speaking in Cape Town on Wednesday, the former British MP said South Africa’s decision to bring Israel before the International Court of Justice (ICJ) represented the country’s “second age of greatness”. He described the first as its liberation from apartheid and the second as its role in seeking to “liberate Palestine”.

Galloway was speaking alongside British journalist and author Yvonne Ridley at the Bo-Kaap Cultural Hub during their People Power Tour, which focuses on Palestine and wider human-rights issues. The media breakfast addressed South Africa’s international role, the destruction in Gaza, colonialism and the responsibilities of journalists.

He credited South Africa’s government and legal team with pursuing the case, arguing that it had attracted support well beyond the country’s borders. While acknowledging that South Africans would assess their government on its domestic record, he urged them not to underestimate the significance of the intervention.

“It’s your job now to judge your government,” Galloway said. “But please don’t underestimate the service you did to humanity by bringing that genocide case before the ICJ.”

A legal case with political consequences

South Africa brought the case under the Genocide Convention. The ICJ has issued three binding provisional-measures orders aimed at protecting Palestinians in Gaza, but it has not delivered a final judgment on whether Israel has committed genocide. Israel denies committing genocide.

Galloway said he was sceptical about what the court would ultimately deliver. Asked what countries could do while the proceedings continued, he said he was “not expecting anything very much from the ICJ”, describing the case as “political theatre”. He argued, however, that such theatre could be powerful because it had focused international attention on Palestine and on the responsibilities of other governments.

The question of whether the evidence establishes the specific intent required under the Genocide Convention remains before the court. Galloway’s own argument rests on an intention he attributes to Israel’s occupation: that Palestinians are to be removed from their land. He contrasted this with apartheid South Africa, where he said the economy depended on Black people as workers and consumers.

“In Palestine,” he argued, “the objective is to possess the country while excluding its people.” He linked the destruction in Gaza to the displacement of Palestinians and to generations living in refugee camps or exile, citing a Palestinian family from Jaffa whom he met during a speaking tour in Yellowknife, Canada.

South Africa also submitted a further dossier to the ICJ in August, alleging that Israel had failed to comply with the court’s provisional orders. The international relations department said the submission reaffirmed the country’s commitment to international law and multilateral institutions.

Galloway placed the court case within a wider struggle over whether countries in the Global South can hold powerful states and their allies accountable. He portrayed American pressure on South Africa as punishment for its position on Palestine, arguing that Washington’s support for Israel made South Africa’s intervention a challenge to both countries.

Language, journalism and continued pressure

Ridley focused on the role of journalism in shaping public understanding of the conflict. She criticised descriptions of Palestinians “losing” their children or lives, saying such language obscured the violence responsible for their deaths.

“Words are the tools of our trade, and we have to use them correctly,” she said. Ridley argued that giving a voice to oppressed people was central to journalism and rejected an interpretation of impartiality that treated oppression and those subjected to it as morally equivalent.

“Impartiality is a myth in journalism,” she said. “All there needs to be in journalism is some context and balance, and we’re not getting it.”

She said reporting could mobilise people, while misleading language could direct them towards the wrong target. Both speakers called for continued public pressure while the legal proceedings continued. Ridley drew on the history of international solidarity against apartheid, telling campaigners to persist even when their efforts appeared insufficient.

Galloway said governments sympathetic to Palestine had a particular duty to use the powers available to them. He also warned against applying accusations of terrorism and antisemitism so broadly that they lost their meaning, while stressing that hatred of Jews was real and should not be conflated automatically with support for Palestinian rights.

He predicted that support for Palestinian freedom would eventually become as widely accepted as opposition to apartheid, saying future generations might claim they had always supported the cause.

Tennessee execution of Christa Pike can go ahead, US supreme court rules

What happened

The US Supreme Court has cleared the way for Tennessee to execute Christa Pike, overturning a last-minute stay that had paused her lethal-injection execution while a federal appeals court considered whether her claims of childhood sexual abuse were properly addressed at sentencing.

Pike, 50, has been on death row since she was convicted and sentenced to death in 1996 for the murder of Colleen Slemmer. She was initially scheduled to be executed on Wednesday morning, but judges on the Sixth US Circuit Court of Appeals halted the process earlier that day.

The Supreme Court later vacated that stay in an order signed by Justice Brett Kavanaugh. The order did not explain why the stay was lifted. The Guardian reported that a Tennessee Department of Correction spokesperson said the execution would proceed on Wednesday night, while BBC News said it was not immediately clear when it would take place. The state’s execution order was due to expire at midnight; if it did, the Tennessee Supreme Court would need to set a new date.

A last-minute legal challenge

The Sixth Circuit had paused the execution to allow more time to consider Pike’s argument that allegations of childhood sexual abuse had not been adequately considered during her sentencing. The court issued what was described as a short stay and did not specify how long its review would take.

Pike’s lawyers said Tennessee had for years argued in court that she had lied about being sexually abused as a child. At a more recent hearing concerning her execution method, however, the state said it did not dispute that she had suffered sexual abuse and rape. Her legal team argued that earlier federal review of her case may have been affected if it relied on Tennessee’s former position.

Tennessee’s attorney general immediately asked the Supreme Court to overturn the stay. The attorney general described the delay as rewarding what he called “abusive delay tactics” by Pike’s lawyers, according to the Associated Press account cited by The Guardian.

The Supreme Court had earlier refused to hear a separate appeal from Pike. Tennessee Governor Bill Lee also rejected her clemency request during the week, saying he was upholding the state’s sentence after reviewing the case.

The dissent over timing

The court’s three liberal justices—Sonia Sotomayor, Elena Kagan and Ketanji Brown Jackson—dissented from the order. Sotomayor wrote that the decision allowed Tennessee to execute Pike before her remaining legal challenges to her conviction could be properly considered.

“Tennessee’s desire to expedite Pike’s execution by a few days or even weeks cannot outweigh her ‘fundamental interest in [her] own life,’” Sotomayor wrote.

The disagreement focused on whether the execution should proceed before the appeals court completed its consideration of Pike’s claims. The Supreme Court’s order did not resolve those underlying challenges; it vacated the stay that had temporarily prevented the execution from going ahead.

The case and its significance in Tennessee

Pike was 18 when she and her then-boyfriend, Tadaryl Shipp, killed Slemmer, a 19-year-old they had met at the Knoxville Job Corps. Prosecutors said the pair lured Slemmer into a wooded area near the University of Tennessee on 12 January 1995. Pike has never denied involvement in the killing.

Shipp was convicted of first-degree murder in 1997 and received a life sentence with the possibility of parole. Pike was later diagnosed with bipolar disorder and post-traumatic stress disorder. In her clemency petition, she described herself as a mentally ill 18-year-old and said she had come to understand the consequences of taking Slemmer’s life.

If carried out, Pike’s execution would be Tennessee’s first execution of a woman in more than 200 years. Slemmer’s mother, May Martinez, said she continued to think about her daughter’s suffering and was waiting for Pike’s execution.

Israeli forces overwhelmed by violent settlers in ‘terrorist attack’ on Palestinian family

What happened

More than 100 Israeli settlers attacked a Palestinian family returning to homes in Jalud that had been seized by settlers, overwhelming a small Israeli security escort, setting houses on fire and injuring three security personnel. The assault triggered unusually sharp Israeli condemnation and renewed warnings about worsening settler violence in the occupied West Bank.

The Tubasi family had been forced from its homes earlier in the summer after what the account describes as a weeks-long siege by settler militants. Last week, an Israeli high court ruling ordered the state to evict the settlers and secure the family’s return. Late on Monday, the military told the family that roughly 15 military police officers would escort them home immediately.

When the family arrived early on Tuesday, settlers attacked one of the houses and forced the family onto the roof. The military then evacuated them in armoured vehicles. The crowd blocked roads, threw stones at Israeli security forces and set the homes on fire. Three security officials were injured.

The escort was quickly outnumbered. A security source described the operation as reckless because it took place during Sukkot, when settler outposts were reportedly crowded and the number of violent extremists tends to rise. The military had not informed national police or the Shin Bet domestic intelligence service, leaving the force without additional security support.

The damage and the response

The two homes were left severely damaged, according to Wael Tubasi. He said doors, tiles, stairs, bathrooms and windows had been destroyed. The family’s attempted return therefore ended with its evacuation and the burning and destruction of the properties it had been ordered to regain.

The attack’s immediate political impact was intensified by the fact that Israeli security personnel were themselves targeted. President Isaac Herzog called the events in Jalud a “moral stain”. Gadi Eisenkot, a former military chief and a leading challenger to Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu in the upcoming elections, called for action against what he described as a “campaign of terror and violence”.

A military spokesperson said two suspects had been detained and said violence against soldiers crossed a “clear red line”. Netanyahu condemned the “violent riots” and called for the attackers to be prosecuted, but defended the settler movement, saying the “handful of rioters” did not represent what he called the law-abiding settler public in Judea and Samaria, Israel’s term for the occupied West Bank.

The severity of the incident also prompted criticism that the state had lost control. Avi Ashkenazi, writing in Maariv, described it as a “terrorist attack” and a failure of Israel’s defence establishment, saying extreme rightwing activists had become the real sovereign in the West Bank. The International Crisis Group analyst Mairav Zonszein said the political concern was less about Palestinian safety than about Israel appearing weak and the military losing control.

A wider crisis for Palestinian civilians

The International Committee of the Red Cross said that attacks, settlement expansion and severe restrictions on movement had made everyday life “untenable” for Palestinians. Julien Lerisson, head of the ICRC delegation for Israel and Palestine, called for the violence to end and said international humanitarian law protecting private and public property and access to essential services must be respected. The ICRC also said Israel had a responsibility under international law to protect Palestinian civilians.

The Guardian’s account places the Jalud assault within a broader escalation in the occupied West Bank, where it says settler militants backed by cabinet ministers have accelerated a campaign described as ethnic cleansing. According to the Israeli rights group B’Tselem, more than 60 Palestinian communities have been wiped off the map since October 2023 and 18 others have been partly displaced, forcing nearly 5,000 people from their homes, almost half of them children.

The account says deadlier settler attacks, including attacks on Palestinian children, have received less attention inside Israel. It also describes growing international pressure: the Netherlands has introduced a ban on settlement goods, the UK has announced plans for broader trade and financing controls, and a similar bill was due to be introduced in the US legislature. A US Senate bill seeking a human-rights report on Israel’s occupation of the West Bank was narrowly defeated.

The Israeli government has rejected the idea that the settler movement as a whole is represented by violent attackers, while the military says such incidents must not become normal. The Jalud assault nevertheless exposed a direct confrontation in which settlers overpowered the forces assigned to enforce a court-ordered return, leaving the Tubasi family displaced again and their homes badly damaged.

What happened

Colombia’s Interior Ministry has published a draft decree proposing changes to the protocol governing authorities’ conduct during protests. The proposal would amend Decree 003 of 2021 and remain open for public comment until October 4. It retains the presumption that demonstrations are peaceful and says protective measures should target only people engaging in violence, while allowing police to intervene when firearms, bladed weapons, explosives or incendiary devices are present, or when there are detentions, assaults or infrastructure destruction. Police would be able to act against those involved while continuing dialogue with peaceful demonstrators, without first exhausting dialogue, mediation and facilitation with people accused of violent acts.

The draft also proposes more detailed advance notifications covering routes, schedules and estimated attendance; designated areas where demonstrations would receive priority or face possible restrictions, particularly near commercial zones or critical infrastructure; and increased police presence under proportionality and gradualism criteria. Gabriel Rondón, a former deputy interior minister under Gustavo Petro, said the proposal contradicts Supreme Court rulings and 2021 recommendations from the Inter-American Commission on Human Rights. He also criticized the public-comment period as insufficient for a measure of this significance. The proposal comes amid mobilization by social and trade-union organizations, while demonstrations in Bogotá have caused reported mobility problems along Carrera 7 and routes toward Calle 26.

In Brazil’s capital, residents grow their own heat-fighting solutions
global_south_and_exploitedmaterial_harmpolitics_and_powerregional expansion
What happened

Brasília’s unequal distribution of trees and parks is leaving residents of peripheral Sol Nascente exposed to intense heat, respiratory problems and difficult living conditions, while residents are creating their own shade and green spaces. Their efforts range from planting trees beside homes to transforming rubbish-strewn land into an agroforest.

Brasília was designed as a green, “park city.” Its central Plano Piloto has tree-lined avenues and parks, but the city’s planned landscape did not extend evenly to its outskirts. In Sol Nascente, about 30 kilometres from the central area, concrete, tarmac and tightly packed houses dominate the streets, with little room for vegetation.

A University of Brasília study found that the Plano Piloto averages 138 square metres of green space per resident. In some peripheral areas, the figure is up to 23 times smaller. Those areas have limited vegetation and greater distances to parks, the study found.

Sol Nascente emerged in the 1990s on illegally occupied rural land and now has more than 70,000 residents across 9 square kilometres. Its population density is almost 16 times higher than the Federal District average. The area’s sparse vegetation and extensive tarmac create conditions for an urban heat island, in which built surfaces absorb and retain heat more effectively than vegetation.

Heat, health and unequal access to nature

The heat is not only uncomfortable. Resident Francisco Silva says there are times when an entire street is exposed to the sun and air does not circulate. The 34-year-old, who has rhinitis and bronchitis, says dry conditions cause nosebleeds and disrupt his sleep. Closing the window makes it difficult to breathe; opening it allows dry wind and dust inside. During the dry season, he washes his yard daily, places wet towels around the house and wears a mask.

Silva is renovating his home to make room for grass and young trees. He also planted the only tree on his street, an ipê, on the pavement.

Larissa Brenda Cordeiro, chair of the Daughters of the Earth Institute, a socio-environmental justice organisation in Sol Nascente, says afternoon heat can make it impossible to hold meetings or work in the group’s office. She also experiences muscle and joint pain linked to the conditions, alongside asthma and fibromyalgia.

Patricia Fabian, a heat expert at Boston University, says people living in urban heat islands face greater exposure to the harmful effects of high temperatures, including cardiovascular disease, cognitive problems, respiratory symptoms and death. During the dry season, temperatures can reach 37.8°C in parts of central Brasília, while Ceilândia, which included Sol Nascente until 2019, reaches 46.6°C.

Valdir Steinke, who authored the University of Brasília study, says the Federal District reflects a wider national pattern: green spaces are concentrated in affluent areas, while the outskirts have almost none. Average household income in the Plano Piloto is 12 times higher than in Sol Nascente. Steinke describes this as a pattern of socioeconomic segregation in which urban planning on the outskirts ignores nature.

Residents turn neglected land into green space

As climate change drives higher temperatures, residents are responding where formal planning has not provided enough shade or vegetation. One example is Floresta da Nasaré, an agroforest on the border between Sol Nascente and Ceilândia.

In 2009, Nasaré Francisca da Silva cleared a football-pitch-sized piece of public land opposite her home. The site had been used as a rubbish tip, where people dumped construction debris and dead animals. She planted a seedling, and the area gradually became an agroforest maintained by neighbours, non-governmental organisations and University of Brasília researchers.

The Federal District government formalised Silva’s care for the land in 2023. The project does not receive public investment, but the formal arrangement reduces the risk that the land will be seized or the work interrupted without warning. Its tree canopy now provides a cooler place where residents can escape the heat, and Silva says everyone is welcome to cool off there.

Activists hope Lagoinha Park, one of the few remaining green spaces in Sol Nascente, can receive similar attention. The park is now largely an open lawn with few native trees. Larissa Cordeiro wants it to become a proper public park with woodland, picnic tables and sports courts.

The area has also suffered from encroaching settlements, rubbish and deforestation. Buriti palms point to abundant groundwater, but waste accumulates around springs that feed the Melchior River and the Descoberto Basin, which provide more than 60 percent of the Federal District’s water supply.

Government plans face practical barriers

The Federal District approved a plan in December 2025 to reduce the “green deficit” in outlying areas. However, the government told Dialogue Earth that planting cannot begin in Sol Nascente until problems including narrow pavements and inadequate stormwater drainage are addressed. Without those works, officials said, saplings would not survive. They did not specify whether or when the infrastructure improvements would take place.

A broader National Urban Tree Planting Plan, launched at the UN’s COP30 climate conference in 2025, sets targets through 2045. Drawing on the “3-30-300” principle, it aims to increase the number of people able to see at least three trees from their homes. The plan says this could provide green-space access to about 40 million additional people.

The principle also calls for neighbourhoods to have at least 30 percent tree cover and for residents to live no more than 300 metres from a park or other green space. But Paulina Achurra of the educational platform Arq.Futuro warns that such programmes must be adapted to places with barely enough pavement to plant a tree.

For Sol Nascente’s residents, the immediate response remains local: creating shade, protecting neglected land and growing green spaces themselves in a city where access to nature—and protection from extreme heat—depends heavily on where people live.

As Bangladesh ages, support systems fall behind
global_south_and_exploitedmaterial_harmpolitics_and_powerregional expansion
What happened

Naharul Ahmed’s experience reflects a wider challenge in Bangladesh, where longer lives and a rapidly growing older population are outpacing pensions, healthcare and family support, leaving many older people dependent on relatives while facing rising medical and daily living costs.

At 72, Ahmed retired in 2010 after more than 25 years as a depot manager at a private chemicals company in Gazipur. He received no pension or other retirement benefits. After returning to his village, he tried to support himself through farming and fish cultivation, but a stroke in 2015 left him progressively weaker and dependent on regular treatment.

He now spends around Tk 2,000 a month on medicines. He and his wife depend on their children for daily expenses, illustrating how illness and the absence of retirement income can push older households into reliance on family members.

The pressure is growing as Bangladesh’s population ages. The share of people aged 60 and above rose from 5.9 percent in 1989 to 10.6 percent in 2022, according to the Bangladesh Demographic and Health Survey. The 2022 national census recorded the country’s total population at 16.98 crore.

Longer lives are driving the shift. Life expectancy at birth increased from about 59 years in 1990 to nearly 75 in 2023, while fertility rates have fallen. The number of people aged 65 and above is projected to exceed the number of children under five for the first time in the 2030s, according to a Global Burden of Disease study published by the University of Washington in 2021. By 2050, people aged 65 and above are projected to account for 18 percent of the population—four percentage points more than the share of people aged 5 to 19.

Healthcare is not yet organised around older people

The Health Sector Reform Commission, established by the interim government, warned last year that the elderly population was growing rapidly. It recommended organised geriatric services and a stronger social safety net.

Most public hospitals still have no dedicated geriatric services, while health insurance coverage among older people remains very limited. Bangladesh Medical University has only recently introduced formal postgraduate training in geriatric medicine, highlighting how new the specialty is within the country’s healthcare system.

Older people are more likely to need long-term treatment and care for chronic conditions. Public health expert Prof Abu Jamil Faisel said the health system remains focused mainly on women, mothers and children because maternal mortality and infectious diseases among children continue to be concerns. As the older population expands, he said, the system must give greater attention to non-communicable diseases including diabetes, hypertension, cardiovascular disease and cancer.

Faisel also called for a dedicated strategy covering dementia and Alzheimer’s disease, as well as training for caregivers and networks of volunteers or community groups able to provide long-term support. He said elderly care should combine medical services with social support and wider community involvement.

A limited allowance meets a widening need

The state’s main support programme is the Old Age Allowance, introduced in the 1997-98 fiscal year for women aged 62 and above and men aged 65 and above. It now reaches around 62 lakh beneficiaries, each receiving Tk 700 a month.

That payment is increasingly questioned against the scale of older people’s needs, particularly as medical treatment and long-term care become more important. The challenge is also extending beyond formal benefits: Dhaka University population sciences professor Aminul Haque said joint families were increasingly giving way to smaller nuclear households, while children often move elsewhere for education or work.

As a result, older parents may have less day-to-day support from relatives. Haque said replacing traditional family care was difficult and that existing initiatives were not enough for the needs of a growing older population. Discussions have included engaging young people in communities to check on older residents and help them when needed.

Government plans remain under development

The government says it is working to make healthcare more elderly-friendly. SM Ziauddin Hyder, special assistant on health affairs to the prime minister, said efforts were under way to strengthen nurses’ geriatric-care capacity and give the subject greater emphasis in medical education and training.

He also said the government was considering a broader initiative covering all 64 district towns. Under the proposal, each district town could develop a comprehensive elderly village offering accommodation, healthcare, livelihood activities and skills training, with residents engaged in productive work suited to their abilities.

Hyder described the goal as “healthy longevity”: not merely extending life, but enabling people to remain healthy for longer. The plans come as Bangladesh marks the International Day of Older Persons under the theme “The Age of Longevity: Rethinking Systems for Longer Lives,” focusing attention on the gap between demographic change and the systems available to support it.

State refinery ‘renaissance’ plan raises fresh questions over pollution, health and taxpayer risk
global_south_and_exploitedmaterial_harmpolitics_and_powerregional expansion
What happened

The proposed revival and expansion of South Africa’s mothballed Sapref refinery has placed Durban’s nearby communities in a dispute over renewed air pollution, inherited contamination, climate exposure and the possibility that taxpayers could finance an ageing, uneconomic asset.

The state-owned Central Energy Fund (CEF), backed by Mineral and Petroleum Resources Minister Gwede Mantashe, bought the refinery for one rand in 2024 and is proposing a R117-billion redevelopment, mainly funded by the state. The facility is now known as the South African National Petroleum Company (SANPC) refinery. Production has not yet restarted. The CEF plans to recommission it in three stages, eventually increasing capacity from its former maximum of 180,000 barrels of crude oil a day to about 400,000 and then 650,000 barrels a day.

Sapref was established and jointly operated by Shell and BP from 1963. For nearly 60 years, it released sulphur dioxide and other chemicals into the air above Durban. Operations stopped in April 2022 after floodwaters inundated the site and overturned some fuel-storage tanks, although the owners had already indicated that they intended to close the ageing refinery.

Communities fear renewed exposure

The proposed redevelopment is close to residential areas adjoining the South Durban industrial zone and storage facilities in Durban’s Island View harbour area. Merebank, Wentworth, Umlazi, Bluff and Isipingo are predominantly poor or lower-income communities located near these industrial sites.

Bongani Mthembu, an air-quality and health officer with the South Durban Community Environmental Alliance (SDCEA), said members raised concerns at a 14 July stakeholder meeting with CEF officials. They referred to 2007 studies by the University of KwaZulu-Natal and the University of Michigan, which found substantially higher asthma rates among children living near the Sapref and Engen refineries than among children in northern Durban. The studies also identified significant exposure to airborne benzene, a cancer-causing component of petrol, in southern Durban’s industrial area.

Mthembu said exposure to airborne pollution would increase dramatically if the SANPC refinery were expanded. SDCEA has also repeatedly questioned the reliability of air-pollution monitoring stations operated by industry and government.

The concerns include the site’s existing environmental liabilities. In 2001, nearly one million litres of petrol leaked from corroded pipelines beneath homes in Bluff and Wentworth. Several homes were evacuated for months while Sapref carried out remediation.

The Department of Forestry, Fisheries and the Environment has confirmed that the SANPC site is listed on the national Contaminated Land Register under the Waste Act. The department said it was officially notified of the contamination in a letter dated 18 October 2024, while remediation had begun before 2014 under a directive from the Department of Water and Sanitation. Monitoring and management remain ongoing, it said, and an independent environmental consultant had previously been appointed by Sapref to assess and monitor the site.

Under the notification, the CEF is now regarded as responsible for ongoing monitoring and management. The department also said the CEF must complete an environmental impact assessment for listed activities connected to the proposed redevelopment and expansion, allowing interested and affected parties to participate in the decision-making process.

SDCEA is seeking a broader, independently verified environmental and health assessment covering soil and groundwater contamination, risks to residents, possible marine impacts and longer-term health effects. It also wants a site inspection to verify reported corrosion, blockages and contamination.

Dispute over historic liabilities

The refinery was sold under “clean break” or *voetstoots* terms, intensifying disagreement over who should pay for historic contamination. SDCEA opposes any arrangement that transfers environmental liabilities to the state or public before the extent of the pollution and responsibility for remediation are established.

In a 27 May letter to the alliance, SANPC refinery managing director Sesakho Magadla said the CEF was responsible for liabilities arising from the asset from its transfer on 1 December 2024, as well as liabilities arising from its subsequent operation. Historic operator liabilities, he said, would remain with the previous operator.

Open Secrets investigative reports argued that the one-rand sale did not reflect the likely costs of recommissioning the refinery, cleaning up contamination or eventually decommissioning it. University of KwaZulu-Natal environmental law expert Professor Michael Kidd said environmental and waste legislation was designed to address heavily contaminated sites, but questioned whether the CEF had adequately considered future liability when entering the deal.

The CEF says the redevelopment will be “commercially disciplined, technically credible and financially sustainable”. Dave Wright, director of the SA National Energy Association, told *Engineering News* that acquiring such an old refinery could cost taxpayers more than it was worth to operate and leave the country with a stranded asset within 10 to 15 years. The CEF declined to answer several written queries, while spokesperson Jacky Mashapu said the issues had already been addressed when the asset was purchased.

Climate and flood risks

Critics say the financial exposure is also tied to longer-term climate risks. David Hallowes of the environmental justice group groundWork argued that increased carbon emissions from an expanded refinery would undermine the government’s net-zero transport pathway and could make the facility a stranded asset by 2045 or sooner.

Hallowes also pointed to the refinery’s location on a reclaimed floodplain near the mouth of the canalised uMlazi River. Citing research by Wits University climatology professor Francois Engelbrecht, he said a weather event similar to the April 2022 KwaZulu-Natal floods could produce twice as much rain in the warmer conditions expected in the 2040s. He argued that rebuilding the refinery at the same location would therefore expose it to physical flood risk alongside pollution and liability concerns.

Researchers at the National University of Singapore and the University of Oxford have proposed Omni-IO Skills, a preprint describing a plug-and-play harness intended to help general-purpose agents coordinate text, images, audio, video, documents, 3D assets and code without changing the host model’s reasoning core.

PanoVLN is a proposed vision-and-language navigation system that uses 360-degree RGB panoramas to help an agent follow natural-language instructions. Developed by researchers at Zhejiang University and the University of Hong Kong, it reports higher success rates on two benchmark tasks and faster movement in quadruped-robot trials.

The work addresses a limitation in conventional navigation systems, which generally use perspective images. A panorama can expose passages, landmarks and route alternatives outside a camera’s forward field of view, potentially allowing the agent to choose a route without further exploration. However, the researchers found that simply replacing perspective images with equirectangular panoramas produced only limited gains. They argue that panoramic navigation requires changes to action prediction, training supervision and visual representation.

PanoVLN makes the model predict longer sequences of actions so it can make larger turns and continue moving from one observation. Its confidence-guided execution strategy dynamically decides how many predicted actions to carry out before observing the scene again and replanning. To improve decisions at junctions, the researchers created 98,000 trajectories across 800 HM3D scenes, emphasizing frequent branching points, clearly specified route choices, turns and stopping points. The instructions were generated and checked against route observations, according to the paper.

The system also combines semantic features from a vision-language model with geometric features extracted by PanoVGGT from the same RGB panorama. These features are aligned across corresponding regions of the equirectangular image to represent both scene content and spatial layout, without adding visual tokens or using depth input.

With a 4B backbone and RGB-only observations, the paper reports success rates of 77.3% on R2R-CE Val-Unseen and 78.0% on RxR-CE Val-Unseen. These figures are presented as 11.9 and 8.7 percentage points above the previous state of the art, respectively. In real-world experiments, a quadruped robot reportedly completed indoor and outdoor routes in less time, with fewer policy calls and pauses than prior vision-and-language navigation methods.

The paper is an arXiv preprint dated 28 September 2026 and has not been peer reviewed. Its results therefore represent the authors’ reported evaluation rather than an established consensus.

What happened

A preprint introduces VoxMem, a benchmark testing whether large audio language models can remember not only spoken content but also speaker identity, vocal manner and environmental sounds across multiple conversations—capabilities that remain unreliable in evaluations of 15 models.

VoxMem is designed around a gap the authors identify in existing benchmarks: most focus on the lexical content of speech, test only limited memory operations and use a single session rather than a history accumulated over separate interactions. The benchmark separates two dimensions of spoken memory: the acoustic evidence a system must retain and the operation it must perform with that evidence.

The dataset contains 3,196 evaluation instances built from 34,743 spoken sessions totaling 177 hours. It covers four evidence types—speech semantics, speaker identity, paralinguistic cues such as vocal manner, and environmental sound—and four operations: information extraction, multi-session reasoning, temporal tracking and answer refusal. The instances span 15 valid evidence-operation combinations and are embedded in histories scaled from 8,000 to 64,000 tokens, corresponding to roughly 2.5 to 20 minutes of audio. The same question and relevant evidence are held fixed as the history grows, allowing context length to be studied directly.

The authors evaluated 15 systems, including 10 open-weight and five proprietary models, at four history lengths. None exceeded 40% overall accuracy at 32,000 tokens. At that length, proprietary models averaged 55.6% on speech semantics but 32.7% on speaker identity, 20.0% on paralinguistic cues and 21.9% on environmental sound. Open-weight models showed the same broad separation, with lower averages for each category.

Performance also depended on the kind of memory operation. Models reached 44.5% when tracking speech semantics, but scores fell to 20.0% for speaker identity, 3.4% for paralinguistic evidence and 1.2% for environmental sound in the reported operation breakdown. Multi-session reasoning was relatively stronger than information extraction for several audio-native categories, while answer refusal was more successful on paralinguistic and environmental questions.

The preprint reports that accuracy generally declined as histories lengthened: from 8K to 32K, average performance fell from 40.1% to 33.0% for proprietary models and from 26.6% to 21.9% for open-weight models. Its error analysis distinguishes failures to associate an acoustic cue with the correct context from failures that occur before useful acoustic information is recovered, suggesting that “remembering audio” is not one uniform capability.

What happened

A controlled study of world action models finds that retaining a representation of the future during inference is important for generalization, even when that future is never denoised into visible frames. The proposed approach preserves most of this benefit while substantially reducing the computational cost of explicit future generation.

World action models (WAMs) jointly predict future visual dynamics and actions from observations and language instructions. The future-prediction objective provides dense visual supervision beyond action labels, and is widely associated with the models’ robustness. But whether future modeling must continue at deployment has remained contested: explicit WAMs generate future frames alongside each action chunk, while latent WAMs use the future during training but omit it during inference to accelerate control.

The study by researchers from Tsinghua University, the University of Science and Technology of China, and Beijing Institute of Technology compares these approaches using a matched backbone, training data and computational budget. It evaluates generalization along three axes: robustness to environmental changes, performance with fewer demonstrations, and transfer to tasks not represented by action-training data.

The researchers report that the explicit and latent systems are nearly tied on in-distribution tasks, scoring 97.75 and 96.85 respectively in one comparison. Their performance separates when conditions change, however. The study says the latent approach consistently loses the generalization advantages associated with WAMs across all three axes, indicating that future modeling functions as more than a training objective.

The results also suggest that full future-video generation is unnecessary. In the proposed “Simple-WAM,” future video tokens remain in the model’s input but are left as pure Gaussian noise. A single video-expert pass produces features that condition every action-denoising step. On environmental-perturbation tests, Simple-WAM averaged 79.5, compared with 67.7 for the explicit model and 53.8 for the latent model among the compared systems without embodied pretraining. Its performance was strongest on six of seven LIBERO-Plus factors, although the advantage narrowed when perturbations changed what the camera saw.

This design also reduced latency. Simple-WAM took 74.7 milliseconds per action chunk, compared with 286.9 milliseconds for the explicit model and 62.0 milliseconds for the latent model. The authors report that the three approaches remained within 2.3 percentage points of one another on in-distribution tasks, while Simple-WAM combined near-latent speed with stronger performance across the tested generalization settings.

The findings are presented in an arXiv preprint and therefore remain a research claim rather than peer-reviewed evidence.

Researchers are moving corals from shallow areas into deeper, cooler water, treating reefs with protective microorganisms and microbial cocktails, adding micronutrients, and breeding corals for greater tolerance to heat and acidity. What happened

At Sandia National Laboratories in Albuquerque, mineral physicist Alisha Clark is using the Z machine to test whether Earth trapped water inside its molten interior during formation—a possibility that could reshape explanations for how the planet’s surface oceans originated.

Geoscientists have debated whether water survived Earth’s violent birth about 4.5 billion years ago. One long-standing theory holds that the young planet was so hot that water molecules escaped into space, with surface water arriving later through icy comets and asteroids. Water now found inside Earth could then have been carried downward by tectonic plates.

Clark is investigating an alternative: that molten rock retained some of the water incorporated into Earth from cosmic dust and gas. If so, volcanic activity may have released part of the planet’s original interior water over geological time, while much larger amounts—potentially oceans’ worth—could remain underground. Discoveries including a 2014 analysis of a deep-Earth diamond, whose mineral impurity contained substantial water within its crystal structure, have pointed to a larger interior reservoir than plate tectonics alone might explain.

To test the idea, Clark sends shockwaves through water-infused glass, using the material as a proxy for molten silicate rock. The Z machine briefly reproduces pressures found near Earth’s centre. In earlier experiments, she found that glass containing substantial water became stiffer but increasingly compressible as pressure rose. Clark says this unusual behaviour could indicate that molten rock can trap water under the pressures of Earth’s mantle, even during the planet’s magma-ocean phase.

The machine’s electrical pulse lasts only a few billionths of a second but produces more power momentarily than all the world’s power plants. Its capacitor ring fills a space roughly the size of two basketball courts, and each shot requires months of preparation, more than 100 people and about $250,000.

Is exposomics the key to making personalized medicine a reality?

What happened

Exposomics seeks to make personalized medicine more precise by measuring the environmental and lifestyle factors that interact with each person’s genes. The approach could improve disease-risk prediction and treatment choices, but researchers still cannot measure every exposure or reliably connect all detected signals to individual outcomes.

The proposed future is one in which routine medical visits include blood and urine samples, a patient’s postal code, and information about diet, medication, physical activity and other habits. Those samples could reveal nutrients, pharmaceuticals, infections and chemicals encountered, while location data could indicate exposure to air pollution, noise and other environmental hazards. Combined with genomic information, these measurements might help clinicians identify distinctive disease risks and select interventions to slow disease progression.

The exposome—the totality of environmental exposures and lifestyle factors that act alongside genetics—was named in 2005 by Christopher Wild, a cancer epidemiologist formerly at the International Agency for Research on Cancer in Lyon, France. Researchers describe it as relevant to conditions including cancer, heart failure, diabetes and dementia. Exposure can alter gene activity: tobacco smoke, for example, can silence tumour-suppressor genes. Nature’s Precision Medicine Spotlight says environmental exposures account for between 70% and 90% of chronic-disease risk, while Gary Miller of Columbia University argues that care cannot be fully personalized without accounting for lifestyle, diet, medications and environmental exposures.

The field is developing tools to assemble that information. Researchers combine biobanks, long-term cohort studies, lifestyle surveys and environmental datasets with biomarkers in blood or urine. High-throughput mass spectrometry can detect large numbers of proteins and metabolites, while transcriptomics—such as Ana Maretti Garcia’s work on cells exposed to “forever chemicals”—can show how cells respond. Persistent pollutants may leave signatures in blood lipids, and smoking-related epigenetic markers can remain in DNA decades after someone stops smoking.

Early results suggest that combinations of exposures may be more informative than individual measurements. In March, Chirag Patel of Harvard Medical School and colleagues compared 619 exposure markers with 305 measurable traits using data from ten cohorts of the US National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey. Individual exposures explained less than 1% of variation in disease outcomes, but combinations of 20 exposures explained an average of 3.5%, a level the researchers said rivalled the predictive power of disease-related genetic variants. A combination including trans fats and polychlorinated biphenyls explained 43% of variation in participants’ triglyceride levels.

Major obstacles remain. Critics question whether exposomics is distinct from environmental epidemiology and whether the totality of human exposure can ever be measured. Mass-spectrometry scans can produce 10,000 to 100,000 peaks, many of whose chemical identities are unknown. The Exposome-Scan facility in Leiden can currently identify about 700 chemicals—estimated to represent 10–20% of the few thousand chemicals causing significant exposures globally. Researchers therefore see exposomics as a promising route toward precision prevention, but one whose clinical use depends on better standards, larger studies and improved data analysis.

What happened

A large genomic and fossil analysis suggests that bats most likely evolved in Europe about 65 to 60 million years ago, before spreading into Africa and then across the Americas, Asia and Australia. The finding revises a decades-old debate over where these flying mammals first appeared.

The study, published in *Nature*, was conducted through the Bat1K consortium, an international effort to sequence the genomes of living bat species. It brought together 137 researchers from 64 countries and examined 103 bat genomes, including 42 newly generated chromosome-level assemblies representing all 21 recognized bat families. The team also incorporated evidence from 44 fossil bats.

Africa, Asia and North America had all previously been proposed as possible birthplaces of bats. By combining genetic data from living species with fossils, the researchers reconstructed a family tree that points to Europe during the late Paleocene as the most likely origin. Their analysis suggests that descendants subsequently moved into Africa, while separate lineages later expanded into the Americas, Asia and Australia.

The reconstruction also offers clues about when bats acquired their defining abilities. The fossil bat *Vielasia*, positioned on the oldest branch of the bat family tree, suggests that echolocation had already evolved close to the beginning of bat history. The findings indicate that echolocation and true powered flight appeared before modern bat groups began diversifying, potentially helping bats spread and become highly diverse.

Building the family tree was difficult because different parts of bat genomes sometimes preserve conflicting evolutionary signals, possibly reflecting gene exchange among early lineages. The researchers found that the X chromosome carried a particularly clear record of relationships between bat groups, helping resolve disputes that had persisted for decades.

The project also computationally reconstructed the genome of the common ancestor of all living bats. That resource could allow scientists to investigate the genetic changes associated with flight, echolocation, longevity and disease resistance. Bats account for about one fifth of living mammal species and are the only mammals known to have evolved sustained powered flight, but the study’s geographic conclusion remains a research finding expressed as the most likely evolutionary history rather than an observed historical record.

Sharks can hear nearly 250 feet away and pinpoint the source

What happened

Wild blacktip sharks detected low-frequency sounds from up to 74 meters (243 feet) away and often turned sharply from the source, suggesting they can identify where distant sounds originate. The finding gives researchers new evidence about a poorly understood sense that may help sharks interpret their surroundings beyond visual range.

Researchers from Florida Atlantic University tested sharks in shallow coastal waters across Palm Beach County, where blacktip sharks gather predictably. They used an underwater speaker, calibrated hydrophones and a drone recording the animals from 40 to 50 meters above the water. The speaker drifted with the current, reducing the chance that the anchored boat would affect the sharks’ behavior.

The team played sounds in three low-frequency ranges: 100–200, 200–400 and 400–800 hertz. A 10-kilohertz sound, outside sharks’ known hearing range, served as a control. The sounds were designed to provoke a startle response rather than lure the animals toward the speaker.

The sharks responded to all three low-frequency ranges but not to the control. They detected sounds from as far as 74 meters, or 243 feet, and more than 70% of recorded responses occurred in the acoustic far field, where sound behaves differently from how it does near its source. Their rapid turns away from the speaker indicated that they were responding not only to sound but also to its direction. Lower-frequency sounds were detected from greater distances and at lower sound levels.

The findings, published in *Integrative Organismal Biology*, provide what the researchers describe as the first quantified evidence that free-swimming sharks can detect and orient away from sounds in the acoustic far field. Sharks lack the gas-filled swim bladder found in many bony fish and are instead believed to depend largely on their inner ears, including a sensory structure called the macula neglecta.

The researchers say ocean experiments are important because tank walls can reflect sound and create confusing acoustic patterns.
