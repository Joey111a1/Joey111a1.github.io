The Collapse of Cuba
global_south_and_exploitedmaterial_harmpolitics_and_powermajor supplement
What happened

In Cuba, prolonged economic and institutional breakdown is leaving people without reliable food, electricity, water, healthcare or state assistance. An account from across the island describes churches and informal networks taking on essential support roles as migration, poverty and public anger deepen.

In eastern Havana, priest Iván Guerra Álvarez is helping his church recover an 18th-century cloister that the government took after the 1959 revolution and left damaged. The church, once watched by armed guards and used partly for state education, is again hosting worship, Bible lessons and meals. Guerra Álvarez believes Cuba is nearing another political turning point and that religious institutions will matter in any transition, though no timetable or mechanism is clear.

The broader picture is not of a single crisis but of a state retreating from daily life. During a reported August visit, ration cards had provided no food for weeks, blackouts were routine, and banks sometimes restricted withdrawals to less than $1.50 a day. Public-sector workers, including doctors and teachers, were earning about $10 a month, while a carton of eggs could cost as much as 4,700 pesos.

Basic services are failing

The effects are especially severe in eastern Cuba, traditionally poorer and less developed than Havana. The account describes damaged roads, sparse traffic, horse-drawn carts and growing numbers of people begging. In Santiago de Cuba, a 76-year-old woman and her daughter reportedly survived by asking for money and collecting bottles after the woman’s son, their previous support, died of cancer. They lacked mattresses and electricity at home.

Healthcare has also been weakened by staff departures, shortages of medicine and inadequate infrastructure. On Cayo Granma, nurse Clara Luaces was caring for the island’s 870 residents and people from nearby coastal areas in a cramped clinic. She said doctors had left so quickly that she could no longer track the patients seeking replacement care. After piped water stopped for three months, she reported more fevers and skin infections linked to poor access to clean water.

Families are adapting however they can. A fisherman without refrigeration had returned to drying and salting fish. In other places, residents relied on charity, begging or local religious groups. The account says Cuba may have lost up to a quarter of its population to migration in recent years, leaving elderly people and other vulnerable residents without family support.

Churches fill the gap

Catholic and evangelical congregations are expanding their practical role as government provision deteriorates. In Baracoa, Pentecostal pastor Raul de Jesus Muñoz Munder described a weekly system in which local pastors identify households needing food or medicine and organize assistance. His congregation has about 1,100 regular members but supports a wider population; his denomination has grown from two churches in the region to 40 over three decades.

Independent religious-affiliation figures are unavailable because the government controls surveys, so the scale of the revival cannot be measured precisely. But the account describes growing Catholic, evangelical and Afro-Cuban religious activity, including small house churches.

The political meaning remains uncertain. Some residents blamed Cuba’s internal economic system rather than the United States, while others identified the pandemic’s damage to tourism and remittances as a major accelerant. The government continues to project authority through official ceremonies and revolutionary symbolism, even as many people appear increasingly dependent on institutions outside the state.

Three weeks after leaving a refugee camp, a Sudanese woman returned to el-Fasher to recover identity documents and found a city marked by looting, destroyed homes and continued RSF intimidation. Her brief return ended with threats and a decision never to go back, illustrating how survivors remain unsafe after fleeing the massacre.

Hala, who uses a pseudonym for security reasons, travelled from Tawila to el-Fasher, about 60km away, in a vehicle operated by the Rapid Support Forces (RSF). Fighters questioned her destination, the location of her family home and how long she intended to stay before issuing an entry permit. The next day, RSF fighters confronted her in the street with abusive language. She said civilians nearby prevented a physical assault, but described women’s travel to el-Fasher as extremely dangerous and said people who resist RSF demands risk rape, beatings, torture or imprisonment. She returned to Tawila and said she would not return, even if conditions improved.

Her account comes after the RSF’s capture of North Darfur’s capital on 26 October 2025, following more than 500 days of siege. The source reports that the assault killed civilians in large numbers, including nearly 500 people at the Saudi Maternity Hospital, while UN investigators documented killings at the hospital, el-Fasher University, roads and the berms surrounding the city. Monitoring groups counted at least 6,000 deaths during the first 72 hours, though the communications blackout and restricted access leave the full toll unknown.

Survivors remain trapped in displacement

Those who escaped have faced further detention, ransom demands and hunger. One monitoring group documented the detention of nearly 68,000 civilians, while Tawila has become host to about 800,000 displaced people, most from el-Fasher. The source also reports that one in six people screened by a UN team that entered the city in December had severe acute malnutrition.

The UN Independent International Fact-Finding Mission for Sudan determined in February that the RSF committed genocidal acts in el-Fasher. Meanwhile, survivors and aid workers describe detention sites, shortages of food and medical care, and continuing danger for civilians trying to return.

China: Ensuring Guidance Escort Services Are Protected from Overstepping Boundaries (Information Service Desk)
china_domesticpolitics_and_powerregional expansion
What happened

A national standard for socialized medical-visit escort services, GB/T 47801-2026, will take effect on November 1 after approval by the State Administration for Market Regulation. It responds to difficulties faced by older people navigating large hospitals alone and by patients traveling from other areas. The standard defines providers, service content and operating procedures, seeking to address unclear boundaries and inconsistent quality in the emerging escort industry.

Under the standard, institutions provide the service and escorts assist people with mobility, cognitive or regional barriers, or those seeking help during medical visits. Permitted tasks include navigating departments, arranging appointments and examinations, handling payments and admission procedures, collecting medicines or documents, and providing practical or emotional support with the patient’s or legal guardian’s consent. Escorts are not medical professionals: they may not interpret results, give medical advice, alter medication, participate in treatment, make treatment decisions, conceal medical information or sign binding medical documents. Deng Yong of Beijing University of Chinese Medicine said escorts should understand hospital procedures, basic medical terminology and emergencies, while avoiding appointment-slot resale and drug promotion. He also proposed hospital registration, identity checks, access credentials, complaints channels and stronger action against price fraud and unlicensed operations.

What happened

Fifty years after Richard Dawkins’s *The Selfish Gene* was published, a reassessment argues that its influential gene-centred account of evolution is too deterministic, and that readers seeking to understand life today need a broader view of heredity and biological change.

Published in 1976, Dawkins’s book presented a clear explanation of evolution in which organisms function, in effect, as means through which genes are reproduced across generations. The assessment recalls that this framework once appeared consistent with mainstream sociobiology and with ideas being taught in university biology courses.

Re-reading the book now, the author finds its implications less harmless. The argument is presented as a problem of determinism rather than as a claim that Dawkins explicitly endorsed every such social implication.

The essay places the book within the modern evolutionary synthesis, which combined neo-Darwinian evolution with Mendelian genetics and used changes in gene variants to describe population-level evolution. It traces this gene-focused tradition through figures including Ronald Fisher, George Williams and William Hamilton, while noting that Dawkins pushed their ideas into a more explicit and forceful form.

The historical account also highlights earlier disagreements. August Weismann argued that inherited material passed through germ cells, whereas Charles Darwin increasingly allowed that use and disuse might contribute to inherited change. William Bateson promoted Mendelian rules as a general theory of heredity, despite Walter Weldon’s objection that traits such as height reflect many hereditary and environmental influences.

The reassessment concludes that biology has since developed new tools, models and cross-disciplinary approaches for describing that complexity. It therefore recommends contemporary works that treat genes as part of a wider biological system, rather than as the sole explanation of life and behaviour.

What happened

Brazilian President Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva won re-election on 25 October in a narrow, unexpected result, taking 51.8% of the vote against 48.2% for right-wing candidate Flávio Bolsonaro. Lula had trailed Bolsonaro in the first round on 4 October, but neither candidate won a majority, requiring a runoff. The result brought relief among many Brazilian scientists. Leading scientific organizations, including the Brazilian Academy of Sciences, had circulated an open letter endorsing Lula that gathered more than 20,000 signatures.

Researchers’ concerns centered on the future of science funding, universities and environmental policy. They feared Flávio Bolsonaro would move Brazil’s 69 federal universities from the Education Ministry to the smaller Science and Technology Ministry and prioritize applied research over basic science. By contrast, Lula has pledged to strengthen federal universities and institutes; his government also brought about 600 Brazilian scientists home from overseas institutions in 2024. Supporters further welcomed his commitment to ending Amazon deforestation by 2030 and aligning emissions cuts with the Paris Agreement. The source says deforestation fell to its lowest recorded level under Lula, while it rose by almost 60% between 2019 and 2023 under his predecessor, Jair Bolsonaro.

Can science make your dog live longer? Longevity craze comes to pets
science_generalpublic_healthpolitics_and_powereconomy_and_businessscience anchorscience_journalism
What happened

Earlier this month, researchers from universities and companies, accompanied by some of their dogs, gathered in Toronto, Canada, for the inaugural Animal Longevity Summit. The small meeting highlighted growing interest in extending pets’ healthy lifespans. Topics included ageing across animal species, canine cancer, axolotl regeneration and the genetics of mammals adapted to extreme environments. Cameron Hurlburt of Miami-based Ani VC said the US pet- and animal-health market is estimated at US$150 billion and could expand as pet-health insurance becomes more common.

Most efforts remain focused on diet, including probiotic products, while some companies are adapting drugs already approved for humans. The Cat Health Company in Bucharest is developing treatments for ageing-related conditions in cats, including sarcopenia and kidney disease; its co-founder Alex Băciţa said two compounds are in clinical trials. In the United States, San Diego-based Rejuvenate Bio is developing a gene therapy for mitral valve disease in dogs, with longer-term ambitions involving metabolic disorders and longevity. Brussels-based Genflow Biosciences is pursuing a gene therapy aimed at extending canine longevity and reducing sarcopenia. These approaches are still under investigation, while academic researchers say industry partnerships may be needed to finance dog-focused studies.

A 2,700-year-old solar eclipse just revealed something surprising about Earth
science_generalglobal_south_and_exploitedpolitics_and_powerclimate_disasterscience anchorinstitutional_press_release
What happened

A corrected map of the ancient Chinese capital of Qufu has enabled researchers to reconstruct a total solar eclipse from 709 BCE, refining estimates of Earth’s rotation and offering a tentative glimpse of the Sun emerging from a prolonged period of low activity.

On July 17, 709 BCE, observers at the court of China’s Lu Duchy recorded a total eclipse in the *Spring and Autumn Annals*, a chronicle compiled roughly two to three centuries later. Modern calculations initially suggested that a total eclipse could not have been visible from the location traditionally assigned to the court. The international research team traced the problem to an approximately eight-kilometre error in the ancient city’s coordinates.

Using archaeological excavation reports and historical geographic evidence, the researchers revised Qufu’s position and combined it with astronomical calculations. The reconstruction placed the eclipse within view of the Lu court and produced improved constraints on Earth’s rotation during the eighth to sixth centuries BCE. For the 709 BCE event, the calculated value of ΔT—a measure of the difference between Earth’s irregular rotation and uniform time—was between 20,264 and 21,204 seconds.

Earth’s spin has gradually slowed over long periods, partly because lunar tides create friction in the oceans. The revised eclipse reconstruction therefore provides a more precise historical measurement of a planet that was rotating somewhat faster 2,700 years ago. The results, published in *Astrophysical Journal Letters*, may also improve calculations of when and where other ancient astronomical events were visible.

A possible record of the ancient Sun

The team also examined a later passage in the *Hanshu*, or *Book of Han*, which preserves an account written about seven centuries after the eclipse. Its description of the eclipsed Sun may refer to the solar corona, the faint outer atmosphere visible during totality. Researchers simulated the corona’s possible appearance and found it consistent with a Sun returning to stronger activity after an extended quiet phase.

That interpretation remains uncertain because the corona description was recorded long after the event. The eclipse itself is regarded as a securely dated total eclipse, but the later passage cannot provide definitive proof of the Sun’s condition in 709 BCE.

The possible observation nevertheless aligns with reconstructions based on radiocarbon in tree rings. Those records suggest that the Sun had recently emerged from the Neo Assyrian, or Homer, Grand Minimum, dated approximately from 808 to 717 BCE. The 709 BCE eclipse occurred shortly afterward, when regular solar activity may have resumed. Together, the corrected geography, ancient records and modern modelling show how historical observations can refine knowledge of both Earth’s rotation and long-term solar behavior.

Exercise doesn't just strengthen your heart. It rewires it
science_generalglobal_south_and_exploitedpublic_healthscience anchorinstitutional_press_release
What happened

Regular aerobic activity changed the nerve structures involved in heart regulation in rats, with markedly different effects on the two sides of the body. The findings could eventually inform more targeted treatment for rhythm disorders and chest pain, but whether the same neural pattern exists in humans is not yet known.

Researchers at the University of Bristol, with colleagues from University College London, the University of São Paulo and the Federal University of São Paulo, examined rats after a 10-week exercise programme. The results were published in *Autonomic Neuroscience* on October 26, 2026.

The study examined the stellate ganglia, paired nerve centres located near the lower neck and upper chest. These structures form part of the autonomic nervous system and send signals that influence heart activity. Using three-dimensional stereological methods, the researchers estimated the number, dimensions and distribution of cells within the ganglia.

Exercise produced a strong asymmetry. The right-side cardiovascular ganglion had about four times the neurons found in the left-side ganglion, a pattern that differed from that seen in sedentary rats. The cells also changed in contrasting ways: those on the left grew to nearly twice their previous size, while those on the right became somewhat smaller.

The results indicate that exercise may remodel the heart’s neural control system in a side-specific manner, rather than producing an identical change throughout the body. That distinction could eventually matter for treatments that reduce excessive nerve signalling, including stellate ganglion blocks and denervation procedures. The research team said the findings may help refine approaches to arrhythmias, angina and stress-induced “broken-heart” syndrome.

However, the study did not establish what the structural differences do to heart performance during rest or activity. It also provides no evidence yet that the same changes occur in people. The researchers plan to test how the altered nerve anatomy relates to cardiac function, examine additional animal models and explore non-invasive indicators that could support future human research. Any clinical application therefore remains prospective rather than established.

Chinese scientists discover “D-rex,” a giant fungus with roots stretching back 65 million years
science_generalglobal_south_and_exploitedscience anchorattributed source material (republished)
What happened

Scientists in China have identified a previously unknown giant fungus in Yunnan, with structures reaching 50 centimetres and weighing up to 2.2 kilograms. Its lineage is estimated at about 65 million years old, offering a possible link to environmental changes after the dinosaur-era extinction.

The species, *Dianjunus rex*, was encountered during surveys in several Yunnan habitats, including conifer forests near Damaidi and Yipinglang and tropical rainforest near Nanbang. Its pale reproductive structures emerged from the woody roots of Chinese cork oak trees. The researchers named a new genus and family, *Dianjunaceae*, for the organism.

The structures are the largest fruiting bodies yet recorded among fungi in the phylum Ascomycota. To characterize the discovery, the team preserved specimens, grew spores in the laboratory, assessed physical features and sequenced the fungus’s genome. Comparisons with genetic data from hundreds of other fungi supported the classification and helped estimate the family’s evolutionary age.

An ancient lineage and an unresolved evolutionary puzzle

Using molecular-clock modelling, the researchers placed the lineage’s origin approximately 65 million years ago, around one million years after the Cretaceous–Paleogene mass extinction. They suggest that major forest changes and the increased supply of dead plant matter after that event might have opened ecological opportunities for wood-decomposing fungi. The evidence presents a possible timing relationship rather than proof that the extinction caused the fungus to evolve.

The researchers also propose that the organism’s exceptional height may help release spores into moving air above forest-floor vegetation and debris. That strategy would carry a substantial energy cost, however, leaving the reason for its size unresolved. Published in *Frontiers in Fungal Biology*, the study also points to the limited documentation of fungal diversity in China’s subtropical forests, where difficult terrain, seasonal growth and concealed reproductive structures hinder discovery.
