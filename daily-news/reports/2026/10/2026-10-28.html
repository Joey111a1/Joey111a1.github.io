What happened

China’s national standard for socialized accompanied medical visits, approved and issued by the State Administration for Market Regulation, will take effect on November 1, 2026. The services target people who struggle with medical visits because of mobility, cognitive or regional barriers, including elderly patients and people traveling to unfamiliar hospitals.

politics_and_powermaterial_harmmajor supplement
What happened

Cychlorphine has been linked to 122 or more US fatalities from July 2025 onward and has been identified in roughly 25 states, according to medical and toxicology experts. The Office of National Drug Control Policy classified it as an emerging drug threat in April. In August, the Drug Enforcement Administration temporarily assigned it to Schedule I, a designation that made the substance illegal in the United States.

The drug is part of the newer orphine group, which Dr. Nabarun Dasgupta of the University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill said has not been used medicinally. UNC researchers detected cychlorphine in the country in August 2024. Laboratory modeling indicates greater potency than fentanyl, and Dr. Shravani Durbhakula said opioid-related effects can include slowed breathing, sedation, reduced consciousness and unresponsiveness. It has also been found alongside fentanyl, heroin and in counterfeit pills. Naloxone can reverse its effects, but fentanyl strips and standard hospital screening generally do not identify it. Dasgupta described US cases as sporadic relative to fentanyl, while Dr. Alex Krotulski expects occurrences to diminish after scheduling, even as other novel opioids continue to appear.

California voters are due to decide on Proposition 40, a proposed one-time 5% tax on billionaires resident in the state on 1 January. Supporters say it could fund public services and challenge concentrated wealth; opponents warn that wealthy residents and tax revenue could leave.

California’s proposal targets assets rather than income, amid claims that the richest Americans can reduce taxable income, shift resources across jurisdictions and defer taxation on accumulated wealth. Opposition from wealthy Californians is already organized. The commentary presents the vote as a test of whether American governments can tax extreme wealth without accelerating interstate flight. To investigate the effect, the team combined virology experiments, analysis of gene activity and organoid models. The work used cells associated with the lungs, eyes and brain, together with laboratory-grown miniature tissue systems. These experiments were designed to assess whether the drugs’ effects differed across human tissue types.

The researchers identified possible implications for Rift Valley fever virus, influenza A, adenovirus and SARS-CoV-2, the virus responsible for COVID-19. Denis Kainov, a professor at NTNU’s Department of Clinical and Molecular Medicine, said the results point to an important unintended effect of this class of anti-inflammatory treatment. Erlend Ravlo, the study’s first author, linked the concern to patients who already carry or are experiencing viral infections.

The findings do not negate the drugs’ role in treating inflammatory disease. Instead, the researchers say their impact on immune defenses should be considered when prescribing them during an infection or a period of increased viral spread. They call for additional research to determine how the medicines can be used most safely during outbreaks.

The same immune-suppressing action may also have a research benefit: under controlled laboratory conditions, it could help scientists study vaccines or assess antiviral medicines. The reported results therefore identify a potential treatment risk while also suggesting a possible experimental application.

Scientists discover a surprising clue to why some brains resist Alzheimer’s
science_generalglobal_south_and_exploitedpublic_healthscience_cs_aiscience anchorinstitutional_press_release
What happened

Some older adults show Alzheimer’s-related pathology without developing dementia, and researchers in the Netherlands say differences in rare immature neurons may offer a clue to that resilience. The finding could redirect attention toward how some brains preserve function amid disease-related damage, though the cells’ role remains unproven.

Researchers at the Netherlands Institute for Neuroscience examined donated human brain tissue from the Netherlands Brain Bank. They compared three groups: people with no detectable brain pathology, people who developed Alzheimer’s dementia, and people whose brains contained Alzheimer’s pathology but who remained cognitively healthy. The team focused on a small area within the brain’s memory center where new neurons might arise.

The cells appeared in all three groups, including participants whose average age exceeded 80. Their presence suggests that some cellular characteristics associated with development and adaptability persist into later life. However, resilient participants did not have substantially larger numbers of immature neurons than those who developed dementia.

The more notable difference involved cellular activity. In the resilient group, the neurons displayed patterns associated with surviving and responding to harmful conditions. The tissue also showed weaker activity linked to inflammation and cell death. Researchers therefore propose that the cells might support nearby brain tissue, rather than functioning only as replacements for neurons lost through disease.

This interpretation relates to adult neurogenesis, the possibility that new nerve cells can arise in the adult human brain. The question remains scientifically contested. Because the cells were exceptionally rare, the team developed techniques to identify and assess them in precisely targeted tissue while limiting dependence on assumptions based on animal studies.

Evgenia Salta, the research’s senior author, said the observations may help explain why some people retain memory and thinking abilities despite Alzheimer’s pathology. But she also stressed that the study did not directly demonstrate what the neurons do. Their proposed protective function is inferred from patterns in preserved brain tissue, not shown through experiments that test their activity. Their presence likewise does not establish that older human brains continuously generate new neurons or that the cells prevent dementia.

The researchers regard the result as one element of a broader explanation for cognitive resilience. They plan to study how immature neurons interact with neighboring brain cells, which may clarify whether they actively contribute to preserved brain function and whether their responses could eventually guide strategies for protecting memory.
