What happened

The Ebola outbreak declared on May 15 in eastern Democratic Republic of the Congo has killed 4,018 people among 8,300 confirmed cases, according to the country’s National Institute of Public Health. With a case-fatality rate of 48.4%, it is described as the second-deadliest Ebola outbreak globally, behind the 2014–2016 West African epidemic, which killed roughly 11,000 people. The outbreak has reached 63 health zones in seven provinces, with Ituri the epicenter. A further 2,162 patients have recovered, while 842 remain hospitalized or in strict isolation.

Response efforts are being disrupted in Bunia, Ituri’s capital, where unpaid frontline health workers—including contact tracers—are protesting overdue wages. Workers have warned that strikes are halting data collection needed to identify transmission chains. Violence and insecurity are also making it harder to reach vulnerable communities and deliver medical assistance. The outbreak crossed into Uganda, where 20 cases and two deaths were recorded before the World Health Organization declared that regional cluster over. The Bundibugyo strain has no specific treatment, while two vaccines are in phase 1 human trials. The Congolese government has launched mass immunization with Ervebo, which showed promising results against the variant in animal testing.

Intensifying El Niño Deepens Economic Risks Across LATAM
global_south_and_exploitedeconomy_and_businessmaterial_harmregional expansion
What happened

Intensifying El Niño is disrupting rainfall and temperatures across Latin America, putting harvests, household incomes, water supplies and electricity systems under pressure. Farmers in El Salvador are replanting after losses, while forecasts and official warnings point to wider risks for food, exports, public resources and poverty.

Around Tecoluca in central El Salvador, some farmers who lost their first harvest are planting again, hoping that scattered showers will provide enough water for a second crop. Salvadoran meteorologist Danilo Ramirez said heat and water shortages damaged crops planted during the first sowing season, normally between April and June. Rainfall has since remained irregular: “It can rain one day, and then five, six or eight days can pass without rain,” he said.

El Salvador is part of Central America’s Dry Corridor, where many rural families depend on corn and beans both for food and for income from selling part of their harvest. The country issued a nationwide red alert over drought in August. For farmers already dealing with losses, another poor harvest could further reduce their ability to feed and support their families.

Harvest losses spread through food and incomes

Patricio Gonzalez, a professor of agricultural climatology at Chile’s University of Talca, said the effects move through several economic channels. Smaller harvests leave families with less food and less produce to sell. Reduced supplies can raise prices, while farms under financial pressure may hire fewer workers. Farmers without reliable access to credit or financial assistance are particularly exposed.

The risks are not confined to El Salvador. In Brazil, an official forecast for September through November points to below-average rainfall in the north and northeast and parts of the central and southeastern regions, while parts of the south are expected to be wetter than usual. Brazilian agricultural meteorologist Luis Renato Lazinski said the contrasting conditions leave major crops vulnerable to drought in some producing areas and excessive rainfall in others, threatening both domestic supplies and agricultural exports.

In Peru, the effects are already reflected in the economic outlook. The central bank lowered its 2026 growth forecast in September from 3.4 percent to 3.2 percent, citing a greater-than-expected impact from coastal El Niño conditions. Peruvian fisherfolk historically named the phenomenon after warm seas emptied anchovy nets, linking the climate pattern to a major source of livelihoods.

Water shortages threaten power and trade

Electricity systems are another point of vulnerability. Venezuela obtains roughly 60 percent of its electricity from hydroelectric plants in the south. Ecologist Francisco Herrera warned that severe drought combined with high temperatures there could put substantial pressure on electricity supplies early next year. Reduced water availability could constrain generation just as hotter conditions increase demand.

Herrera said thermal power plants could help offset lower hydropower generation, but relying on them would require more fossil fuels and increase greenhouse gas emissions. Venezuela has announced an electricity-saving plan and called for agricultural contingency measures.

Water stress also affects trade infrastructure. At the Panama Canal, rainfall and water-saving measures have recently improved navigation, allowing additional transit slots and a higher maximum permitted draft. The canal’s watershed nevertheless continues to face water shortages. Exporters therefore face risks both where goods are produced and on the route to market: lower output can reduce shipments, while transport constraints can complicate deliveries and increase costs.

Gonzalez said drought, flooding and prolonged heat can damage production and disrupt water supplies, translating climate risks into pressure on employment, incomes and prices. The UN Economic Commission for Latin America and the Caribbean has warned that an extreme El Niño event could cause losses equivalent to at least 2 percent of the region’s gross domestic product and push an additional 4.8 million people into poverty.

Governments prepare for worsening conditions

Authorities are responding to both current losses and the possibility of more severe conditions. Peru declared a 60-day state of emergency in August in 607 districts across 16 regions because of the risk of water shortages. The government has provided livestock feed, vaccines, drinking facilities, water-storage infrastructure and small dams to help farmers manage drought.

In Brazil, federal agencies are coordinating climate and water monitoring to support preparedness. They are also urging residents to follow official warnings, identify local risk areas and know evacuation routes. In El Salvador, Ramirez identified securing staple grains, managing water carefully and protecting soil as immediate priorities.

Celeste Saulo, secretary of the World Meteorological Organization, said seasonal forecasts provide countries with time to prepare and that existing knowledge and tools can help protect lives and livelihoods before hazards become crises. The forecasts do not remove the immediate uncertainty for farmers replanting in El Salvador, but they offer governments a window to reduce the effects on food, water, power and trade.

A local farmer discovered the specimen, which was described by researchers from China, Italy and Slovakia in *Nature Communications*.

The finding adds to evidence that birds and their closest relatives evolved flight multiple times rather than through a single lineage. Microraptorines were small, meat-eating dinosaurs weighing about one kilogram; hundreds of specimens have been found. Comparing 194 flight-related adaptations in microraptorines with those in the bird lineage, the researchers found that 30% also evolved in Avialae, including wing feathers, hook-shaped toe bones and a relatively elongated forearm bone. However, the adaptations appeared in different sequences, leading the researchers to conclude that they did not reflect a shared developmental regime. Palaeobiologist Michael Pittman said the study provides important evidence for multiple origins of flight among feathered dinosaurs.

A seemingly ordinary question traps a technologist in an endless queue stretching toward a vast data centre. Set against memories of John von Neumann’s parties and ideas, the story turns computing, identity and time into a darkly comic test from which escape appears impossible.

Men and women, technology workers and academics, shuffle along a corridor while cheerful young women ask each person, “Were you at von Neumann’s party?” One insists that there is no right or wrong answer, but the narrator doubts her and tries to leave. She grips the narrator’s arm, and the corridor behind appears to contain a mile-long queue.

The question becomes increasingly unsettling. The narrator considers whether the ordeal is a game such as the prisoner’s dilemma or a lowest-unique-number contest, while memories of an office park, possible locations and repeated tomorrows suggest disorientation and a loop. The interviewer demands a binary yes-or-no answer, prompting the narrator to object that not everything is made of ones and zeroes.

Beyond the door is an enormous room filled with server racks and the roar of cooling fans. The narrator asks whether it is a data centre, but the young woman says she knows nothing about it. When the narrator turns the question back on her, she dismisses von Neumann as belonging to a time before hers. The narrator notes that he died in 1957, while recalling his work on game theory, computer architecture and nuclear-defence strategy, as well as his final illness.
